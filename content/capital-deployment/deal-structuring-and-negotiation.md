Deal Structuring and Negotiation

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

When you decide to acquire a small or medium-sized business, the purchase price is only one variable in a much larger equation. How you structure the deal determines your actual risk exposure, your tax treatment for years to come, your leverage if things go wrong, and your ability to walk away if the business turns out to be something other than what the seller represented. This is the domain of deal structuring, and it is where sophisticated buyers separate themselves from amateurs who overpay and under-protect.

As a high-income surgeon, you bring something unusual to the negotiation table. You have strong cash flow from your clinical practice, you have credibility with lenders, and you have a professional reputation that makes sellers take you seriously. But you also have a critical vulnerability. You do not have time to recover from a catastrophic deal gone wrong. A bad acquisition that consumes your attention, drains your capital, and creates legal headaches can derail not just your investment portfolio but your surgical career. That is why deal structure matters more to you than it does to a full-time private equity operator who can absorb losses across a portfolio of twenty companies.

The core principle here is straightforward. You want to acquire businesses in a way that minimizes your downside risk, maximizes your tax efficiency, preserves your optionality, and aligns the seller's incentives with a successful transition. Every element of deal structure, from the letter of intent through the closing documents, is a tool for achieving those objectives. Let me walk you through each of those tools in detail.

MECHANICS

The process begins with the Letter of Intent, commonly called the LOI. This is a non-binding document that outlines the key terms of the proposed acquisition. I want to emphasize the non-binding part. The LOI is not a contract to purchase the business. It is a framework for negotiation. It says, in effect, here is what I am willing to pay, here is how I propose to structure the payment, here are the major conditions that must be satisfied before I will close, and here is the timeline I envision. The LOI typically includes an exclusivity period, usually sixty to ninety days, during which the seller agrees not to entertain other offers while you conduct due diligence. This exclusivity clause is usually the one binding provision in an otherwise non-binding document.

Your LOI should be specific enough to anchor the negotiation but flexible enough to leave room for adjustments based on what you discover during due diligence. A good LOI covers the purchase price, the proposed deal structure, the due diligence timeline, key contingencies, and any major terms like non-compete agreements or transition periods. Do not over-negotiate the LOI. Its purpose is to get you into the due diligence phase, not to finalize every detail.

Now let us talk about the most fundamental structural decision you will make: asset purchase versus stock purchase. In almost every small and medium-sized business acquisition, you want to do an asset purchase. Let me explain why. In an asset purchase, you are buying the individual assets of the business, the equipment, the customer list, the intellectual property, the inventory, the contracts. You are not buying the legal entity itself. This distinction matters enormously for two reasons. First, liability protection. When you buy assets, you generally do not inherit the unknown liabilities of the seller's company. If the previous owner had an environmental violation, a pending lawsuit, or an undisclosed tax obligation, those liabilities stay with the seller's entity. In a stock purchase, you are buying the entire legal entity, warts and all, and every hidden liability comes with it.

Second, and this is critical for a high-income earner, an asset purchase gives you a stepped-up basis on the acquired assets. That means you can depreciate and amortize the purchase price over time, creating tax deductions that shelter your other income. If you buy a business for one point two million dollars as an asset purchase, you can allocate that purchase price across tangible assets like equipment, which might depreciate over five to seven years, and intangible assets like goodwill and customer relationships, which amortize over fifteen years. On a one point two million dollar deal, you might generate eighty thousand to one hundred fifty thousand dollars per year in depreciation and amortization deductions for the first several years. At a marginal tax rate of thirty-seven percent federal plus state taxes, that is real money.

The seller will often prefer a stock purchase because it allows them to treat the entire gain as a capital gain taxed at twenty percent rather than facing the ordinary income recapture that comes with an asset sale. This is a negotiation point. You may need to increase your offer price by five to ten percent to compensate the seller for the tax disadvantage of an asset sale. That premium is almost always worth paying because the liability protection and tax benefits to you far exceed the incremental purchase price.

Let me turn to the components of the purchase price itself, because in a well-structured deal, the price is not a single lump sum. It is a combination of several elements that spread your risk and align incentives.

The first component is the cash at closing. This is the amount you pay on the day the deal closes. In a typical small business acquisition, this might be fifty to seventy percent of the total purchase price. On a one point two million dollar deal, you might pay seven hundred twenty thousand to eight hundred forty thousand at closing.

The second component is the seller note. This is financing provided by the seller, typically covering ten to twenty percent of the purchase price. On our one point two million dollar example, that is one hundred twenty thousand to two hundred forty thousand dollars. The seller note usually carries an interest rate of four to six percent and is paid over three to five years. Here is why seller notes are so valuable to you as a buyer. If the seller is willing to finance part of the purchase, it signals confidence in the business. A seller who knows the business is in decline will not want to be holding a note that depends on the business continuing to generate cash flow. The seller note also gives you leverage. If it turns out the seller misrepresented something material about the business, you can offset your damages against the remaining note balance rather than having to file a lawsuit to recover money you already paid.

The third component is the earnout. This is a portion of the purchase price, typically ten to twenty percent, that is contingent on the business achieving certain performance targets after the closing. On our example deal, that is one hundred twenty thousand to two hundred forty thousand dollars. The earnout might be structured as follows: if the business maintains at least ninety percent of its trailing twelve-month revenue for each of the two years following the closing, the seller receives an additional sixty thousand to one hundred twenty thousand per year. Earnouts are powerful tools for bridging valuation gaps. If the seller believes the business is worth one point five million and you believe it is worth one million, an earnout lets you pay one million upfront with the potential to pay up to one point five million if the business performs as the seller claims it will. The risk shifts to the seller to prove their valuation was justified.

However, earnouts are also a source of post-closing disputes. The seller will argue that any decline in performance was caused by changes you made as the new owner. You will argue that the decline reveals the business was never as strong as represented. To mitigate this, you need crystal-clear earnout metrics defined in the purchase agreement, an obligation to operate the business in the ordinary course during the earnout period, and a dispute resolution mechanism, ideally binding arbitration rather than litigation.

Now let us discuss the non-compete agreement. This is not optional. In any business acquisition, you must require the seller to sign a non-compete agreement. The standard terms are three to five years and a geographic radius of one hundred miles or more, depending on the nature of the business. If you are buying a regional service business, you might need a two-hundred-mile radius. If you are buying an e-commerce business, the non-compete might be industry-wide rather than geographic. The non-compete should also include a non-solicitation clause preventing the seller from poaching employees or customers of the business you just bought. Without a non-compete, the seller could take your money, wait six months, and open a competing business across the street, taking all the customer relationships with them. The entire value of your acquisition evaporates.

The transition period is closely related. You should require the seller to remain involved in the business for six to twelve months after closing. During this period, the seller introduces you to key customers, trains you on operational processes, and helps ensure continuity. The transition period can be compensated separately or built into the purchase price. I recommend a separate consulting agreement at a reasonable rate, perhaps five thousand to ten thousand dollars per month, because it gives you the legal framework to hold the seller accountable for actually showing up and being helpful during the transition. If you build the transition into the purchase price and the seller checks out after two weeks, you have limited recourse.

Two more structural elements deserve your attention. The first is the working capital peg. Working capital is the difference between the business's current assets, primarily cash, accounts receivable, and inventory, and its current liabilities, primarily accounts payable and accrued expenses. When you buy a business, you need it to come with a normal level of working capital so you can operate it from day one without injecting additional cash. The working capital peg establishes a target level of working capital based on the business's historical average. If the actual working capital at closing is below the peg, the purchase price is reduced dollar for dollar. If it is above the peg, the purchase price increases. Without a working capital peg, the seller has an incentive to drain the business of cash, accelerate collections, delay paying vendors, and generally strip out working capital between signing and closing. You show up on day one to find a business that technically transferred but has no cash to operate. The working capital peg prevents this.

The second is representation and warranty insurance, often called R and W insurance. This is a policy that covers losses arising from breaches of the seller's representations and warranties in the purchase agreement. In larger deals, say five million dollars and above, R and W insurance is standard. In smaller deals, it is less common but increasingly available. The premium is typically two to four percent of the policy limit. The benefit is that it provides a source of recovery for breaches without requiring you to pursue the seller personally, which can be expensive, time-consuming, and emotionally draining. If R and W insurance is available for your deal size, it is worth the premium.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through a realistic deal from start to finish so you can see how all these pieces fit together.

You identify a home services business, let us say an HVAC company, that has been operating for fifteen years. The owner is sixty-two years old and wants to retire. The business generates eight hundred thousand dollars in seller's discretionary earnings, which is the owner's total economic benefit including salary, benefits, and add-backs. You agree on a valuation of three times seller's discretionary earnings, or two point four million dollars.

You submit an LOI proposing the following structure. The total purchase price is two point four million dollars structured as an asset purchase. One point six million in cash at closing, funded by an SBA seven(a) loan. Three hundred sixty thousand as a seller note at five percent interest over four years. Two hundred forty thousand as an earnout over two years, contingent on the business maintaining at least eighty-five percent of trailing twelve-month revenue. A five-year non-compete with a one-hundred-fifty-mile radius. A twelve-month transition period with the seller serving as a consultant at eight thousand dollars per month. A working capital peg set at the average of the trailing twelve months of net working capital, which in this case is approximately one hundred eighty thousand dollars.

The seller counters. They want two point six million total, a three-year non-compete with a seventy-five-mile radius, and a six-month transition period. Here is where negotiation skill matters. You hold firm on the non-compete. Five years, one hundred fifty miles. This is non-negotiable because the seller's personal relationships with customers are the primary asset you are acquiring. If those relationships walk out the door with the seller, you have nothing. You compromise on the transition period, agreeing to nine months instead of twelve, and you increase the earnout to three hundred thousand, bringing the total potential price to two point four six million, which is close to the seller's ask of two point six million but protects you because that extra sixty thousand is contingent on performance.

The revised deal looks like this. One point six million in cash at closing. Three hundred sixty thousand as a seller note. Three hundred thousand as a performance-based earnout. The seller's maximum total consideration is two point two six million guaranteed plus three hundred thousand contingent, for a maximum of two point five six million. You have reduced your guaranteed outlay by one hundred forty thousand compared to the seller's initial ask while offering a path to nearly the same total price if the business performs well.

During due diligence, you discover that one of the business's three largest commercial contracts is up for renewal in four months and the customer has been expressing dissatisfaction. This is material. You renegotiate the earnout to specifically tie a portion of it to the renewal of that contract. If the contract renews, the seller gets the full earnout. If it does not, the earnout is reduced by one hundred thousand. The seller agrees because they believe the contract will renew, and you have protected yourself against a known risk.

You close the deal. The SBA loan covers one point six million at a rate of prime plus two point seventy-five percent. Your monthly debt service is approximately eighteen thousand five hundred dollars. The business generates roughly sixty-seven thousand per month in seller's discretionary earnings. After debt service, you have forty-eight thousand five hundred per month in cash flow before paying a general manager, which you will need since you are not leaving your surgical practice. A good general manager costs eight thousand to twelve thousand per month. That leaves you thirty-six thousand five hundred to forty thousand five hundred per month in pre-tax cash flow from the acquisition. That is four hundred thirty-eight thousand to four hundred eighty-six thousand per year in additional income, and a significant portion of it is sheltered by the depreciation and amortization deductions from the asset purchase structure.

RISK ANALYSIS

Let me be direct about the risks. The number one risk in any business acquisition is that the business underperforms after closing. Revenue declines, key employees leave, or a major customer defects. Your deal structure mitigates this in several ways. The earnout means you pay less if performance drops. The seller note gives you leverage and a partial offset if the seller misrepresented something. The non-compete prevents the seller from becoming your competitor. But none of these protections eliminate the risk entirely. If the business loses fifty percent of its revenue in the first year, you still owe one point six million on the SBA loan and you are in serious trouble.

The second risk is opportunity cost. The time you spend managing an acquisition, even with a general manager in place, is time you are not spending on your surgical practice, which generates high-certainty income at a known rate. If your clinical income is six hundred thousand per year and you reduce your clinical schedule by twenty percent to manage the acquisition, you are giving up one hundred twenty thousand per year in guaranteed income for the uncertain prospect of four hundred thousand or more in business income. The math works if the business performs, but you need to be honest about the time commitment.

The third risk is legal liability. Despite the asset purchase structure, there are scenarios where liabilities can follow the assets. If the business has environmental contamination on its property, you could inherit cleanup obligations. If the business has been violating employment laws, you could face claims from employees who remain with the business. Thorough due diligence and strong representations and warranties in the purchase agreement are your primary defenses, but they are not absolute.

The fourth risk is the SBA loan personal guarantee. SBA loans require a personal guarantee from anyone who owns twenty percent or more of the borrowing entity. That means your personal assets, including your home and investment accounts, are at risk if the business fails and cannot service the debt. This is a real risk that many physician investors underestimate. You are accustomed to earning income with no capital at risk. Business ownership puts capital at risk, and the personal guarantee extends that risk to your entire balance sheet.

The fifth risk is overpaying. The multiples I used in this example, three times seller's discretionary earnings, are reasonable for a well-established home services business with strong cash flow. But multiples vary significantly by industry, geography, business quality, and market conditions. If you pay five times earnings for a business that typically trades at three times, you have overpaid by forty percent and it may take years to recover that premium through operations. You must understand the relevant comparable transactions for the type of business you are acquiring. Your M and A attorney and your broker can help you assemble this data.

RISK MITIGATION THROUGH STRUCTURE

Let me highlight how specific structural elements mitigate specific risks. The asset purchase structure protects you from unknown historical liabilities and provides tax-efficient depreciation. The seller note ensures the seller has skin in the game post-closing and gives you an offset right if representations prove false. The earnout shifts performance risk back to the seller and bridges valuation gaps. The non-compete and non-solicitation protect the customer relationships and employee base you are paying for. The working capital peg prevents the seller from stripping the business before closing. The transition period ensures operational continuity and knowledge transfer. And representation and warranty insurance, when available, provides a funded source of recovery without requiring you to personally pursue the seller.

Each of these elements costs you something in negotiation. The seller will resist the earnout because they want certainty. They will resist a long non-compete because it limits their future options. They will resist a seller note because they want all cash at closing. Your job as a buyer is to explain that these structural elements are not adversarial. They are alignment mechanisms. A seller who is confident in their business should welcome an earnout because it proves the business is worth what they claim. A seller who is truly retiring should not care about a five-year non-compete because they have no intention of competing.

When a seller aggressively resists reasonable structural protections, that itself is information. It may indicate that the seller knows something about the business that would come out during due diligence, or that the seller's representations are not entirely accurate. Pay attention to what sellers resist and why.

ACTION STEPS

Here is exactly what you need to do to prepare for your first deal.

Step one. Hire an M and A attorney who specializes in small and medium-sized business acquisitions. This is not your estate planning attorney. This is not your malpractice defense attorney. This is a specialist who closes five to fifteen deals per year in the one-million to ten-million dollar range. They will cost you three hundred to five hundred dollars per hour, and your total legal bill for an acquisition will be twenty thousand to forty thousand dollars. That sounds expensive until you realize that a single poorly drafted provision in a purchase agreement can cost you hundreds of thousands. Ask potential attorneys how many SMB acquisitions they closed in the past twelve months. If the answer is fewer than five, keep looking. The right attorney will also serve as a strategic advisor during the negotiation, helping you understand which terms to hold firm on and which to concede.

Step two. Establish your entity structure before you begin looking at deals. Work with your CPA and your attorney to create the acquisition entity. For most physician investors, this will be a holding company LLC that owns a subsidiary LLC for each acquired business. The holding company provides liability separation between businesses, and the LLC structure provides pass-through taxation that lets you take advantage of those depreciation and amortization deductions against your other income.

Step three. Get pre-qualified for SBA financing. Talk to at least three SBA-preferred lenders before you start looking at deals. Understand how much they will lend you based on your income, credit profile, and the type of business you are targeting. SBA seven(a) loans can finance up to five million dollars with terms up to twenty-five years for real estate and ten years for business acquisitions. The current interest rates for SBA loans are typically prime plus one point five to three percent. Having a pre-qualification letter makes your LOI more credible to sellers.

Step four. Build your deal checklist. Before you submit any LOI, you should have a written checklist of every structural element we discussed today. Asset purchase versus stock purchase. Seller note terms. Earnout metrics and duration. Non-compete scope and duration. Transition period terms. Working capital peg methodology. Representations and warranties you require. Indemnification provisions. Conditions to closing. Walk through this checklist for every deal and make sure each element is addressed in your LOI and ultimately in the purchase agreement.

Step five. Practice negotiation on smaller stakes before you sit across from a seller in a two-million-dollar deal. Negotiate your next equipment purchase. Negotiate your next office lease renewal. Negotiate your next employment contract. The skills are transferable. The key principles are the same: understand the other side's priorities, know your walk-away point before you enter the room, make concessions strategically rather than emotionally, and always tie concessions to reciprocal concessions from the other side.

Step six. Build relationships with business brokers in your target market. Brokers see deal flow before it hits the open market. If a broker knows you are a qualified, serious buyer who can close quickly, they will bring you opportunities first. Attend local business broker association events, join the International Business Brokers Association as an associate member, and let brokers know exactly what type of business you are looking for, your size range, your industry preferences, and your timeline.

The bottom line is this. Deal structure is not paperwork. It is strategy. Every clause in a purchase agreement either protects you or exposes you, and the difference between a good deal and a catastrophic mistake often comes down to structural elements that were either present or absent. You have the income to absorb moderate losses. You do not have the time to recover from preventable disasters. Structure your deals to eliminate preventable disasters, and the economics of business acquisition will work powerfully in your favor.
