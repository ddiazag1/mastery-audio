HVAC and Trades Business Operations

Section One. Concept Overview.

Let me walk you through one of the most overlooked and misunderstood asset classes available to high-income physicians, and that is the acquisition and operation of HVAC and mechanical trades businesses. When most surgeons think about deploying capital, they think about real estate syndications, the stock market, maybe a med-spa or surgery center. Very few think about buying a company that installs and services furnaces, air conditioners, and plumbing systems. And that is exactly why this space is so attractive.

The HVAC and mechanical trades industry in the United States generates over two hundred billion dollars in annual revenue. There are roughly one hundred and twenty thousand HVAC contractors in the country, and the vast majority of them are small operations, doing between seven hundred thousand and five million dollars a year in revenue. Many of these businesses are owned by aging founders in their late fifties and sixties who built the company with their own hands, and they have no succession plan. They have no children interested in taking over. They have no management team groomed to buy them out. They just have a business that throws off three hundred thousand to seven hundred thousand dollars a year in owner benefit, and they want to retire.

This is where you come in. Not as the person turning wrenches or running service calls, but as the capital deployer and strategic operator who acquires this business, installs professional management, and scales it using the same systems thinking you already apply in the operating room. You already understand complex systems, risk stratification, and protocol-driven outcomes. Those skills translate directly.

Here is why the economics work for a surgeon buyer. A typical HVAC business doing two million dollars in revenue with four to six technicians will generate somewhere between three hundred thousand and five hundred thousand dollars in seller's discretionary earnings, which is the owner's total economic benefit including salary, benefits, personal expenses run through the business, and add-backs. You can acquire this business for two and a half to four times that discretionary earnings figure, meaning a purchase price of roughly seven hundred fifty thousand to two million dollars. With SBA 7(a) financing, you can put down as little as ten percent, sometimes fifteen percent, and finance the rest over ten years at competitive rates. So your out-of-pocket capital to acquire a business generating three to five hundred thousand dollars a year in cash flow might be seventy-five to two hundred thousand dollars. That is a fundamentally different risk-return profile than almost anything else you can do with that money.

But here is the critical point. The returns only materialize if you understand operations. You are not buying a passive investment. You are buying a machine, and that machine has specific components, specific metrics, and specific failure modes. This lesson is about understanding those components so you can evaluate, acquire, and operate an HVAC business intelligently.

Section Two. Mechanics.

Let us break down the key operational metrics that determine whether an HVAC business is healthy, mediocre, or dying. These are the vital signs of the business, and you need to monitor them the same way you monitor intracranial pressure or cerebral perfusion.

The first and most important metric is revenue per technician. A well-run HVAC company should generate between two hundred thousand and three hundred fifty thousand dollars in revenue per technician per year. If you have a company doing two million in revenue with ten technicians, that is two hundred thousand per tech, which is at the low end and suggests inefficiency. If that same two million is being generated by six technicians, you are at three hundred thirty-three thousand per tech, which is excellent. This number tells you how productive your workforce is. Low revenue per tech usually means one of several things: poor dispatching, too much windshield time driving between jobs, underpricing, technicians who are not trained to identify and sell additional services, or simply too many bodies on the payroll relative to the work volume. When you are evaluating an acquisition target, calculate this number immediately. If it is below two hundred thousand, you are looking at either a turnaround situation or a business with significant upside if you can improve operational efficiency.

The second metric is your service agreement count and renewal rate. Service agreements are the recurring revenue backbone of any HVAC business. A service agreement is a contract where the homeowner pays a fixed annual fee, typically one hundred fifty to three hundred dollars, and in return they get two maintenance visits per year, priority scheduling, and discounted repairs. These agreements are gold for three reasons. First, they create predictable recurring revenue. Second, maintenance visits generate repair and replacement leads, because when your technician is in the home doing a tune-up and finds a cracked heat exchanger or a failing capacitor, that is a warm lead for a repair or full system replacement. Third, they reduce customer acquisition cost because these customers are already in your system and already trust you. A healthy HVAC business should have at least one service agreement per technician per working day being serviced, and overall the company should maintain a renewal rate above eighty percent. If a company has two thousand service agreements renewing at eighty-five percent, that is seventeen hundred renewals generating at minimum two hundred fifty-five thousand to five hundred ten thousand dollars in recurring maintenance revenue before you even count the repair and replacement work those visits generate. When you see a company with a low service agreement count or a renewal rate below seventy percent, that tells you either the agreements are poorly structured, the company is not emphasizing them in their sales process, or the service quality is so poor that customers do not see the value. Any of those problems can be fixed with better systems.

The third metric is average ticket size. This is the average dollar amount per service call or job. For residential HVAC service calls, a healthy average ticket is between three hundred fifty and six hundred dollars. For commercial service, it can be significantly higher. For installation and replacement jobs, you are looking at five thousand to fifteen thousand dollars on the residential side. Average ticket size is a function of pricing strategy, technician training in diagnostics and communication, and the company's willingness to present options rather than just doing the minimum repair. Many small HVAC shops leave enormous money on the table because the owner trained his techs to fix what is broken and get to the next call. A professionally managed company trains technicians to perform a thorough system evaluation, present the customer with a good-better-best option menu, and let the customer choose their comfort level. This consistently increases average ticket by twenty to forty percent without any deceptive practices, just by giving the customer more information and more choices.

The fourth metric is technician utilization rate. This measures what percentage of a technician's available working hours are spent on revenue-generating activities versus non-billable time like driving, waiting for parts, doing paperwork, or sitting at the shop. A well-run company achieves a utilization rate above seventy percent. An average company runs fifty to sixty percent. A poorly run company might be at forty percent or below. Every ten percentage points of improvement in utilization rate translates directly to the bottom line because the technician's salary is a fixed cost whether they are generating revenue or not. Utilization is improved through better dispatching software, tighter geographic routing, pre-stocked trucks with the most common parts, and streamlined administrative processes.

The fifth metric is customer acquisition cost. How much does it cost the company to acquire a new customer? This includes marketing spend, advertising, referral bonuses, and the allocated cost of the sales team or call center. A healthy HVAC company should have a customer acquisition cost below three hundred dollars for a service customer and below eight hundred dollars for a replacement or installation customer. If the company is spending more than that, the marketing is inefficient, the website is not converting, or the brand reputation is not generating enough organic and referral business. Digital marketing, particularly Google Local Services Ads and a strong Google Business Profile with good reviews, has dramatically lowered customer acquisition costs for HVAC companies that manage these channels well.

The sixth metric is gross margin by service type. Not all revenue is created equal. Service and repair work typically carries a gross margin of fifty-five to sixty-five percent. Installation and replacement work runs thirty-five to fifty percent gross margin. Maintenance agreement work can run sixty to seventy-five percent margin because it is highly efficient and predictable. Commercial work varies widely but often runs lower margin on a percentage basis while being higher in absolute dollars. Understanding the margin mix tells you where the profit actually comes from and where to focus growth efforts. A company doing sixty percent of its revenue in low-margin new construction installation is a very different animal than one doing sixty percent in high-margin service and repair.

Seventh is the seasonal revenue pattern. HVAC is inherently seasonal. In most markets, you see revenue peaks in June through August for cooling season and November through February for heating season. The shoulder seasons of spring and fall can see revenue drop thirty to fifty percent from peak months. A well-managed company mitigates this through maintenance agreement scheduling in shoulder seasons, indoor air quality product sales, plumbing or electrical service diversification, and commercial contracts that provide year-round work. When you are analyzing the books of an acquisition target, look at monthly revenue for at least three years. If the company has violent seasonal swings with almost no revenue in April and October, that tells you there is significant operational risk and cash flow management challenges that need to be addressed.

Eighth is truck and equipment age. Every service technician needs a fully equipped service van or truck. These vehicles typically cost forty to sixty thousand dollars when upfitted with shelving, inventory, and tools. A well-run company replaces trucks on a four to six year cycle. If you look at the fleet and see trucks that are eight, ten, twelve years old, that tells you deferred capital expenditure. You will need to budget for fleet replacement shortly after acquisition, which can represent a significant capital outlay. Similarly, look at diagnostic equipment, recovery machines, vacuum pumps, and other tools. Outdated equipment slows technicians down and signals underinvestment.

The ninth and final key metric is callback rate. A callback is when a customer calls back within thirty days because the original repair did not hold or the problem recurred. A healthy callback rate is below five percent. If you see a callback rate above eight or ten percent, you have a quality problem. Callbacks are pure cost. The technician has to go back to the job, the parts might need to be replaced again, and the customer is unhappy. High callback rates indicate poor technician training, rushed diagnostics, use of substandard parts, or a culture that prioritizes speed over quality. This is directly analogous to surgical complication rates. You would not tolerate a ten percent return-to-OR rate, and you should not tolerate a ten percent callback rate in your HVAC business.

Section Three. Deal Walkthrough.

Let me walk you through a realistic acquisition scenario so you can see how these numbers come together. Imagine you identify a company called Comfort Pro Mechanical in a mid-sized city. The owner, let us call him Dave, is sixty-two years old and has been running the company for twenty-eight years. He has eight technicians, two installers, an office manager, and a dispatcher. The company did two point four million in revenue last year. Dave takes a salary of one hundred fifty thousand dollars, runs another sixty thousand in personal expenses through the business, and the company shows a net income of one hundred twenty thousand dollars on the tax return. After adding back Dave's salary, personal expenses, depreciation, interest on a small equipment loan, and a one-time expense from a roof repair on the shop building, the seller's discretionary earnings come to four hundred ten thousand dollars.

You negotiate a purchase price of three and a half times SDE, which is one million four hundred thirty-five thousand dollars. You use an SBA 7(a) loan to finance ninety percent, putting down one hundred forty-three thousand five hundred dollars plus covering about thirty thousand in closing costs, legal fees, and due diligence expenses. Your total out-of-pocket is roughly one hundred seventy-five thousand dollars. The SBA loan of one million two hundred ninety-one thousand five hundred dollars at a rate of prime plus two and three-quarters, which let us say is currently around eight and a half percent, amortized over ten years, gives you a monthly payment of approximately sixteen thousand dollars, or one hundred ninety-two thousand dollars per year in debt service.

Now let us look at the operating picture. The four hundred ten thousand in SDE included Dave's compensation and personal expenses. You need to replace Dave with a general manager, which costs you one hundred twenty thousand per year in salary plus benefits. You also need to budget twenty thousand for accounting and financial oversight that Dave was doing himself. So your adjusted cash flow before debt service is four hundred ten thousand minus one hundred forty thousand in management costs, which gives you two hundred seventy thousand dollars. After debt service of one hundred ninety-two thousand, you have seventy-eight thousand dollars in free cash flow in year one. That is a forty-five percent cash-on-cash return on your one hundred seventy-five thousand dollar investment, which is solid but not spectacular.

Here is where operational improvements change the picture dramatically. In year two, you implement a flat-rate pricing system that increases average ticket size by twenty-five percent. You invest in a service agreement sales campaign that grows your agreement base from eight hundred to twelve hundred contracts. You install GPS dispatching and routing software that improves technician utilization from sixty percent to seventy-two percent. You hire one additional technician to handle the increased call volume these improvements generate. The combined effect of these changes can reasonably increase revenue by twenty to thirty percent while improving margins by three to five percentage points. Let us say conservatively that year two revenue hits three million with adjusted cash flow of three hundred seventy-five thousand before debt service. After the same one hundred ninety-two thousand in annual payments, you now have one hundred eighty-three thousand dollars in free cash flow. That is over a hundred percent cash-on-cash return on your original investment.

By year three, if you continue executing, you can be at three point five million in revenue with four hundred fifty thousand or more in adjusted cash flow. At this point you have multiple options. You can continue to operate and enjoy the cash flow. You can acquire a second HVAC company in an adjacent market and consolidate. Or you can sell the company, because a three point five million dollar HVAC company with strong systems, clean books, and a management team in place will sell for five to six times EBITDA, which at three hundred fifty thousand in EBITDA could mean a sale price of one point seven five to two point one million dollars. After paying off the remaining loan balance of approximately nine hundred fifty thousand, you walk away with eight hundred thousand to over one million dollars in equity gain, plus the two to three hundred thousand in cumulative free cash flow you already collected. Your total return on one hundred seventy-five thousand invested over three years is north of one million dollars.

Section Four. Risk Analysis.

Now let us talk about what can go wrong, because this is not a fairy tale and you need to understand the risks with the same rigor you apply to surgical decision-making.

The single biggest risk in any trades business acquisition is key person dependency. If Dave is the reason customers call, if Dave is the one who trained every technician, if Dave is the only one who knows how to bid commercial jobs, then when Dave leaves, the business can deteriorate rapidly. During due diligence, you must assess how much of the business's value is in Dave versus in the systems, the brand, and the team. Ideally, Dave stays on for a transition period of six to twelve months, and you structure part of his payment as a seller note contingent on transition milestones.

The second risk is technician retention. In today's labor market, skilled HVAC technicians are in extremely high demand. If your techs leave after the acquisition because they do not like the new ownership or they get poached by a competitor, you can lose revenue capacity almost overnight. Mitigation includes meeting every technician before closing, understanding their compensation and concerns, potentially offering retention bonuses or equity incentives, and demonstrating that you are investing in the business rather than stripping it.

The third risk is regulatory and licensing. HVAC businesses require specific state and local licenses, EPA certifications for refrigerant handling, and often contractor's licenses that are held personally by the owner. You need to ensure that the business can continue operating legally after the ownership change. This may require hiring a licensed qualifier or having the owner maintain his license during a transition period.

The fourth risk is warranty liability. If the company installed systems in the past that are now failing, you may inherit warranty obligations or even legal liability for faulty installations. A thorough review of outstanding warranties, pending complaints, and any litigation history is essential during due diligence.

The fifth risk is capital expenditure surprises. Beyond the fleet age issue I mentioned earlier, look at the shop or warehouse facility, the inventory management system, the software stack, and any deferred maintenance on assets. A seller who knows he is selling often stops investing in the business twelve to eighteen months before the sale, and you inherit a capital expenditure backlog.

The sixth risk is concentration. If more than twenty percent of the company's revenue comes from a single customer, a single builder, or a single commercial contract, that is a concentration risk. If that customer leaves, you have a significant revenue hole. Diversified residential service businesses have the lowest concentration risk. Companies heavily dependent on new construction or a single commercial client are much riskier.

Section Five. Action Steps.

Here is your concrete action plan if you want to explore this asset class seriously.

Step one. Educate yourself on the trades business model before you spend a dollar. Read The E-Myth Contractor by Michael Gerber and HVAC Spells Wealth by Ron Smith. Listen to the HVAC Uncensored podcast and the Blue Collar Gold podcast. Join the Service Nation Alliance or Nexstar Network online communities where you can learn from operators who have built and sold HVAC companies.

Step two. Identify your target market. You want a metropolitan area with at least one hundred fifty thousand people, a housing stock that is at least twenty years old on average to ensure a healthy replacement market, and ideally a climate with both heating and cooling demand. Avoid markets dominated by a single large competitor with more than thirty percent market share, and avoid markets with an oversupply of technicians which drives down pricing.

Step three. Build your acquisition team. You need a CPA with experience in trades business valuations, a business attorney experienced in SBA acquisitions, an SBA-preferred lender who has closed trades business deals before, and ideally a business broker who specializes in HVAC and mechanical contractors. The broker alone can source deals you would never find on your own because many of the best acquisitions are never publicly listed.

Step four. Get pre-qualified for SBA financing before you look at deals. This gives you credibility with sellers and brokers and tells you exactly how much capital you need to bring to the table. Your surgeon income actually works heavily in your favor here because the SBA wants to see that the buyer can support the debt service, and your W-2 income provides a substantial safety net.

Step five. Evaluate at least five to ten businesses before making an offer on one. Use the metrics I have outlined in this lesson as your screening criteria. Revenue per technician above two hundred and fifty thousand. Service agreement renewal rate above eighty percent. Callback rate below five percent. A diversified customer base without heavy concentration. Reasonable fleet and equipment age. And most importantly, a business where the systems and team can operate without the owner doing every critical function.

Step six. Structure the deal with downside protection. Use an SBA 7(a) loan for leverage. Include a seller note for ten to twenty percent of the purchase price, with at least a portion tied to transition milestones and revenue maintenance. Negotiate a training and transition period of at least six months. Include representations and warranties about customer contracts, employee agreements, pending litigation, and equipment condition. Set up an escrow holdback of five to ten percent of the purchase price for twelve months to cover any undisclosed liabilities.

Step seven. On day one of ownership, do not change anything for the first ninety days. Observe, listen, and learn. Meet every customer you can. Ride along with technicians. Sit with the dispatcher. Understand how the business actually operates before you start implementing changes. The fastest way to destroy value in an acquisition is to come in on day one with a surgeon's confidence and start restructuring everything before you understand the ecosystem.

After that initial observation period, begin implementing improvements methodically. Start with the highest-impact, lowest-disruption changes first. Usually that means implementing or improving dispatch software, launching a service agreement sales initiative, and introducing flat-rate pricing if the company is still using time-and-materials billing. Each of these changes can add fifty to one hundred thousand dollars in annual profit without significant risk or disruption to the existing operation.

This is not a passive investment, but it does not require you to quit surgery either. With a competent general manager in place and modern cloud-based business management software, you can oversee operations in two to four hours per week, reviewing dashboards, holding a weekly management call, and making strategic decisions. The day-to-day operations are handled by your manager and your team. Your role is capital allocation, strategic direction, and accountability, which are exactly the skills you already deploy in your clinical practice and departmental leadership.

The HVAC and trades business space represents one of the most compelling opportunities for physician capital deployment available today. The businesses are essential, recession-resistant, and generate strong cash flow. The baby boomer ownership transition creates a massive supply of acquisition opportunities. SBA financing provides leverage that amplifies returns. And the operational complexity, while real, is manageable for anyone who has mastered the far greater complexity of neurosurgical practice. The question is not whether you can do this. The question is whether you will take the time to learn the model, build the team, and execute the acquisition. The numbers say you should.
