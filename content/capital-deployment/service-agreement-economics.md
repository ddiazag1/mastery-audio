Welcome back. Today we are going deep on what I consider the single most important asset class inside a home services business, and specifically inside an HVAC company. We are talking about service agreements, sometimes called maintenance contracts, sometimes called membership plans. Whatever label you put on them, they represent the economic engine that separates a mediocre acquisition from a truly exceptional one. By the end of this session, you will understand exactly why service agreements drive valuation multiples, how the economics work at a granular level, how to evaluate them during due diligence, what can go wrong, and what concrete steps you should take as an acquiring physician-investor. Let us get into it.

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

To understand service agreement economics, you first need to understand the fundamental problem that every HVAC company faces. HVAC revenue is inherently volatile. In a typical market, eighty percent of demand is weather-driven. A mild summer means fewer air conditioning calls. A warm winter means fewer furnace emergencies. This creates wild swings in revenue, and wild swings in revenue make it nearly impossible to staff properly, manage cash flow, or plan capital expenditures. Service agreements solve this problem in a way that nothing else can.

A service agreement is a contract between the HVAC company and a homeowner or commercial building owner. The customer pays a fixed annual fee, typically somewhere between one hundred fifty and three hundred dollars for residential, and the company provides scheduled maintenance visits, usually two per year, one before the cooling season and one before the heating season. In exchange for that annual fee, the customer often receives priority scheduling, discounted repair rates, sometimes waived diagnostic fees, and the peace of mind that their system is being professionally maintained. It sounds simple, and that simplicity is exactly what makes it powerful.

Here is why service agreements matter so much from an acquisition standpoint. They create four compounding economic advantages simultaneously. First, recurring revenue. When you have five hundred active service agreements at two hundred dollars per year, that is one hundred thousand dollars in revenue that shows up whether the weather cooperates or not. That revenue is contracted. It is predictable. It is bankable. Lenders love it. Valuators love it. You should love it.

Second, customer retention. A customer on a service agreement renews at rates above eighty percent, and in well-run companies, above ninety percent. Compare that to a one-time repair customer, where you might see that person again in three years, or never. The agreement creates a relationship. It creates switching costs. It creates loyalty that has real economic value.

Third, lead generation. This is the part that most people outside the industry miss entirely. Every maintenance visit is a sales opportunity. Your technician walks into a home, inspects the furnace, and finds a cracked heat exchanger, a failing capacitor, or a system that is fourteen years old and running on R-22 refrigerant that is no longer manufactured. That visit, which the customer is already paying you to perform, generates a repair ticket or a replacement lead. Industry data shows that a well-trained technician generates between two hundred and five hundred dollars in additional repair or upsell revenue per maintenance visit, on average. When you multiply that across a thousand visits per year, you are looking at two hundred thousand to five hundred thousand dollars in additional revenue that flows directly from the agreement base.

Fourth, seasonal smoothing. Because maintenance visits are scheduled in advance, you can spread work across the shoulder seasons, spring and fall, when demand would otherwise crater. This means your technicians are productive year-round instead of sitting idle in October and April. Idle technicians are extraordinarily expensive. A single HVAC technician costs you between sixty-five and eighty-five thousand dollars per year in total compensation, and if they are only generating revenue seven months out of twelve, you are hemorrhaging money during the off months. Service agreements fill that gap.

Now here is where it all connects to valuation. An HVAC company without a significant agreement base is a project company. It lives and dies by the phone ringing. It gets valued at two to three times seller discretionary earnings, sometimes less. An HVAC company with a thousand or more active service agreements is a systems company. It has predictable revenue, high customer retention, built-in lead generation, and stable technician utilization. That company commands three and a half to five times seller discretionary earnings, sometimes more. The difference between a two and a half times multiple and a four and a half times multiple on a company generating four hundred thousand in SDE is the difference between a one million dollar purchase price and a one point eight million dollar purchase price. But here is the critical insight: the company at four and a half times is almost always the better buy, because the agreement base de-risks the entire investment. You are paying more, but you are buying a fundamentally more stable business.

MECHANICS

Let me walk you through the unit economics of a single service agreement so you can see exactly how the money flows. We will use a residential agreement priced at two hundred and nine dollars per year. That is a common price point in mid-market metros.

The customer pays two hundred and nine dollars. Some companies collect this annually, some monthly at around seventeen dollars and forty-two cents per month. Monthly billing has a higher retention rate because the payment feels smaller, but it also creates a small accounts receivable management burden. Either way, the company receives two hundred and nine dollars in revenue per agreement per year.

Now, the cost side. Each agreement includes two maintenance visits. A residential maintenance visit takes a competent technician between forty-five minutes and seventy-five minutes, including drive time. Let us call it one hour per visit, two visits per year, so two technician-hours per agreement per year. If your fully loaded technician cost is forty-two dollars per hour including wages, payroll taxes, benefits, vehicle cost, fuel, tools, and insurance, then your direct labor cost per agreement is eighty-four dollars per year. Add in parts and materials used during maintenance, typically a filter, some cleaning supplies, maybe a capacitor on occasion, and you are looking at another fifteen to twenty-five dollars per agreement per year. Call it twenty dollars. Add a small allocation for scheduling, dispatching, and customer communication at roughly ten dollars per agreement. Your total direct cost to service one agreement is approximately one hundred fourteen dollars.

That leaves you with ninety-five dollars in gross profit per agreement, a gross margin of roughly forty-five percent. That is healthy, but it is not spectacular. If you stopped the analysis here, you might conclude that service agreements are a decent but unexciting revenue stream. That would be a mistake, because the direct profitability of the agreement itself is only the beginning.

The real economic power comes from what happens during and after those maintenance visits. Remember the lead generation function I mentioned earlier. Let us say your technicians are reasonably well-trained and they identify additional work on forty percent of maintenance visits. On a base of five hundred agreements generating one thousand visits per year, that is four hundred leads. Of those four hundred leads, assume a sixty percent close rate, which is conservative for maintenance-discovered issues because the technician is already in the home and has built trust. That gives you two hundred forty completed repair or replacement jobs directly attributable to the agreement base.

If the average repair ticket is three hundred fifty dollars and ten percent of those leads are full system replacements averaging eight thousand dollars, then you have two hundred sixteen repair jobs at three hundred fifty each, which is seventy-five thousand six hundred dollars, plus twenty-four replacement jobs at eight thousand each, which is one hundred ninety-two thousand dollars. The total derived revenue from the agreement base is two hundred sixty-seven thousand six hundred dollars per year, on top of the one hundred four thousand five hundred in direct agreement revenue. The agreement base, which represents one hundred four thousand five hundred in direct revenue, is actually the catalyst for three hundred seventy-two thousand one hundred in total revenue. The true economic contribution of each agreement is not two hundred nine dollars. It is closer to seven hundred forty-four dollars.

This is why sophisticated buyers value agreement bases at three to five times the annual agreement revenue, not the profit. Five hundred agreements at two hundred nine dollars each produces one hundred four thousand five hundred in recurring revenue. At three times, that agreement base is worth three hundred thirteen thousand five hundred dollars. At five times, it is worth five hundred twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars. The premium multiple is justified because the buyer is not just acquiring the maintenance revenue. They are acquiring the customer relationship, the lead generation engine, and the retention flywheel.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through an actual deal structure so you can see how service agreements influence the acquisition math. Imagine you are evaluating Comfort Pro Mechanical, a residential HVAC company in a secondary metro with a population around two hundred fifty thousand. The company has been in business for eighteen years. The owner is sixty-two and wants to retire. Here are the relevant numbers.

Total revenue is two point one million dollars. The company has seven hundred twenty active service agreements priced at an average of one hundred ninety-five dollars per year, generating one hundred forty thousand four hundred in direct agreement revenue. Cost of goods sold runs fifty-two percent, so gross profit is roughly one million eight dollars. Operating expenses including the owner's salary run six hundred fifty thousand. Seller discretionary earnings, which is net income plus owner compensation plus add-backs, comes to four hundred ten thousand dollars.

Without the agreement base, this company might be valued at two point five to three times SDE, placing the enterprise value between one million twenty-five thousand and one million two hundred thirty thousand. But with seven hundred twenty active agreements, a renewal rate of eighty-seven percent, and a documented average agreement tenure of four point two years, the company commands a premium. A fair multiple is three point five to four times SDE, placing enterprise value between one million four hundred thirty-five thousand and one million six hundred forty thousand.

You negotiate a purchase price of one million five hundred thousand dollars. The deal structure looks like this. SBA 7(a) loan covers eighty percent, or one million two hundred thousand, at a variable rate around prime plus two and three quarters, which at current rates puts you around eight and a quarter percent. The term is ten years. Your annual debt service on the SBA loan is approximately one hundred seventy-nine thousand dollars. The remaining three hundred thousand is a seller note at six percent interest, interest-only for two years then amortizing over three years. Year one seller note payments are eighteen thousand in interest only.

Your year one cash flow picture looks like this. SDE of four hundred ten thousand, minus debt service of one hundred seventy-nine thousand on the SBA loan, minus eighteen thousand on the seller note interest, minus thirty thousand for a general manager salary bump since the departing owner was underpaying the GM, leaves you with one hundred eighty-three thousand in pre-tax free cash flow. That is a forty-five percent cash-on-cash return on your three hundred thousand dollar equity investment, assuming the seller note was your only out-of-pocket cost and you structured the rest through the SBA loan.

Now here is the service agreement play that makes this deal even better. Comfort Pro was charging one hundred ninety-five dollars per agreement. The market in that metro will support two hundred twenty-nine dollars, and with an enhanced benefits package including a fifteen percent parts discount and a no-breakdown guarantee, you can push it to two hundred forty-nine dollars at renewal. You do not raise prices on day one. You wait for the natural renewal cycle. Over twelve months, as agreements come up for renewal at the new price point, your agreement revenue increases from one hundred forty thousand four hundred to approximately one hundred fifty-six thousand seven hundred, assuming the same eighty-seven percent renewal rate. You also implement an agreement sales initiative, training your technicians to offer agreements on every service call. A good technician should sell one to two agreements per week. With four technicians, that is two hundred to four hundred new agreements per year. Even at the low end, after twelve months you have gone from seven hundred twenty agreements to roughly eight hundred twenty, and your agreement revenue has grown to over two hundred thousand dollars.

But the downstream effects are what really matter. Those additional one hundred agreements generate two hundred more maintenance visits, which generate roughly eighty more repair and replacement leads, which generate another forty-eight completed jobs, which contribute roughly fifty-five thousand dollars in additional revenue at strong margins. Within eighteen months of acquisition, you have organically grown revenue by over ten percent and improved margins by shifting revenue mix toward higher-margin agreement and agreement-derived work.

RISK ANALYSIS

Now let me walk you through what can go wrong, because service agreements are not risk-free.

The first and most dangerous risk is agreement attrition after acquisition. Customers signed up for Comfort Pro because they trusted the owner, Bob, who has been in their community for eighteen years. When Bob retires and the company changes hands, some percentage of customers will leave regardless of what you do. In a poorly managed transition, attrition can spike to thirty or even forty percent in the first year. This is catastrophic because you paid a premium multiple based on the agreement base, and if that base evaporates, you overpaid for the business. Mitigation is straightforward but critical. First, retain the outgoing owner for a sixty to ninety day transition period, ideally with customer-facing introductions. Second, send a personalized letter from the new ownership to every agreement customer within the first two weeks. Third, do not change the company name, branding, or phone number for at least twelve months. Fourth, ensure the first maintenance visit under new ownership is flawless. Arrive on time. Be thorough. Leave the system cleaner than you found it. That first touch sets the tone for the entire relationship.

The second risk is technician quality. The entire economic model depends on technicians performing competent maintenance visits and identifying legitimate additional work opportunities. If your technicians are poorly trained, they will miss real problems during maintenance visits, and the lead generation engine stalls. If your technicians are overly aggressive, they will recommend unnecessary work, customers will feel scammed, and your renewal rate collapses. The calibration here matters enormously. You need technicians who are technically excellent, honest, and trained in consultative communication. This is a human capital problem, and it requires investment in training, supervision, and compensation that rewards both customer satisfaction and legitimate upsell identification.

The third risk is pricing sensitivity. If you push agreement prices too aggressively or too quickly, you will see renewal rates drop. The sweet spot is usually a five to eight percent annual increase, coupled with a genuine improvement in the benefits package. If you raise prices twelve percent in one year without adding value, expect your renewal rate to drop five to ten points. On a base of seven hundred agreements, a ten-point drop in renewal means seventy lost agreements, which at two hundred forty-nine dollars each is seventeen thousand four hundred thirty in lost direct revenue, plus the downstream lead generation and replacement revenue those seventy accounts would have generated. Price increases must be gradual and justified.

The fourth risk is seasonality of cash collection. If you bill agreements annually and most customers signed up in the fall, you receive a large bolus of cash in September and October and then very little agreement revenue the rest of the year. This creates cash flow timing issues that can be painful if you are also servicing debt. The fix is to move as many customers as possible to monthly billing, which creates a smoother cash flow profile. Most modern HVAC service software, platforms like ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or FieldEdge, support automated monthly billing with stored payment methods.

The fifth risk is competitive displacement. If a well-funded competitor enters your market and offers aggressive agreement pricing, say ninety-nine dollars for the first year, they can poach your customers. This is rare but it happens, particularly in markets where private equity-backed consolidators are active. Your defense is the depth of the customer relationship. A customer who has been on your agreement for four years, trusts your technicians, and has had consistently good experiences is unlikely to switch for a hundred-dollar savings. But a customer who signed up six months ago and has only had one visit is vulnerable. Tenure matters. When evaluating an agreement base for acquisition, look at the average tenure. An average tenure above three years is strong. Below two years is a yellow flag.

ACTION STEPS

Here is exactly what you should do to apply this knowledge. Step one, when evaluating any HVAC acquisition, request the complete agreement roster. This should include every active agreement with the customer name, address, agreement type, price paid, start date, last renewal date, and renewal rate by cohort. If the seller cannot produce this data, that tells you something important about their operational sophistication, and it is not good.

Step two, calculate the agreement coverage ratio. This is the number of active agreements divided by the total number of unique customers served in the last twenty-four months. A healthy HVAC company has an agreement coverage ratio between fifteen and twenty-five percent. Below ten percent means the company has not prioritized agreements and there is significant upside. Above thirty percent is exceptional and you should be willing to pay a premium for it.

Step three, analyze the renewal cohorts. Look at agreement customers by the year they signed up and track how many renewed each subsequent year. You want to see cohort retention rates above eighty percent at year one, above seventy-five percent at year two, and stabilizing above seventy percent at year three and beyond. If you see a sharp drop-off at any point, investigate why. Was there a price increase that was too aggressive? Did a key technician leave? Did service quality decline? The cohort data tells the story.

Step four, stress-test the deal at different attrition scenarios. Model the acquisition assuming fifteen percent agreement attrition in year one, then again at twenty-five percent, then at thirty-five percent. At what attrition level does the deal stop working? If the deal only pencils out at fifteen percent attrition or below, it is fragile. You want a deal that still generates acceptable returns at twenty-five percent attrition because things will go wrong during transitions.

Step five, build a ninety-day agreement growth plan before you close. Know exactly what your target agreement price will be. Know what benefits you will include. Have the technician training program outlined. Have the customer communication templates written. Have the monthly billing migration plan ready. The first ninety days after acquisition are when you set the trajectory for the entire agreement base, and you do not get a second chance to make that first impression.

Step six, benchmark against industry standards. The average residential HVAC company in the United States has between three hundred and five hundred active agreements. Top-performing companies have over a thousand. If the company you are acquiring has fewer than two hundred, the agreement program is likely an afterthought, which means there is enormous upside if you invest in building it out, but also means you cannot rely on agreement revenue in your acquisition underwriting. If it has over five hundred, you are buying a real asset, and you should value it accordingly.

Let me close with this. In my experience advising physician-investors on home services acquisitions, the single best predictor of a successful HVAC deal is the quality and depth of the service agreement base. It is not the revenue number. It is not the truck count. It is not the Google reviews, although those matter. It is the agreements. A robust agreement base means the business has recurring revenue, loyal customers, a built-in lead generation engine, and stable technician utilization. It means the business will survive a mild winter, a rainy summer, or a new competitor entering the market. It means your debt service gets paid even when the weather does not cooperate. And it means that when you eventually exit, whether that is in five years or fifteen years, the next buyer will pay a premium for the same reason you did: because the agreements make the business predictable, and predictability is what sophisticated buyers pay for.

That is service agreement economics. Understand these numbers cold. They will serve you well in every HVAC deal you evaluate. I will see you in the next session.
