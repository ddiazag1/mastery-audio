Assemble before you buy. That is the single most important sentence in this entire module, and I want you to internalize it before we go any further. Every surgeon who has lost money in a business acquisition, every physician who got burned on a deal that looked perfect on paper, they all share one thing in common. They tried to build the plane while flying it. They found the deal first, got excited, wrote a check, and then scrambled to figure out the legal structure, the tax implications, the insurance requirements, and the operational realities after the fact. You are not going to do that. You are going to assemble your advisory team before you ever look at a single business listing, before you attend a single broker meeting, before you sign a single letter of intent. The team comes first. The deal comes second. Always.

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me explain why this matters so much for physicians specifically. You are trained to be the decision maker. In the operating room, the buck stops with you. You assess, you plan, you execute, and you manage complications in real time. That skillset is extraordinary, and it is precisely what makes you dangerous as a business buyer if you do not check it at the door. Business acquisitions are not surgery. You are not the expert here. You are the capital partner, the strategic visionary, and eventually the owner operator or absentee owner, but you are not the expert on tax structuring, legal documentation, loan underwriting, or commercial insurance. You need people who do this every single day, and you need them aligned and communicating before you are under the pressure of a live deal with a ticking clock.

Your advisory team has five core members. First, a CPA who specializes in service businesses and physician tax planning. Second, an M&A attorney who handles small business acquisitions and understands the SBA lending process. Third, an SBA lender who can pre-qualify you before you start shopping. Fourth, an insurance broker who handles commercial general liability, workers compensation, and auto fleet coverage. And fifth, a mentor, someone who has actually bought, operated, and ideally sold a small business, someone who can tell you what the spreadsheet does not show. Each of these five roles is non-negotiable. You do not skip any of them. You do not combine roles. You do not let your personal tax guy who does your ten forty handle the acquisition accounting. You do not let your real estate attorney review a stock purchase agreement. Specialization matters here just as much as it matters in medicine. You would not send a rotator cuff to a hand surgeon. Do not send a business acquisition to a generalist.

Let me be direct about cost because I know that is on your mind. Assembling this team before you have a deal in hand feels like spending money for nothing. It is not nothing. A one hour consultation with a qualified CPA runs one fifty to four hundred dollars depending on your market. An initial meeting with an M&A attorney might cost three hundred to five hundred dollars. SBA lender pre-qualification is free. An insurance broker consultation is free because they earn commission on the policies they place. And a SCORE mentor is completely free because SCORE is a nonprofit funded by the SBA. So your total out of pocket cost to assemble an entire advisory team, have initial conversations with all five members, and get pre-qualified for financing is somewhere between five hundred and fifteen hundred dollars. That is a rounding error on your W-2 income, and it is the single best investment you will make in this entire process. Compare that to the sixty thousand dollar legal bill one of my colleagues received after buying a plumbing company without proper legal review and discovering an undisclosed environmental liability six months later. Five hundred dollars upfront or sixty thousand dollars after the fact. The math is not complicated.

MECHANICS

Let me walk you through each team member in detail, what they do, how to find them, what to ask in your first meeting, and what red flags should make you walk away.

Your CPA is your financial architect. This is not someone who just files your taxes. This is someone who understands how business acquisitions are structured from a tax perspective, who knows the difference between an asset purchase and a stock purchase, who can model the depreciation schedules on equipment and goodwill, who understands Section 338(h)(10) elections, and who can tell you how your new business income will interact with your existing physician income from a tax bracket perspective. When you are earning four hundred thousand or more as a surgeon and you layer a business on top generating another two hundred thousand in pass-through income, the tax planning gets complex fast. You need someone who lives in that complexity.

To find this person, do not Google "CPA near me." Instead, call two or three business brokers in your area and ask them who they see on the other side of deals. Brokers interact with buyer CPAs constantly, and they know who is competent and who is not. You can also check the American Institute of CPAs directory and filter for members with the ABV credential, which is the Accredited in Business Valuation designation. That tells you they have specific training in valuing and analyzing businesses. In your first meeting, ask three questions. First, how many business acquisitions have you supported in the last twelve months? You want the answer to be at least five. Second, do you have physician clients? You want someone who already understands the unique tax situation of a high-income W-2 earner who is adding business income. Third, can you model the tax impact of this acquisition on my overall tax picture before we close? The answer must be yes. Red flags include anyone who has never worked on an acquisition, anyone who seems unfamiliar with SBA loan structures, and anyone who quotes you a flat fee before understanding the scope of work. A good acquisition CPA will charge you somewhere between three thousand and eight thousand dollars for the full engagement, which includes due diligence review of the target company's financials, tax structure recommendations, and closing support.

Your M&A attorney is your deal protector. This is not your estate planning attorney, not your malpractice attorney, and definitely not your buddy from college who went to law school. This is someone who drafts and negotiates asset purchase agreements, who knows what representations and warranties should be in the contract, who understands earnout provisions and seller financing structures, who can navigate the SBA's standard operating procedures because the SBA has very specific requirements about how deals must be structured to qualify for their guaranteed loans. If your attorney does not know SBA requirements, you will waste weeks going back and forth between your lawyer and your lender trying to get the documents right.

Finding an M&A attorney follows the same process as finding a CPA. Ask business brokers. Ask your SBA lender once you have one. Ask at your local SCORE chapter. In your first meeting, ask these questions. How many small business acquisitions have you closed in the last two years? You want at least ten. What percentage of those involved SBA financing? You want at least half. What is your typical fee structure for a buy-side acquisition in the one to three million dollar range? Expect to hear twelve to twenty thousand dollars for a straightforward deal, potentially up to thirty thousand for something complex with real estate, multiple entities, or seller financing components. Red flags include attorneys who primarily do litigation, attorneys who charge hourly with no estimate of total cost, and attorneys who have never worked with SBA lenders. The SBA piece is critical because roughly seventy percent of small business acquisitions by first-time buyers involve SBA 7(a) loans, and if your attorney does not understand that process, your deal will stall.

Your SBA lender is your leverage partner. SBA 7(a) loans are the most common financing vehicle for small business acquisitions, and for good reason. They allow you to buy a business with as little as ten percent down, they offer repayment terms of up to ten years, and the interest rates are typically prime plus two to three percent. As of mid twenty twenty-six, that puts you in the range of nine to ten and a half percent, which sounds high compared to a mortgage but is standard for business acquisition debt. The key advantage for physicians is that SBA lenders love your profile. You have high verifiable income, strong credit scores, and professional stability. You are what lenders call a "preferred borrower."

Getting pre-qualified is free and takes about two weeks. Start by contacting at least three SBA preferred lenders. These are banks that have been granted authority by the SBA to approve loans without sending each application to the SBA for individual review, which means faster closings. Major national SBA lenders include Live Oak Bank, which is the number one SBA lender in the country by volume, Harvest Small Business Finance, and Celtic Bank. You can also work with regional banks that have strong SBA departments. In your pre-qualification conversation, tell the lender your annual income, your credit score range, your available cash for a down payment, and the general size and type of business you are looking to acquire. They will give you a pre-qualification letter that tells you the maximum loan amount they would consider. For a surgeon earning four hundred fifty thousand with good credit and a hundred thousand available for a down payment, you can typically pre-qualify for an SBA loan of seven hundred thousand to one million dollars, which means you can target businesses priced up to about one point one million. That pre-qualification letter also makes you a credible buyer in the eyes of brokers and sellers. It is the business acquisition equivalent of a mortgage pre-approval, and no serious seller will entertain your offer without one.

Your insurance broker is your risk manager. Before you close on any business, you need to understand the full insurance picture, what coverage the business currently carries, what coverage you will need, and what it will cost. The four main policies you will need are commercial general liability, which covers slip and fall type claims and runs anywhere from one thousand to five thousand per year depending on the business type. Workers compensation, which is mandatory in almost every state if you have employees and is priced as a percentage of payroll, typically one to five percent depending on the industry classification. Commercial auto if the business operates vehicles, which can run three to eight thousand per year per vehicle depending on the type and use. And an umbrella policy that sits on top of everything, typically one to two million in additional coverage for one to three thousand per year.

Finding a commercial insurance broker is straightforward. Look for an independent broker, not a captive agent for a single carrier. Independent brokers can shop your coverage across multiple insurance companies and find the best combination of coverage and price. Ask other business owners for referrals, or search for commercial insurance brokers through the Independent Insurance Agents and Brokers of America directory. The initial consultation is free. Bring the business's current insurance policies or at minimum its declarations pages, and your broker will tell you whether the coverage is adequate, what changes you need at closing, and what the annual cost will be. This number, your total annual insurance cost, needs to go directly into your financial model. I have seen physicians budget zero for insurance in their acquisition proformas because they simply forgot about it. On a service business with ten employees and three vehicles, your total annual insurance cost can easily be thirty to forty-five thousand dollars. That is real money that comes directly off your bottom line.

Your mentor is your reality check. This is perhaps the most undervalued member of your team, and ironically, this one is free. SCORE, which stands for the Service Corps of Retired Executives, is an SBA-funded nonprofit that provides free mentoring to entrepreneurs. You can find a mentor at score dot org, and I specifically recommend searching for someone with experience in the industry you are targeting. If you are looking at HVAC companies, find a mentor who has owned or operated an HVAC business. If you are looking at home services, find someone from that world. The alternative to SCORE is finding a mentor on your own. If you are targeting HVAC, go to your local HVAC supply house and ask the counter staff which owners in town have been around the longest. Call that person, take them to lunch, and tell them you are a surgeon looking to buy an HVAC company and you would value their perspective. I have never seen this approach fail. Business owners, especially ones who have been through acquisitions, love talking about their experience. They will tell you things that no CPA, attorney, or lender will ever tell you. They will tell you which technicians are worth their weight in gold and which ones will walk the day after a sale. They will tell you which suppliers give kickbacks and which ones will drop you the moment they hear about a change of ownership. They will tell you about the seasonal cash flow dynamics that make January terrifying and July euphoric. This is operational intelligence that you cannot get from a spreadsheet, and it is absolutely free.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me put this all together with a concrete example. Say you have identified a residential plumbing company listed at eight hundred fifty thousand dollars. The business does one point two million in revenue with three hundred thousand in seller discretionary earnings. Here is how your advisory team engages, in sequence.

First, your SBA lender confirms you are pre-qualified for a loan of this size. You need roughly eighty-five thousand as a down payment, ten percent of the purchase price, plus another fifteen to twenty thousand for closing costs, due diligence expenses, and working capital reserves. Call it a hundred to a hundred and ten thousand total cash to close.

Second, your M&A attorney reviews the listing materials, the confidential information memorandum, and any financials provided by the broker. They advise you on what to include in your letter of intent and help you submit it. The LOI is typically non-binding but sets the framework for price, terms, and the due diligence period.

Third, once the LOI is accepted, your CPA begins financial due diligence. They are reviewing three years of tax returns, profit and loss statements, balance sheets, bank statements, and accounts receivable aging reports. They are looking for inconsistencies, red flags, and opportunities. Maybe the seller has been running eighty thousand a year in personal expenses through the business. That is actually good news for you because it means the true earnings are higher than reported. Maybe they have deferred maintenance on their equipment, which means you need to budget for capital expenditures in year one. Your CPA will model all of this and give you an adjusted earnings figure that represents what the business will actually produce under your ownership.

Fourth, your insurance broker reviews the existing coverage and quotes the policies you will need at closing. They identify that the current owner has been underinsured on workers compensation, which is a red flag that your attorney needs to address in the purchase agreement through representations and warranties about prior claims.

Fifth, your mentor reviews the deal from a practical standpoint. They tell you that the seller's lead plumber, who drives sixty percent of the revenue, has been there for eighteen years and is likely to stay only if he gets a raise and a title change. They tell you that the company's Google reviews are strong but the website is terrible, which represents an easy growth opportunity. They tell you that residential plumbing in your market is seasonal and you should expect a forty percent revenue drop in November through February, so make sure your cash reserves can cover payroll during the slow months.

All five team members are now giving you intelligence that shapes your decision. Your CPA says the deal works financially. Your attorney says the contract protects you. Your lender says the financing is approved. Your insurance broker says the risk is manageable. Your mentor says the operation is sound. You close the deal with confidence, not hope.

RISK ANALYSIS

Let me be honest about what goes wrong when you skip this process. The most common failure mode is the physician who finds a deal through a friend or a broker, gets emotionally attached to it, and rushes to close without proper diligence. They use their personal CPA who has never reviewed business financials for an acquisition. They use their estate planning attorney who has never drafted an asset purchase agreement. They skip SBA financing and pay all cash because it seems faster. They do not think about insurance until after closing. And they have no mentor telling them what questions to ask.

Here is what that looks like financially. You pay nine hundred thousand cash for a business that was worth seven hundred thousand because nobody told you the earnings were overstated. You miss the environmental liability in the old building because your attorney did not know to ask for Phase I reports. You are underinsured for six months because nobody told you the prior coverage lapsed at closing. And your best employee quits three weeks in because you did not know that the seller had promised him a partnership stake that you are now not honoring. Total damage: two hundred thousand in overpayment, sixty thousand in environmental remediation, a forty thousand dollar workers comp claim with no coverage, and a thirty percent revenue decline from the lost employee. You just turned a nine hundred thousand dollar investment into a four hundred thousand dollar headache. I have seen this exact scenario play out with two different physician buyers, and in both cases, the disaster was entirely preventable with a properly assembled advisory team.

The second risk worth discussing is advisor conflict of interest. Your SBA lender wants the deal to close because they earn fees. Your broker wants the deal to close because they earn commission. Even your attorney earns more on a complex closing than on a deal that falls apart. The only people on your team with zero financial incentive tied to the deal closing are your CPA and your mentor. This is why those two voices are so important in the final decision. When your CPA says the numbers do not work, listen. When your mentor says the operation has problems that money will not fix, listen. They have no reason to tell you anything other than the truth.

The third risk is team fragmentation. Your advisors need to communicate with each other, not just with you. Your CPA needs to talk to your attorney about how to structure the purchase for tax optimization. Your attorney needs to talk to your lender about SBA requirements for the closing documents. Your insurance broker needs to talk to your attorney about coverage requirements in the purchase agreement. You are the quarterback, but you are not the only line of communication. In your first meeting with each advisor, tell them who the other team members are and give permission for them to communicate directly. This saves you from being a bottleneck and reduces the risk of miscommunication.

ACTION STEPS

Here is exactly what I want you to do in the next fourteen days, and I am going to be very specific because I know your time is limited.

Days one through three. Go to score dot org and register as a client. Search for a mentor with experience in the industry you are most interested in acquiring. Request a meeting. This takes ten minutes online and the first meeting can be done by video call.

Days four through seven. Call two business brokers in your market and ask each of them for their top recommendation for a CPA who handles acquisition work and an M&A attorney who does SBA deals. You will likely get overlapping recommendations, which tells you who the real players are in your area. Schedule initial consultations with one CPA and one attorney.

Days eight through ten. Contact Live Oak Bank, Harvest Small Business Finance, and one local SBA preferred lender. Tell them you are a physician interested in pre-qualification for a small business acquisition loan. Provide your income documentation and credit information. This can be done entirely by email and phone.

Days eleven through fourteen. Ask your CPA or attorney for a referral to a commercial insurance broker. Schedule a brief introductory call so the broker knows who you are and what you are planning. No cost here, just relationship building.

At the end of fourteen days, you will have all five advisory team members identified and engaged. You will have a pre-qualification letter from at least one SBA lender. You will understand the approximate cost of legal and accounting support for an acquisition. And you will have a mentor who can pressure-test your thinking on any deal you consider. Total time invested: maybe six to eight hours spread across two weeks. Total cash invested: five hundred to fifteen hundred dollars. Total value created: immeasurable, because you are now approaching business acquisition like the disciplined, well-advised professional you are, not like an amateur writing checks based on excitement.

One final thought. The best advisory team is one you build before you need it. Do not wait until you find the perfect deal to start assembling your team. By the time you find that deal, someone else who already has their team in place will move faster, negotiate better, and close before you even get your CPA on the phone. The team comes first. The deal comes second. Always. Assemble before you buy.
