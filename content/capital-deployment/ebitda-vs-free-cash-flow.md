EBITDA versus Free Cash Flow. This is one of the most important distinctions you will ever learn in business acquisition and capital deployment. If you only remember one thing from this lesson, let it be this: EBITDA is not cash flow. It has never been cash flow. And every single time a business broker or seller quotes you a number, they are quoting EBITDA because it is the larger, more flattering number. Your job, as the buyer, is to cut through that and calculate what actually matters, which is free cash flow. That is the money you can actually put in your pocket, reinvest, or use to service debt. Everything else is theater.

Let me set the stage for why this matters to you specifically. You are a high-income surgeon. You earn somewhere between eight hundred thousand and one point three million dollars a year in clinical income. You are now looking at deploying capital into businesses, whether that is a laundromat, a medical equipment leasing company, a franchise, or an existing small business. The brokers you will deal with have one goal: to make the business look as profitable as possible so they can justify their asking price. The single most common tool they use to do this is EBITDA. And if you do not understand the gap between EBITDA and free cash flow, you will overpay for businesses, you will miscalculate your returns, and you will find yourself subsidizing a business from your surgical income instead of the other way around.

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. On the surface, it sounds like a reasonable way to measure how a business performs. You take the net income and you add back interest payments, tax obligations, depreciation charges, and amortization. The idea is that you strip away financing decisions, tax strategies, and non-cash accounting entries to reveal the core operating profitability of the business. And in theory, that is useful. It lets you compare two businesses side by side regardless of how they are financed or how aggressively they depreciate their assets. Investment bankers and private equity firms use EBITDA constantly for this exact reason.

But here is the problem. EBITDA ignores several enormous categories of real cash spending. It ignores capital expenditures, meaning the money you have to spend to maintain and replace the physical assets of the business. It ignores changes in working capital, meaning the cash tied up in inventory, accounts receivable, and accounts payable as the business operates. It ignores debt service, meaning the principal payments you must make on any loans used to buy or run the business. It ignores owner distributions and draws. And it ignores one-time or irregular items that nonetheless require real cash to resolve. When you add all of those back, the actual cash available to you as the owner can be dramatically less than the EBITDA number suggests. Sometimes it is half. Sometimes it is even less than half.

Free cash flow is the number that tells you the truth. The basic formula is straightforward. You start with EBITDA, and then you subtract capital expenditures, subtract changes in working capital, and subtract cash taxes paid. What remains is free cash flow. This is the cash the business actually generates after maintaining itself, after funding its operations, and after paying the government. This is what you can use to service acquisition debt, pay yourself, or reinvest for growth. When someone tells you a business does two hundred and seventy thousand in EBITDA, you need to immediately ask what the capital expenditure requirements are, what the working capital cycle looks like, and what the actual tax burden is. Only then will you know what you are actually buying.

MECHANICS

Let me walk you through the mechanics in detail so you understand exactly how each component works and why it matters.

Capital expenditures, often called CapEx, represent the money a business must spend on physical assets to keep operating. This is not optional spending. This is the cost of replacing trucks that wear out, equipment that breaks, roofs that leak, and technology that becomes obsolete. Depreciation, which EBITDA adds back, is supposed to be the accounting proxy for this wear and tear. But depreciation is calculated using IRS schedules and accounting conventions that almost never match reality. A delivery truck might be depreciated over five years on paper, but in practice it might need replacement every three years if the business runs heavy routes. A piece of medical equipment might be depreciated over seven years, but a new model comes out in four years and your competitors all upgrade, forcing you to do the same. The gap between depreciation on the books and actual required capital expenditure in cash is one of the biggest traps in business valuation.

Here is how this plays out in numbers. Suppose a business shows forty thousand dollars a year in depreciation expense. EBITDA adds that back, making the business look forty thousand dollars more profitable. But in reality, the business needs to spend eighty thousand dollars a year replacing aging equipment. That means the true capital expenditure is double the depreciation charge. EBITDA overstates the cash-generating ability of this business by forty thousand dollars. That might not sound like much, but when you are valuing a business at a multiple of EBITDA, that forty thousand dollar error gets multiplied. At a four times multiple, you just overpaid by one hundred and sixty thousand dollars for the business. At a five times multiple, two hundred thousand.

Working capital is the second major item EBITDA ignores. Working capital is the cash tied up in the day-to-day operations of a business. It includes inventory that sits on shelves, accounts receivable from customers who have not paid yet, minus accounts payable that the business owes to its suppliers. When a business is growing, working capital requirements increase. You need more inventory. You have more receivables outstanding. That growth consumes cash even though it does not show up as an expense on the income statement and therefore does not reduce EBITDA.

Consider a service business that is growing ten percent a year. It might need an additional thirty to forty thousand dollars in working capital each year to support that growth. That is real cash going out the door, cash that the owner cannot touch, cash that is not available for debt service or distributions. EBITDA completely ignores this. A business showing two hundred and seventy thousand in EBITDA that requires forty thousand in incremental working capital each year is really only generating two hundred and thirty thousand before you even get to CapEx and taxes.

Taxes are the third piece. EBITDA is before taxes by definition. But you absolutely pay taxes. A business with two hundred and seventy thousand in EBITDA, after deducting real depreciation and interest, might show taxable income of one hundred and eighty thousand. Depending on your structure and your marginal rate, you could owe forty to sixty thousand dollars in taxes. That is real cash leaving the business. You cannot pretend it does not exist just because EBITDA ignores it.

Finally, debt service. If you are acquiring a business with an SBA loan or seller financing, you will have principal and interest payments. EBITDA adds back interest and ignores principal entirely. But your lender does not ignore principal. If you take a five hundred thousand dollar SBA loan at eight percent over ten years, your annual debt service is roughly seventy-three thousand dollars, of which about thirty-three thousand is principal in the early years. EBITDA treats that thirty-three thousand dollars as if it does not exist. But it absolutely exists. It comes out of your bank account every single month.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me give you a concrete example that ties all of this together. You are looking at a regional medical courier service. The business picks up lab specimens, delivers medical supplies, and runs time-sensitive routes between hospitals, clinics, and reference labs in a mid-sized metro area. The broker sends you a Confidential Information Memorandum, the CIM, and right there on the front page it says the business generates two hundred and seventy thousand dollars in adjusted EBITDA. They are asking one point zero eight million dollars, which represents a four-times EBITDA multiple. The broker tells you this is a fair price for a healthcare-adjacent service business with recurring contracts. On the surface, it looks attractive. You run the quick math in your head: two hundred and seventy thousand divided by one point zero eight million is a twenty-five percent return on investment. That sounds great compared to the stock market. But then you dig in.

You request the detailed financials and start building your free cash flow analysis. First, capital expenditures. The business runs twelve delivery vehicles. The average vehicle costs thirty-five thousand dollars and lasts about five years in this kind of service before it needs replacement. That means the business needs to replace roughly two to three vehicles per year at a cost of thirty-five thousand each. Call it eighty thousand dollars a year in vehicle replacement CapEx. The CIM shows depreciation of forty-five thousand. The broker added that back to get to EBITDA, but the real cash cost of maintaining the fleet is eighty thousand, nearly double the depreciation figure. You are already down to one hundred and ninety thousand.

Next, working capital. The business has contracts with hospitals and clinics that pay on net-thirty and sometimes net-forty-five terms. That means the business is always carrying about sixty days of revenue in accounts receivable. Meanwhile, it has to pay its drivers every two weeks and cover fuel costs weekly. This mismatch between when cash comes in and when it goes out requires the business to maintain a working capital reserve. Reviewing three years of balance sheets, you see that working capital needs have been growing by about forty thousand dollars per year as the company adds routes and contracts. Forty thousand more out of your pocket. You are now at one hundred and fifty thousand.

Now taxes. After accounting for real depreciation, interest on acquisition debt, and legitimate business expenses, the taxable income from this business will be approximately one hundred and forty thousand dollars. At a blended federal and state rate of thirty percent, you are looking at forty-two thousand in cash taxes. But wait. Some of this tax burden is offset by the depreciation and interest deductions on your acquisition loan, so let us say the incremental tax hit is closer to twenty-five thousand once you factor in the loan deductions. Still, that brings you to one hundred and twenty-five thousand.

And then debt service. You are financing the purchase with a seven hundred and fifty thousand dollar SBA loan and putting up three hundred and thirty thousand of your own capital. The SBA loan at eight and a half percent over ten years carries annual debt service of roughly one hundred and twelve thousand dollars. That includes both principal and interest. EBITDA only excluded the interest portion, but the principal portion, roughly forty thousand in year one, is also cash going out the door.

So let us total this up. You started with two hundred and seventy thousand in EBITDA. You subtract eighty thousand for real capital expenditures. You subtract forty thousand for working capital growth. You subtract twenty-five thousand for taxes. You are left with one hundred and twenty-five thousand in free cash flow. Out of that, you need to cover one hundred and twelve thousand in debt service. That leaves you thirteen thousand dollars. Thirteen thousand dollars in actual cash flow to you after servicing the debt on a business you paid over a million dollars for, put three hundred and thirty thousand of your own cash into, and took on significant personal risk to acquire. That is a far cry from the twenty-five percent return the EBITDA number implied.

Now, does that mean the deal is automatically bad? Not necessarily. The debt is being paid down, building equity. The vehicles you replace become assets on your balance sheet. The business might increase prices or add routes over time. But the point is this: the broker's EBITDA number of two hundred and seventy thousand made this look like a cash machine. The free cash flow analysis reveals it is much closer to a break-even proposition in the early years. If you had relied on EBITDA, you might have paid one point zero eight million dollars expecting to pocket two hundred thousand a year, only to discover you were netting thirteen thousand and one bad month away from subsidizing the business from your surgical salary.

RISK ANALYSIS

Understanding the EBITDA-to-free-cash-flow gap also reveals where the risks hide in a deal.

The first risk is deferred maintenance. Sellers who know they are going to sell often defer capital expenditures in the years leading up to the sale. They squeeze every last mile out of trucks, delay equipment upgrades, and push off facility repairs. This inflates EBITDA in the trailing twelve months, which is exactly the period used for valuation. You buy the business at a multiple of inflated EBITDA, and then in year one you are hit with a hundred thousand dollars in catch-up CapEx that should have been spent by the seller. Always look at CapEx as a percentage of revenue over a five-year period, not just the last twelve months. If CapEx dropped significantly in the last year or two, that is a red flag.

The second risk is customer concentration and working capital volatility. If one hospital system represents forty percent of the courier business's revenue and that system switches to a competitor or brings the service in-house, you lose forty percent of revenue overnight. But your working capital does not adjust instantly. You still have vehicles, you still have drivers under contract, and you still have lease obligations. EBITDA cannot capture this tail risk. Free cash flow analysis, combined with customer concentration analysis, gives you a much clearer picture.

The third risk is what I call the growth trap. A business that is growing rapidly can show beautiful EBITDA while burning cash. Revenue goes up, margins hold, EBITDA rises. But every new contract requires new vehicles, more drivers, higher insurance, and more working capital. The faster it grows, the more cash it consumes. I have seen businesses with three hundred thousand in EBITDA that had negative free cash flow because they were growing so quickly that CapEx and working capital were eating everything. If a seller is showing you strong EBITDA growth as a selling point, you need to ask how much cash went into funding that growth.

The fourth risk is EBITDA adjustments, sometimes called add-backs. Sellers and their brokers love to adjust EBITDA upward by adding back expenses they claim are one-time or personal in nature. The owner's wife is on payroll for sixty thousand but does not actually work in the business. The owner runs personal travel through the company. There was a one-time legal settlement. These add-backs can be legitimate, but they can also be aggressive or outright fictitious. I have seen CIMs where the add-backs were larger than the unadjusted EBITDA. That should make you very nervous. Every add-back needs to be verified and stress-tested. Just because the broker calls it adjusted EBITDA does not make it real.

The fifth risk is interest rate sensitivity. When you calculate free cash flow after debt service, the interest rate on your acquisition loan is a critical variable. In the courier example, I used eight and a half percent. If rates are at ten percent, your annual debt service increases by roughly eight thousand dollars, and your thirteen thousand in residual cash flow drops to five thousand. If rates are at seven percent, your cash flow improves to about twenty thousand. A swing of a few percentage points in interest rates can mean the difference between a deal that works and one that does not. EBITDA does not capture this because it deliberately excludes interest. Free cash flow after debt service absolutely captures it.

ACTION STEPS

Here is what you do with this knowledge, translated into concrete actions you can take on your next deal.

Step one. Every time a broker or seller quotes you an EBITDA number, immediately ask for the full financial statements, not summaries, not the CIM version, but actual profit and loss statements, balance sheets, and cash flow statements for the past three to five years. You need all three. The P and L gives you EBITDA. The balance sheet gives you working capital. The cash flow statement gives you actual CapEx. If they will not provide these, walk away.

Step two. Build your own free cash flow model. Start with their EBITDA. Subtract maintenance CapEx using the five-year average, not the trailing twelve months. Subtract the annual change in working capital using the three-year average. Subtract estimated cash taxes based on your actual tax situation, not theirs. What you have left is free cash flow before debt service. Then subtract your projected debt service based on realistic loan terms. What remains is your actual cash return.

Step three. Calculate the real return on your invested capital. Take the free cash flow after debt service and divide it by your total equity investment, meaning your down payment plus any additional cash you need to inject. In the courier example, thirteen thousand divided by three hundred and thirty thousand equals a three point nine percent cash-on-cash return in year one. That is barely better than a high-yield savings account, and it comes with infinitely more risk. You need to decide if the equity build-up, tax benefits, and growth potential justify that thin initial return.

Step four. Stress-test the free cash flow under downside scenarios. What happens if revenue drops fifteen percent? What if a major customer leaves? What if you need to replace four vehicles in one year instead of two? What if interest rates rise before you close? Run each scenario and see what happens to your free cash flow. If one bad quarter puts you into negative free cash flow, the deal is too thin. You need a margin of safety.

Step five. Use free cash flow, not EBITDA, as the basis for your valuation and your offer. If a business generates one hundred and twenty-five thousand in free cash flow before debt service, and you want a fifteen percent return on total investment, the most you should pay is eight hundred and thirty-three thousand, not the one point zero eight million the broker is asking based on four times EBITDA. This reframing will dramatically change your negotiating position. When the broker says the business is worth four times EBITDA, you respond with your free cash flow analysis and explain why the real value is lower. Some brokers will dismiss you. Good brokers will respect the analysis and work with you.

Step six. Make free cash flow conversion a screening criterion. Before you even get to detailed due diligence, calculate the approximate free cash flow conversion ratio for any business you are considering. That ratio is free cash flow divided by EBITDA. A healthy, capital-light service business might convert seventy to eighty percent of EBITDA to free cash flow. A capital-intensive business like the courier service might convert only forty-five to fifty-five percent. If the conversion ratio is below fifty percent, you need to understand exactly why before proceeding. Some industries are inherently capital-intensive and that is fine if the EBITDA is high enough and the purchase multiple is low enough. But you need to go in with eyes open.

Step seven. During due diligence, examine the seller's CapEx spending relative to depreciation over the past five years. If cumulative CapEx is significantly less than cumulative depreciation, the seller has been under-investing and you are inheriting a maintenance backlog. If CapEx exceeds depreciation, the business has been well-maintained or is investing for growth, both of which are positive signals. This ratio is one of the most underused but most revealing metrics in business acquisition.

Let me close with this. The gap between EBITDA and free cash flow is where fortunes are made and lost in business acquisition. Sophisticated buyers, private equity firms, and experienced operators all know this. They buy on free cash flow and they sell on EBITDA. The seller and the broker will always present the EBITDA number because it is bigger and it justifies a higher price. Your job is to convert that EBITDA into free cash flow and make decisions based on reality, not accounting abstractions. This single skill, the ability to bridge from EBITDA to free cash flow, will save you hundreds of thousands of dollars over your acquisition career and prevent you from buying businesses that look profitable on paper but starve you of cash in practice. Master this, and you have a permanent edge over ninety percent of buyers in the small business acquisition market.
