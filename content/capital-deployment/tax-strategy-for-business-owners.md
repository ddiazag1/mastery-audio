CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me be direct with you. The single largest drag on your wealth as a high-income surgeon is not market volatility, not bad real estate deals, not even malpractice premiums. It is taxes. At an income north of a million dollars per year, you are handing over somewhere between three hundred fifty thousand and four hundred fifty thousand dollars annually to the federal government, your state, and payroll taxes. Over a seven-year accumulation period, that is roughly two and a half to three million dollars in taxes. And here is the key insight that changes everything: every single percentage point you reduce your effective tax rate translates to approximately one hundred fifty thousand dollars more in your pocket at the end of year seven. That is not theory. That is arithmetic. If you move your effective rate from thirty-eight percent down to thirty-two percent, you just freed up nine hundred thousand dollars over seven years. That is a down payment on a surgical center. That is a fully funded retirement account. That is generational wealth.

Now, the vast majority of employed surgeons operate as pure W-2 earners. They show up, they operate, they get a paycheck, and the hospital withholds taxes before they ever see the money. The surgeon has almost zero control over their tax situation. Maybe they max out a 401(k), maybe they do a backdoor Roth, and that is about where the conversation ends. But the moment you transition from pure W-2 to what I call the triad model, which is W-2 income plus business owner income plus investor income, you unlock an entirely different playbook. You go from being a passive participant in the tax code to an active architect of your own tax position.

This is not about doing anything aggressive or gray-area. This is about understanding that the tax code was written with two audiences in mind: individual wage earners and business owners. The provisions for business owners are dramatically more favorable, and they exist because Congress wants to incentivize business formation, capital investment, job creation, and real estate development. When you form an LLC, elect S-Corp status, purchase equipment, hire staff, invest in real estate, or fund a retirement plan through your business, you are doing exactly what the tax code was designed to reward. The question is not whether you should take advantage of these provisions. The question is why you have not started already.

MECHANICS

Let us walk through the structural mechanics of each major tax strategy, starting with entity selection. You have three primary choices: a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship, an LLC with an S-Corp election, and a C-Corporation. For almost every high-income physician with side income, the S-Corp election is the right answer, and I will explain exactly why.

When you operate as a sole proprietor, every dollar of net profit is subject to self-employment tax, which is the combined Social Security and Medicare tax of fifteen point three percent. On the first one hundred sixty-eight thousand six hundred dollars of income in 2025, you pay the full fifteen point three percent. Above that threshold, you still pay the two point nine percent Medicare portion, and once your income exceeds two hundred fifty thousand as a married couple, you pay an additional point nine percent Medicare surtax. So on a hundred thousand dollars of consulting income run through a sole proprietorship, you are paying roughly fifteen thousand three hundred dollars in self-employment tax on top of your regular income tax. That is pure waste if you have the right structure.

Enter the S-Corporation. When your LLC elects S-Corp status with the IRS by filing Form 2553, the entity becomes a pass-through entity that separates your income into two buckets: reasonable compensation, which is your W-2 salary from the S-Corp, and distributions, which is the remaining profit. The critical distinction is that only the reasonable compensation portion is subject to payroll taxes. The distributions pass through to your personal return as ordinary income, but they are exempt from self-employment tax. So if your medical-legal consulting LLC earns two hundred thousand dollars in net revenue, and you pay yourself a reasonable salary of eighty thousand dollars, the remaining one hundred twenty thousand in distributions avoids the fifteen point three percent self-employment tax. That is a savings of roughly eighteen thousand dollars per year. Over seven years, that is one hundred twenty-six thousand dollars, and that is just from the entity election alone.

Now, what counts as reasonable compensation? The IRS does not publish a bright-line number, but they look at several factors: what similar roles pay in your geographic area, the time you spend on the business, your training and expertise, and the revenue generated. For a surgeon doing medical-legal work, an eighty to one hundred thousand dollar salary on two hundred thousand in revenue is defensible. You do not want to set it at thirty thousand and take one hundred seventy thousand in distributions. That will invite an audit, and you will lose. The general guideline I recommend is setting reasonable compensation at approximately forty to fifty percent of net business income, and always having a documented rationale.

The C-Corporation is generally not the right answer for most physician side businesses, and here is why. A C-Corp pays its own federal tax at a flat twenty-one percent rate, which sounds attractive compared to your personal marginal rate of thirty-seven percent. But the problem is double taxation. When you pull money out of the C-Corp as dividends, you pay tax again at the qualified dividend rate of twenty percent plus the three point eight percent net investment income tax. So the effective combined rate on C-Corp income that reaches your pocket is approximately thirty-nine point eight percent, which is actually higher than your marginal rate as an individual. There are narrow scenarios where a C-Corp makes sense, specifically if you plan to retain large amounts of earnings inside the corporation for investment purposes, but for the typical physician side business generating one hundred to four hundred thousand per year, S-Corp is the way to go.

Next, let us talk about the Qualified Business Income deduction, commonly called the QBI deduction or Section 199A. This provision allows owners of pass-through businesses to deduct up to twenty percent of their qualified business income from their taxable income. On two hundred thousand dollars of S-Corp profit flowing to your personal return, that is a forty thousand dollar deduction, saving you roughly fourteen thousand eight hundred dollars in federal tax at the thirty-seven percent bracket. However, there is a significant limitation for specified service trades or businesses, which includes health care, law, consulting, and financial services. Once your taxable income exceeds three hundred sixty-four thousand two hundred dollars for married filing jointly in 2025, the QBI deduction begins to phase out, and it is completely eliminated above four hundred sixty-four thousand two hundred dollars.

Here is where it gets strategically interesting. If your W-2 surgical income already puts you above the phase-out threshold, your medical-legal consulting income will not qualify for the QBI deduction directly. But if you have other business income that is not a specified service trade, such as rental income from a real estate business, equipment leasing, or a management company, that income can qualify for the full twenty percent deduction regardless of your total income, subject to the W-2 wage and property basis limitations. This is one reason why diversifying your business income streams matters beyond just revenue. The tax treatment of different income types creates compounding advantages.

Now let us cover depreciation strategies, starting with Section 179 and bonus depreciation. Section 179 allows you to deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment and software in the year you buy it, rather than depreciating it over several years. The limit for 2025 is one million two hundred fifty thousand dollars. Bonus depreciation, which is a separate provision under Section 168(k), allows one hundred percent first-year depreciation on qualifying new and used assets, though this percentage began phasing down after 2022 and is now at forty percent for 2025, dropping by twenty percentage points each subsequent year.

For a physician with a consulting practice, Section 179 applies to things like computers, office furniture, software, medical-legal reference libraries, and even vehicles over six thousand pounds gross vehicle weight. If you purchase a sixty-five thousand dollar SUV that qualifies under the heavy vehicle exception, you can potentially deduct the full amount in year one under Section 179, rather than depreciating it over five years. That sixty-five thousand dollar deduction saves you roughly twenty-four thousand dollars in federal tax in the year of purchase. The vehicle must be used more than fifty percent for business, and you need to maintain a mileage log to support the business use percentage.

Cost segregation is the depreciation strategy that applies specifically to real estate, and it is one of the most powerful tax tools available to physician investors. When you purchase a commercial or residential rental property, the building itself depreciates over twenty-seven and a half years for residential or thirty-nine years for commercial. But a cost segregation study, performed by a qualified engineering firm, reclassifies components of the building into shorter depreciation categories. Carpet, cabinetry, appliances, and certain fixtures become five-year property. Paving, landscaping, and site improvements become fifteen-year property. Electrical and plumbing components serving specific equipment can also be reclassified.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through a concrete scenario. Say you are earning one point three million dollars in W-2 surgical income. You have formed an LLC called Diaz Medical Consulting, elected S-Corp status, and you are generating two hundred thousand dollars per year in medical-legal consulting revenue. You also purchased a four-unit residential rental property for six hundred thousand dollars.

Starting with the consulting LLC. Your S-Corp pays you a salary of ninety thousand dollars and distributes one hundred ten thousand in profit. The self-employment tax savings on that one hundred ten thousand in distributions is approximately sixteen thousand eight hundred dollars per year. Your S-Corp also purchases a fifty-five thousand dollar vehicle for site inspections and depositions, which you deduct fully under Section 179 in year one, saving you approximately twenty thousand three hundred fifty dollars in federal tax.

Through the S-Corp, you also establish a Solo 401(k). As both the employee and employer, you can contribute up to sixty-nine thousand dollars in 2025. You defer twenty-three thousand five hundred as the employee elective deferral, and the S-Corp contributes an additional twenty-five percent of your ninety thousand dollar W-2 salary, which is twenty-two thousand five hundred, for a total of forty-six thousand dollars. Each dollar deferred saves you thirty-seven cents in federal tax, so the forty-six thousand dollar contribution saves you approximately seventeen thousand twenty dollars. If your consulting income grows and you want to shelter even more, you can layer on a defined benefit plan, which for a surgeon in their late thirties or early forties can allow annual contributions of one hundred fifty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand dollars or more, depending on actuarial calculations. The defined benefit plan contributions are fully deductible by the business.

Now, the rental property. You purchased the four-unit building for six hundred thousand dollars. The land is worth one hundred thousand, so your depreciable basis is five hundred thousand. Under standard depreciation over twenty-seven and a half years, your annual depreciation deduction is eighteen thousand one hundred eighty-two dollars. But you hire a cost segregation firm, which costs around eight thousand dollars for a study on a property of this size. The study reclassifies one hundred fifty thousand dollars of the building into five-year property and fifty thousand dollars into fifteen-year property. Using bonus depreciation at the current forty percent rate, you accelerate sixty thousand dollars of the five-year property into year one, plus the regular first-year depreciation on the remaining amounts. The net result is a first-year depreciation deduction of approximately ninety-five thousand dollars instead of eighteen thousand. That additional seventy-seven thousand in deductions, combined with your other rental expenses, likely creates a paper loss on the property even though it is cash-flow positive.

Now, here is a critical detail. If you qualify as a Real Estate Professional Status taxpayer, or if your modified adjusted gross income is below one hundred fifty thousand, you can use those passive losses to offset your active income. Most employed surgeons earning over one hundred fifty thousand will not be able to deduct passive losses against their surgical income unless they or their spouse qualifies for Real Estate Professional Status by spending seven hundred fifty or more hours per year in real estate activities and more time in real estate than in any other occupation. This is where having a spouse who manages the rental properties can become a legitimate tax planning strategy, but it must be real, documented, and substantive. The hours requirement is strict, and the IRS scrutinizes it heavily.

Let us tally up the year-one tax impact of this structure. Self-employment tax savings from S-Corp election: sixteen thousand eight hundred. Section 179 vehicle deduction tax savings: twenty thousand three hundred fifty. Solo 401(k) contribution tax savings: seventeen thousand twenty. Accelerated depreciation from cost segregation: roughly twenty-eight thousand five hundred in additional tax savings. Total first-year tax reduction: approximately eighty-two thousand six hundred seventy dollars. On a base income of one point five million, that moves your effective tax rate from roughly thirty-seven point five percent down to approximately thirty-two percent. That five and a half point reduction, sustained and compounded over seven years, represents over eight hundred thousand dollars in retained wealth.

And we have not even discussed 1031 exchanges yet. A 1031 exchange, also called a like-kind exchange, allows you to sell an investment property and defer all capital gains taxes by reinvesting the proceeds into a replacement property of equal or greater value within one hundred eighty days. You must identify potential replacement properties within forty-five days of the sale and use a qualified intermediary to hold the funds. You never touch the money directly. The power of the 1031 is that it allows you to trade up indefinitely, deferring gains for decades. If you bought that four-unit building for six hundred thousand, it appreciates to eight hundred thousand over four years, and you sell and 1031 into a one point two million dollar twelve-unit apartment building, you defer the two hundred thousand in gains entirely. Those deferred taxes remain invested and compounding. At your death, your heirs receive a stepped-up basis, potentially eliminating the deferred gain entirely.

RISK ANALYSIS

Let me be honest about the risks, because every strategy has them. The number one risk with the S-Corp reasonable compensation strategy is an IRS reclassification. If the IRS determines your salary is unreasonably low, they will reclassify distributions as wages, assess back payroll taxes, and add penalties and interest. The defense is documentation. Have a compensation study or at least a well-reasoned memo in your files explaining why your salary level is appropriate. Use comparable data from Bureau of Labor Statistics, professional surveys, and industry benchmarks.

The second risk is audit exposure from aggressive depreciation. Cost segregation studies are well-established and legal, but they draw attention. If your Schedule E shows a large loss in year one on a newly acquired property, expect the possibility of an IRS inquiry. The defense here is simple: use a reputable engineering firm for the cost segregation study, keep all documentation, and make sure your tax preparer is experienced with these strategies. A cost segregation study from a qualified firm is itself strong audit defense because it demonstrates that the reclassifications were based on engineering analysis, not wishful thinking.

The third risk involves the Solo 401(k) and defined benefit plan contribution limits. Over-contributing to a Solo 401(k) triggers a six percent excise tax on excess contributions for each year they remain in the account. The contribution limits are based on your W-2 compensation from the S-Corp, so if you set your salary too low to maximize self-employment tax savings, you simultaneously limit your retirement contribution capacity. This is the fundamental tension in S-Corp tax planning. You want the salary low enough to save on payroll taxes but high enough to support maximum retirement contributions. Run the numbers both ways and find the optimal balance point.

The fourth risk is the QBI deduction phase-out trap. Many physicians assume they will get the twenty percent QBI deduction on their business income, only to discover at tax time that their total taxable income puts them above the phase-out threshold for specified service businesses. Do not build your financial plan around a deduction you may not receive. Know your numbers, and if you are in the phase-out range, explore whether income-shifting strategies such as timing deductions, maximizing retirement contributions, or charitable giving through a donor-advised fund can bring you below the threshold.

The fifth risk is structural complexity and compliance costs. Running an S-Corp requires a separate tax return, Form 1120-S, payroll processing, quarterly payroll tax filings, potentially state franchise taxes, and ongoing accounting. A defined benefit plan requires annual actuarial certification. Cost segregation studies have upfront costs. You will spend somewhere between eight thousand and twenty thousand dollars per year in additional accounting fees, depending on your complexity. The question is whether the tax savings justify the cost, and for a surgeon generating over one hundred fifty thousand in side income, they almost always do, typically by a factor of four to one or better.

The sixth and perhaps most underappreciated risk is legislative change. The QBI deduction is currently set to expire after 2025 unless Congress extends it. Bonus depreciation is phasing down. Tax brackets, standard deductions, and contribution limits change regularly. Any long-term tax strategy must be reviewed annually with a qualified CPA and adjusted for current law. Do not set it and forget it. Tax planning is an annual discipline, not a one-time event.

ACTION STEPS

Here is exactly what you should do, in order, starting this quarter. Step one: find a CPA who specializes in physician tax planning and has specific experience with S-Corps, cost segregation, and retirement plan design for high-income professionals. Do not use a generalist. Do not use a chain tax preparer. Ask for references from other surgeons or physician-specific financial communities. Your CPA should be saving you at least five to ten times their fee, and if they are not, you have the wrong CPA.

Step two: form your LLC and file Form 2553 to elect S-Corp status. If you already have consulting or medical-legal income flowing to you as a 1099 contractor, this should happen immediately. The S-Corp election can be filed up to two months and fifteen days into the current tax year to be effective for the current year, or you can file for the following year. Your CPA will handle the state-level filings, operating agreement, and EIN application. Total setup cost is typically one thousand to two thousand dollars.

Step three: set your reasonable compensation. Work with your CPA to determine the right salary level based on your actual business activities, time invested, and comparable market data. Open a business bank account, set up payroll through a service like Gusto or ADP, and begin running regular payroll with proper withholding.

Step four: establish your Solo 401(k) before December thirty-first of this year. You can make employee elective deferrals up until the tax filing deadline, but the plan itself must be established by year-end. If your business income supports it, discuss with your CPA whether a defined benefit plan makes sense. The actuarial setup for a defined benefit plan costs two thousand to three thousand dollars, with annual administration of one thousand five hundred to two thousand five hundred dollars, but the deduction capacity can be enormous.

Step five: if you own or plan to purchase rental real estate, get a cost segregation study on any property worth three hundred thousand dollars or more. The study will cost five thousand to fifteen thousand dollars depending on the property, but the first-year tax savings will typically be three to eight times the cost of the study. Make sure the firm you use employs engineers, not just accountants, and that they physically inspect the property or use a detailed methodology approved by the IRS.

Step six: build your annual tax planning calendar. In January, review the prior year's estimated tax payments and adjust for the current year. In March, make your first quarter estimated payment and review retirement plan contribution targets. In September, do a mid-year tax projection with your CPA to identify opportunities for year-end acceleration or deferral of income and deductions. In November, make any final equipment purchases, charitable contributions, or retirement plan contributions before December thirty-first.

Step seven: track everything. Keep a mileage log for your vehicle. Keep time records for real estate activities. Keep receipts for business expenses. Keep minutes of any meetings of your LLC. The strategies I have described are all perfectly legal and well-supported by the tax code, but they require documentation. The difference between a well-documented tax position and an indefensible one is not the strategy itself. It is the paper trail.

The bottom line is this. As a high-income surgeon with business income and investment income, you have access to a set of tax tools that can reduce your effective tax rate by five to eight percentage points. On income of one point three to one point five million dollars, that translates to seventy-five thousand to one hundred twenty thousand dollars per year in tax savings. Compounded over seven years, that is five hundred thousand to nearly a million dollars in additional retained wealth. The cost of implementing these strategies, including CPA fees, entity formation, retirement plan administration, and cost segregation studies, is typically fifteen to twenty-five thousand dollars per year. The return on that investment is four to one at minimum and often closer to eight to one. This is not optional optimization. This is the foundation of capital deployment. Every dollar you fail to shelter from unnecessary taxation is a dollar that cannot compound in your portfolio, cannot fund your surgical center, and cannot secure your family's financial independence. Get the structure right first, and everything else you build on top of it will grow faster.
