CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me tell you the single most important truth about owning a service business as a practicing surgeon. The general manager hire is the entire bet. Not the trucks. Not the territory. Not the brand. The GM. Everything else is downstream of whether you find the right person to run the operation while you are in the OR twelve hours a day. I cannot overstate this. If you get the GM wrong, you will lose money, you will lose sleep, and you will eventually sell the business at a loss or shut it down. If you get the GM right, you have a cash-flowing asset that compounds wealth while you compound surgical volume. So we are going to spend this entire lesson on how to find this person, how to structure their compensation so their incentives match yours, how to manage them without micromanaging, and how to protect yourself if things go sideways.

Here is the mental model. You are not hiring an employee. You are hiring a co-pilot who will fly the plane while you are in another cockpit entirely. If you have to be on the phone with your GM three times a day, you do not have a GM. You have an expensive assistant. The entire value proposition of absentee ownership depends on finding someone with genuine operational autonomy. That means you are looking for a specific profile, a specific compensation architecture, and a specific management cadence. Let us walk through each one.

The profile you are looking for is not a resume. It is a pattern. You want someone with at least five years managing a service business. Not retail, not corporate, not tech. A service business where trucks roll in the morning, technicians show up at customer homes, and the phone rings all day with complaints and requests. This person needs to have managed a profit and loss statement, not just supervised people. There is a massive difference between a shift supervisor at a franchise and someone who was accountable for revenue, cost of goods sold, labor as a percentage of revenue, and net margin. You want the second person. They need to be comfortable hiring and firing in a blue-collar environment. This is not a white-collar office where you put someone on a performance improvement plan and involve HR. In a home services business, you sometimes need to let someone go on a Tuesday afternoon because they no-showed a customer for the third time, and then you need to have a replacement technician on the schedule by Thursday. Your GM needs to be the kind of person who can have a direct, firm conversation with a twenty-four-year-old technician who just cursed at a homeowner, and then turn around and charm an angry customer on the phone five minutes later.

The last piece of the profile is tech savviness, specifically with platforms like ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or whatever field service management software your business runs on. ServiceTitan is the gold standard for most home services verticals, and it is a complex platform. Your GM needs to be able to pull reports, manage the dispatch board, track marketing source attribution, monitor average ticket, and use the system to hold technicians accountable to conversion rates. If your GM candidate cannot navigate ServiceTitan or is resistant to learning it, that is a disqualifier. The data layer is how you manage the business from a distance. If your GM is not fluent in the data, you are flying blind.

MECHANICS

Let us talk about compensation, because this is where most physician owners make critical errors. They either underpay and get a B-player who costs them far more in lost revenue and operational mistakes, or they overpay with a structure that does not align the GM's incentives with the owner's financial goals. Here is the architecture that works.

Base salary should be in the range of eighty-five thousand to one hundred ten thousand dollars per year, depending on your market and the size of the operation. In a mid-size metro, ninety to ninety-five thousand is the sweet spot for a business doing one and a half to three million in revenue. In a larger metro with a higher cost of living, you may need to go to one hundred five or one hundred ten thousand. Do not try to save fifteen thousand dollars on base salary. The difference between a ninety-thousand-dollar GM and a seventy-five-thousand-dollar GM is not fifteen thousand dollars. It is the difference between a business that runs at twelve percent EBITDA and a business that runs at four percent EBITDA. On a two-million-dollar revenue business, that is one hundred sixty thousand dollars a year in lost profit. You cannot afford to be cheap here.

On top of the base, you layer a performance bonus tied to EBITDA. The range is ten to fifteen percent of EBITDA, paid quarterly. Here is how to structure it. Define EBITDA clearly in the offer letter: revenue minus cost of goods sold, minus labor including the GM's own base salary, minus operating expenses, minus marketing spend. Do not let EBITDA be a fuzzy number. Write the formula down. Then set a threshold. The GM earns zero bonus on the first, say, one hundred fifty thousand dollars of annual EBITDA. That is your baseline return as the owner. Everything above that threshold, the GM earns ten to fifteen percent. So if the business does two million in revenue and generates three hundred thousand in EBITDA, the GM's bonus pool is ten to fifteen percent of one hundred fifty thousand, which is fifteen to twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars per year, or roughly four to six thousand per quarter.

This structure aligns incentives precisely. The GM does not get rewarded for revenue growth alone, which would incentivize reckless spending on marketing or hiring. The GM gets rewarded for profitable revenue growth. If they grow revenue by five hundred thousand but EBITDA stays flat because they overspent on technicians or trucks, they get nothing extra. If they hold revenue steady but tighten operations and EBITDA jumps by fifty thousand, they see that in their bonus. You want your GM thinking like an owner, and this bonus structure makes them think like an owner.

The third layer is phantom equity. This is not real equity. You are not giving away ownership of your LLC. Phantom equity is a contractual right to receive a cash payment equal to a percentage of the business's increase in value at a future liquidity event or at predetermined dates. You offer five to ten percent phantom equity vesting over four years. Typically, you do a one-year cliff where nothing vests, and then monthly or quarterly vesting over the remaining three years. So at the end of year one, twenty-five percent of their phantom equity vests. By the end of year four, they are fully vested.

Here is why phantom equity matters. Without it, your GM has no reason to build long-term value. They will optimize for quarterly bonuses and their own comfort. With phantom equity, they are incentivized to build systems, train a team that can operate without them, increase the multiple the business would command if sold, and stay for four years. If a GM walks after eighteen months, you lose enormous institutional knowledge and momentum. The phantom equity golden handcuffs keep them in the seat.

Let me put real numbers on this. Say you buy a home services business for five hundred thousand dollars. After four years with a good GM, the business is worth one point two million based on improved EBITDA and systems. The increase in value is seven hundred thousand dollars. If the GM has ten percent phantom equity fully vested, they are entitled to seventy thousand dollars upon a qualifying event. That is a meaningful payout for a GM earning ninety-five thousand base, and it cost you nothing out of pocket. It came from the value they helped create. You can also structure the phantom equity agreement to pay out on a schedule even without a sale, say every five years, based on an independent valuation. This gives the GM a predictable wealth-building opportunity that keeps them engaged.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through how a real GM hire plays out from start to finish so you can see the moving pieces.

You close on a plumbing business doing one point eight million in revenue with three hundred thousand in EBITDA. The previous owner was the de facto GM, so there is no existing management layer. You need to hire one immediately. You post the position on Indeed, LinkedIn, and in industry-specific groups. Your job title is General Manager, Residential Plumbing. The posting emphasizes P&L accountability, team leadership in a trades environment, ServiceTitan experience, and the opportunity to run a business with full autonomy. You offer ninety-five thousand base, twelve percent EBITDA bonus above a one hundred fifty thousand threshold, and seven percent phantom equity vesting over four years with a one-year cliff.

You receive eighty applications. Seventy are unqualified: retail managers, people with no service industry experience, or people who managed teams but never owned a P&L. You narrow it to ten and do phone screens. Five cannot articulate what EBITDA means or how they would improve it. You are down to five. You bring them in for in-person interviews. You ask each one the same questions. Tell me about a time you had to fire someone and how you handled it. Walk me through how you would diagnose a declining average ticket in a plumbing business. If I told you marketing was generating leads but revenue was flat, what would you investigate first? How do you hold technicians accountable without creating a toxic culture? What does your ideal Monday morning look like as a GM?

You find your person. She is a thirty-eight-year-old former branch manager for a regional HVAC company. She managed fourteen technicians, handled a two point three million P&L, grew revenue eighteen percent in two years, and left because the owner would not give her equity or a path to ownership. She knows ServiceTitan inside and out. She has fired people and she has developed people. She speaks fluent blue-collar. She is not fazed by a clogged sewer line or an irate customer. She negotiates you to ninety-eight thousand base and you agree because she is clearly the right person.

Now comes onboarding. The first ninety days are critical. You spend the first two weeks side by side, not because you know plumbing operations, but because you need to establish the relationship and align on priorities. You set up a shared dashboard that tracks the five numbers that matter: weekly revenue, average ticket, conversion rate, labor as a percentage of revenue, and customer satisfaction scores. You tell her you want a fifteen-minute call every Monday morning for the first six months, then biweekly, then monthly. You are not checking up on her. You are creating a rhythm that prevents small problems from becoming large ones.

In month two, she identifies that two of the eight technicians are significantly underperforming. One has a conversion rate of thirty-one percent versus a team average of fifty-eight percent. The other has three customer complaints in six weeks. She has direct conversations with both. One improves. One does not. She terminates the underperformer in month three and hires a replacement who is producing at the team average within six weeks. Revenue ticks up. Average ticket improves because the low performer was dragging down the average by offering unnecessary discounts to avoid conflict with customers.

By month six, she has renegotiated your supply house pricing, saving you four percent on parts, which drops straight to the bottom line. She has implemented a ride-along training program for new technicians. She has started tracking marketing ROI by source in ServiceTitan and killed two underperforming lead channels, reallocating that twelve thousand per month to the channels generating the lowest cost per lead. EBITDA is now trending toward three hundred sixty thousand annually, up from three hundred thousand at acquisition. She is earning her bonus, and you are earning more than you would have without her.

RISK ANALYSIS

Now let us talk about what goes wrong, because things go wrong and you need to be prepared.

Risk number one is the GM who manages up instead of managing the business. This person tells you what you want to hear on your Monday calls. Revenue is growing. The team is great. Customers are happy. Meanwhile, accounts receivable is ballooning because they are not following up on collections. Technician turnover is rising because they avoid hard conversations. Marketing spend is climbing with no corresponding revenue increase. You catch this by never relying solely on what the GM tells you. You have your own login to ServiceTitan. You have your bookkeeper or fractional CFO send you a monthly financial package directly, not through the GM. You trust but verify, and you verify through data, not conversation.

Risk number two is the GM who builds a fiefdom. They hire their friends. They create loyalty to themselves rather than the business. They become indispensable on purpose by keeping key relationships in their head rather than in the CRM. If they leave, the business craters. You prevent this by requiring documented standard operating procedures for every key process within the first six months. Hiring, training, dispatch protocols, customer complaint resolution, vendor management. These SOPs belong to the business, not the GM. You also make sure you personally have relationships with your top customers and key vendors. The GM should run the business day to day, but you should not be a stranger.

Risk number three is compensation misalignment over time. Your GM is crushing it. The business is now doing three million in revenue with five hundred thousand in EBITDA. Their bonus is getting large. Forty-two thousand a year on top of their base. They now want more. They want real equity, not phantom equity. They want a higher base. They feel underpaid relative to the value they are creating. This is a real and legitimate tension. You handle it by having the conversation proactively, not reactively. Before they come to you frustrated, you come to them in year two and say, I see the numbers. You are doing an extraordinary job. Let us restructure your phantom equity to reflect the current value of the business and extend the vesting schedule. You might increase phantom equity from seven to twelve percent and restart the four-year vesting clock from the new, higher base value. This gives them a fresh incentive without giving away actual ownership.

Risk number four is the GM who is great at operations but terrible at growth. The trains run on time. Customers are happy. Technicians show up. But the business is not growing. Revenue is flat year over year. This GM is a maintainer, not a builder. You either need to accept that and provide the growth strategy yourself through marketing investment and territory expansion, or you need to supplement the GM with a part-time sales or marketing person who reports to the GM but is specifically tasked with growth. Not every great operator is a great growth leader. Know which one you have and staff accordingly.

Risk number five is the GM who steals from you. It happens in home services more than people like to talk about. Technicians run side jobs using company materials. GMs approve inflated vendor invoices from a company owned by their cousin. Cash payments disappear. You protect yourself with controls. All bank accounts require your signature for transactions above a defined threshold. Your bookkeeper is independent of the GM and reports to you. You do quarterly audits of vendor invoices against market pricing. You use GPS tracking on all vehicles. You implement a policy that all customer payments go through the POS system and you monitor voided transactions. These controls are not about distrust. They are about creating an environment where the temptation does not exist because the opportunity does not exist.

ACTION STEPS

Here is your concrete action plan for the GM hire.

Step one. Before you even begin recruiting, write the job description yourself. Do not copy something off the internet. Sit down and list the ten most important things this person will do in their first year. Manage eight to twelve technicians. Own a P&L of one point five to three million. Achieve an EBITDA margin of fifteen percent or higher. Reduce technician turnover below thirty percent annually. Implement or optimize ServiceTitan. Build SOPs for all core processes. Manage all vendor relationships. Handle all HR functions including hiring, firing, and performance reviews. Report weekly KPIs to the owner. Develop and execute a marketing plan within a defined budget. That is your job description. Those are the outcomes you are paying for.

Step two. Set your compensation package before you start interviewing. Know your numbers. Base salary of ninety to one hundred five thousand depending on your market. EBITDA bonus of ten to fifteen percent above your threshold. Phantom equity of five to ten percent vesting over four years. Total compensation in year one will be ninety to one hundred five thousand. Total compensation in year four, if they perform, should be one hundred forty to one hundred seventy thousand including bonus and any phantom equity payouts. Write the phantom equity agreement with a lawyer who understands this structure. Do not use a template from the internet for this document.

Step three. Source candidates through three channels simultaneously. Post on Indeed and LinkedIn with a salary range to attract serious candidates. Post in industry-specific forums and trade association job boards where experienced managers look for their next opportunity. And tell your vendors, your supply house rep, your equipment dealer, anyone who interacts with multiple service businesses in your market. They know who the good operators are. Offer a two-thousand-dollar referral bonus to anyone who refers the person you hire.

Step four. Check references aggressively. Call three professional references and then ask each reference for one additional name. Call those people too. Ask every reference the same question: would you hire this person to run your business if you could not be there every day? The answer needs to be an enthusiastic yes, not a qualified yes.

Step five. Structure the first ninety days as a probationary period with clear milestones. By day thirty, the GM should have met every employee, every major customer, and every key vendor. They should have a working understanding of the ServiceTitan setup and be pulling their own reports. By day sixty, they should have identified the top three operational improvements and begun implementing at least one. By day ninety, they should be running the business without your daily input, and you should be down to your weekly fifteen-minute Monday call. If they have not hit these milestones, you need to have a direct conversation about whether this is the right fit. Do not let a bad hire linger past ninety days. The cost of a bad GM in a service business is roughly ten to fifteen thousand dollars per month in lost productivity, missed revenue, and cultural damage. Ninety days of that is thirty to forty-five thousand dollars. Six months of it is devastating.

Step six. Set up your verification systems from day one. Get your own logins to every platform. ServiceTitan, QuickBooks, your CRM, your marketing dashboards. Hire an independent bookkeeper who sends you a monthly financial package: profit and loss, balance sheet, accounts receivable aging, and a cash flow summary. This should cost you five hundred to eight hundred dollars per month and it is the single best investment you can make in protecting your business. The GM should know this system exists. It is not adversarial. It is professional. Any GM who resists financial transparency is a GM you should not have.

Step seven. Plan for succession from day one. Your GM should be developing at least one person on the team who could step into an interim management role if the GM gets sick or leaves. If your GM has been in the role for two years and there is no one who could hold things together for two weeks without them, that is a failure of leadership development, and you need to address it.

The GM hire is the most consequential decision you will make as an absentee owner. Get it right and you have a business that builds wealth while you build surgical careers. Get it wrong and you have an expensive hobby that drains your time and capital. Take the hiring process seriously. Structure compensation to align incentives. Set up systems to verify performance. And manage the relationship with the same precision you bring to operative planning. The ROI on getting this right is measured not just in dollars but in the freedom to remain focused on what you do best while your capital works in the background.
