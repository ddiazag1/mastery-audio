Reading a Private Placement Memorandum

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me walk you through one of the most important skills you will ever develop as a passive investor in real estate syndications. It is also one of the most neglected. I am talking about reading a Private Placement Memorandum, or PPM. This is the legal document that governs every single dollar you put into a syndication deal. It is typically eighty to a hundred and fifty pages long. It is written by securities attorneys. And most limited partners, including physicians who are otherwise meticulous about every detail in their operating rooms, never read it. They skim the executive summary, look at the projected returns, and wire the money. That is a mistake that can cost you tens or even hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Here is the good news. You do not need to read every word. You do not need a law degree. What you need is a systematic approach to finding the six sections that actually determine whether this deal is structured fairly for you or whether you are handing your capital to a sponsor who has engineered every advantage in their favor. Those six sections are the fee structure, the preferred return and profit waterfall, the capital call provisions, the limited partner voting rights, the key person clause, and the exit provisions. Master these six sections and you will know more about evaluating a PPM than ninety-five percent of passive investors in the market today.

Before we dive in, let me frame why this matters for you as a high-income surgeon. You are what the SEC calls an accredited investor, meaning your income exceeds two hundred thousand individually or three hundred thousand jointly. That accreditation gives you access to private offerings unavailable to the general public. But here is the irony. The SEC actually provides you with less protection, not more, because accredited investors are presumed sophisticated enough to evaluate deals on their own. The PPM is the document where that evaluation happens. If you cannot read it, your accreditation is a liability, not an asset.

Brian Burke, in his book The Hands-Off Investor, calls the PPM the constitution of the deal. Everything the sponsor has told you on a webinar, everything on the glossy pitch deck, everything in the proforma spreadsheet, none of it is legally binding. The PPM is. If there is a conflict between what the sponsor said on a call and what the PPM says, the PPM wins every time. So let us learn how to read it.

MECHANICS

Let me walk you through each of the six critical sections and teach you exactly what to look for, what numbers are reasonable, and what should trigger concern.

Section one is the fee structure. This is where the sponsor discloses every fee they will charge against your investment. There are typically four categories. The first is the acquisition fee, sometimes called an origination fee. This is a one-time fee the sponsor charges for finding and closing the deal. It is usually expressed as a percentage of the purchase price. A reasonable acquisition fee is one to two percent. On a twenty million dollar apartment complex, that means the sponsor collects two hundred to four hundred thousand dollars at closing, before any value has been created, before a single unit has been renovated, before a single tenant has been placed. If you see an acquisition fee above two percent, that is a yellow flag. Above three percent is a red flag.

The second fee is the asset management fee. This is an ongoing annual fee for overseeing the property and the business plan. It is typically one and a half to two percent of either the effective gross income or the invested equity, and the distinction matters enormously. Two percent of invested equity on a five million dollar raise is a hundred thousand dollars per year. Two percent of effective gross income on a property generating three million in revenue is sixty thousand per year. Same percentage, very different dollar amounts. Ask which basis the sponsor uses and do the math yourself.

The third fee is the disposition fee, charged when the property is sold. This typically runs one percent of the sale price. On a thirty million dollar exit, that is three hundred thousand dollars the sponsor takes off the top before profits are distributed to investors. Some sponsors do not charge a disposition fee, and that is a positive signal. Others charge it in addition to a promote on the back end, which means they are getting paid twice at exit.

The fourth fee category is the construction management fee or renovation oversight fee, particularly common in value-add deals. This is typically five to ten percent of the renovation budget. On a three million dollar renovation, that is a hundred fifty to three hundred thousand dollars. The concern here is misaligned incentives. The more the sponsor spends on renovations, the more they earn in construction management fees, even if those renovations do not produce proportional returns.

Add all the fees together and stress test them. I want you to take out a calculator when you read a PPM and total every fee the sponsor will collect over the projected hold period. Then divide that by the total equity raised. I have seen deals where the all-in fee load exceeds twenty percent of the invested equity over a five-year hold. That means the deal needs to generate over twenty percent in total returns just for you to break even. That is a massive headwind.

Section two is the preferred return and the profit waterfall. This is arguably the most important section of the entire PPM. The preferred return, often called the pref, is the minimum annual return you are entitled to receive before the sponsor participates in any profits. A standard preferred return in the current market is seven to eight percent annually. Some deals offer a six percent pref, which is on the low side. Anything below six percent should give you pause.

Now, here is a critical distinction. Is the preferred return cumulative or non-cumulative? A cumulative preferred return means that if the deal does not generate enough cash flow to pay you your seven percent in year one, that unpaid amount accrues and must be paid to you before the sponsor receives any promote. A non-cumulative pref means that if they miss a year, that shortfall disappears. It does not carry forward. You always want cumulative. Always.

The waterfall describes how profits above the preferred return are split between the limited partners and the general partner. A common structure looks like this. First, limited partners receive their preferred return of seven to eight percent. Second, there is a catch-up tranche where the general partner receives a disproportionate share until they have caught up to a specified percentage of total distributions. Third, above a certain IRR threshold, say twelve percent, the split changes. Below twelve percent IRR, the split might be seventy-thirty in favor of the LPs. Above twelve percent, it shifts to sixty-forty. Above eighteen percent, it might shift to fifty-fifty.

This tiered structure is the promote or carried interest, and it is how sponsors make their real money. I have seen deals where the GP starts collecting promote at a six percent IRR. That is unreasonably low. Look for deals where the promote does not kick in until the LPs have achieved at least an eight percent IRR, preferably ten or twelve.

Section three is capital call provisions. A capital call is the sponsor's right to require additional investment from existing limited partners after the initial closing. Imagine you invest a hundred thousand dollars into a syndication. Two years later, the sponsor issues a capital call for an additional twenty percent. You now owe another twenty thousand dollars, and you may not have the liquidity to fund it.

What you want to know is whether capital calls are permitted at all, what the maximum amount is as a percentage of your original investment, and what happens if you cannot fund one. In many PPMs, failure to fund triggers draconian penalties. Your ownership might be diluted, you might forfeit your preferred return, or your entire interest can be converted to a subordinate position.

The best deals either prohibit capital calls entirely or cap them at ten to fifteen percent of committed capital with reasonable consequences for non-participation. If a PPM allows unlimited capital calls with forfeiture provisions, think very carefully before investing.

Section four is limited partner voting rights. As an LP, you are a passive investor by definition. You have no control over day-to-day operations. But you should have a voice on major decisions. Specifically, look for LP approval requirements on the following actions. The sale or refinance of the property. Any related-party transactions where the sponsor is on both sides of a deal. Changes to the fee structure. Amendment of the PPM itself. Extension of the hold period beyond the originally projected timeline. And removal of the general partner for cause.

The voting threshold matters too. Some PPMs require a simple majority, fifty-one percent of LP interests, to approve major actions. Others require a supermajority of sixty-six or seventy-five percent. And some PPMs give the GP sole discretion on virtually everything, with LP votes being advisory and non-binding. That last structure is the worst case for you. If the GP has unilateral authority to sell the property, refinance it, extend the hold, and modify fees without your approval, you have essentially no protections.

Section five is the key person clause. This provision addresses what happens if the lead sponsor, the human being whose track record you evaluated and whose judgment you are trusting, is no longer involved in the deal. Maybe they become incapacitated. Maybe they have a dispute with their business partners. Maybe they simply lose interest and delegate to junior staff.

A strong key person clause requires that a specific named individual remain actively involved. If that person can no longer fulfill their role, the clause should trigger protections, most commonly a pause on fees or a vote of the limited partners to either approve a replacement manager or begin liquidation.

A weak key person clause either does not exist, which is a significant red flag, or is written so broadly that virtually anyone within the sponsor's organization can step in. You are investing in a specific operator's expertise. The key person clause ensures that operator stays in the picture.

Section six is the exit provisions. This section governs how and when you get your money back. The projected hold period is typically three to seven years for a value-add apartment syndication. But the projected hold period is not binding. The PPM usually gives the sponsor broad discretion to extend the hold if market conditions are unfavorable or if the business plan has not been fully executed.

What you want to find is whether there is a hard deadline by which the sponsor must liquidate the investment. Is there a provision for LP vote on extension beyond the projected hold. Are there any liquidity options during the hold period, such as the ability to sell your LP interest to another accredited investor. And what are the tax implications of exit, particularly whether the sponsor intends to pursue a 1031 exchange at sale, which could delay your ability to receive proceeds.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through a real-world scenario so you can see how these six sections interact. You are evaluating a two hundred unit apartment complex in Dallas, Texas. Purchase price is twenty-eight million dollars. The sponsor is raising eight million in LP equity. You are considering a hundred thousand dollar investment. The projected hold period is five years with a target IRR of sixteen percent and an equity multiple of one point nine.

You open the PPM and go straight to the fee section. The acquisition fee is two percent of the purchase price. That is five hundred sixty thousand dollars. The asset management fee is two percent of effective gross income, projected at about three point two million in year one. That is sixty-four thousand per year, or three hundred twenty thousand over five years. The construction management fee is eight percent of a four million dollar renovation budget. That is three hundred twenty thousand. The disposition fee is one percent of the projected sale price of thirty-eight million. That is three hundred eighty thousand. Total fees over the life of the deal come to roughly one point five eight million dollars. The total equity raised is eight million dollars. The all-in fee load is approximately nineteen point seven percent of invested equity. That is high. Not disqualifying, but high.

Now you look at the waterfall. The preferred return is seven percent, cumulative and compounding. Good. Below a ten percent IRR, the split is eighty-twenty in favor of the LPs. Between ten and fifteen percent IRR, it shifts to seventy-thirty. Above fifteen percent, it becomes sixty-forty. The promote structure is reasonable. The GP is incentivized to deliver strong returns because their compensation accelerates as performance improves.

Capital calls are permitted up to fifteen percent of committed capital, with a sixty-day notice period. Failure to fund results in dilution of your interest proportional to the unfunded amount, but does not trigger forfeiture of your existing position. That is acceptable.

LP voting rights require sixty-six percent approval for sale of the property, refinance above eighty percent loan-to-value, related party transactions, and removal of the GP for cause. The GP has discretion over day-to-day operations, property management selection, and routine refinancing. This is a reasonable split of authority.

The key person clause names the lead sponsor and requires their active involvement. If they are unable to serve, fees are suspended and a vote of sixty percent of LP interests is required within ninety days to either approve a successor or begin liquidation. That is strong protection.

Exit provisions state a target hold of five years with the ability to extend up to two additional years at the GP's discretion. Beyond seven years, extension requires LP approval. There is no secondary market for LP interests, but the PPM does not prohibit private transfers with GP consent.

Looking at this deal holistically, the fee load is on the higher end, but the waterfall is LP-friendly, the capital call provisions are reasonable, voting rights are protective, the key person clause has teeth, and the exit timeline has a hard cap. This is a deal worth serious consideration. The fees are the primary negotiating point. You might ask the sponsor whether the acquisition fee can be reduced to one point five percent or whether the disposition fee can be waived if the deal achieves above a certain IRR threshold. Good sponsors are willing to have these conversations.

RISK ANALYSIS

Let me highlight the five most dangerous provisions I have encountered in PPMs, each of which I have seen in real deals offered to physician investors.

Risk number one is the hidden promote. Some sponsors structure the waterfall so that the GP receives a promote not just on profits above the preferred return, but on all distributed capital including return of principal. If the waterfall language says the GP receives thirty percent of all distributions after the preferred return rather than thirty percent of profits after return of capital and the preferred return, you are looking at a hidden promote. The difference can be tens of thousands of dollars on a hundred thousand dollar investment.

Risk number two is the fee-on-fee structure. I have seen PPMs where the asset management fee is calculated on gross asset value, which includes the debt. On a twenty-eight million dollar property with twenty million in debt and eight million in equity, a two percent fee on gross asset value is five hundred sixty thousand per year versus a two percent fee on equity of one hundred sixty thousand. Same percentage, three and a half times the dollar amount.

Risk number three is the unlimited extension provision. Some PPMs give the GP the right to extend the hold indefinitely at their sole discretion. I have seen deals from 2016 and 2017 that were supposed to be five-year holds and are still active in 2026 because the GP keeps extending, often because selling would crystallize losses. Your PPM should have a hard stop, and LP approval should be required for any extension beyond it.

Risk number four is the cross-collateralization clause. In some PPMs, the sponsor can use your investment in Deal A as collateral or credit support for Deal B. This means you have exposure to deals you never evaluated and never agreed to invest in. This clause should not exist in any PPM you sign.

Risk number five is the indemnification provision. Every PPM has a section where limited partners agree to hold the general partner harmless for certain actions. Reasonable indemnification covers good-faith business decisions. Unreasonable indemnification covers gross negligence, willful misconduct, or fraud. If the indemnification immunizes the GP from liability for gross negligence, you are giving the sponsor a license to be reckless with your money. The indemnification should always carve out fraud, willful misconduct, and gross negligence.

Let me add one more layer of risk analysis specific to your situation as a surgeon. Liquidity risk is magnified for you. Your income is high but it comes from active work. You cannot simply work more hours to generate cash if you need liquidity. If you have three hundred thousand dollars spread across three syndications and all three extend their hold periods by two years, that capital is inaccessible. Make sure your syndication allocation never exceeds capital you can truly afford to lock up for seven to ten years, regardless of what the projected hold period says.

ACTION STEPS

Here is your concrete action plan for the next deal you evaluate.

Step one. Buy and read The Hands-Off Investor by Brian Burke. It is the single best resource for passive real estate investors and covers PPM analysis in far greater detail than we can in this session.

Step two. Create a PPM checklist document with six sections matching the six areas we discussed today. For every deal, fill out this checklist before making an investment decision. Write down the actual numbers. What is the acquisition fee percentage and dollar amount. What is the total fee load as a percentage of equity raised. What is the preferred return. Is it cumulative. What are the waterfall tiers. What triggers a capital call. What are your voting rights. Who is the key person. What is the hard stop on hold period. This exercise forces you to actually find and read these sections rather than relying on the sponsor's summary.

Step three. Compare at least three deals side by side before investing in any one of them. When you have three PPMs open on your desk, the differences become obvious. One sponsor charges a two percent acquisition fee while another charges one percent. One has a six percent non-cumulative pref while another offers eight percent cumulative. Comparison is the fastest way to develop pattern recognition and identify outliers.

Step four. Have a securities attorney review your first two or three PPMs with you. This costs five hundred to fifteen hundred dollars per review, but it is an education expense, not just a legal expense. After two or three reviews, you will be able to read subsequent PPMs on your own with confidence.

Step five. When something in the PPM concerns you, ask the sponsor about it directly and in writing. Email them. Say, I noticed the disposition fee is one point five percent and there is also a promote on exit, can you walk me through how those interact and what the total GP compensation looks like at your projected exit. How they respond tells you as much as the answer itself. A sponsor who is transparent and willing to explain their fee structure is worth investing with. A sponsor who gets defensive or evasive is telling you everything you need to know.

Step six. Track your actual returns against the projections in every PPM you invest through. After your first few deals reach exit, you will have real data on how projections compare to outcomes. You will learn which sponsors are conservative in their underwriting and which are aggressive, how fees actually impact net returns, and you will become a genuinely sophisticated investor, not just an accredited one.

The PPM is not a formality. It is not boilerplate. It is the legal contract that governs your investment, and its provisions will determine your returns just as much as the real estate market does. Six sections. Fee structure. Preferred return and waterfall. Capital calls. Voting rights. Key person. Exit provisions. Find them, read them, understand them, compare them. That is how you protect your capital and deploy it wisely.
