SBA Acquisition Deal Math

Today we are going to walk through the single most powerful wealth-building lever available to a high-income physician who wants to move beyond stocks, real estate, and retirement accounts. We are talking about acquiring an existing, cash-flowing business using an SBA 7(a) loan, and we are going to do it with real numbers so you can see exactly how the math works, where the risks hide, and why this strategy can turn less than a hundred thousand dollars of your capital into a multi-million-dollar asset within a decade. This is not theory. This is deal math. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to pick up a Confidential Information Memorandum from a business broker, run the numbers yourself, and know within fifteen minutes whether a deal is worth pursuing.

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let us start with the core idea. The SBA 7(a) loan program was created by the Small Business Administration to help Americans buy and grow small businesses. The government does not lend you the money directly. Instead, it guarantees a portion of the loan that a participating bank makes to you. That guarantee, typically seventy-five percent of the loan amount, dramatically reduces the bank's risk. Because the bank's risk is lower, they are willing to lend you up to ninety percent of the purchase price of a business. That means you only need to bring ten percent equity to the table. Compare that to commercial real estate, where you typically need twenty-five percent down, or to buying a business without SBA backing, where a seller or bank might demand thirty to fifty percent equity. The SBA program is the highest-leverage acquisition tool legally available to you in the United States.

Now, why does this matter for a surgeon earning five hundred thousand, seven hundred thousand, or over a million dollars a year? Because your clinical income is high but it is linear. You trade hours for dollars. Every dollar you earn requires your hands, your presence, your license. A business you own and do not operate generates income whether you are in the OR or on vacation. And when you buy that business with ninety percent leverage, you are using a small amount of your capital to control a much larger asset and capture all of the upside. That is the fundamental concept. Now let us talk about exactly how it works.

MECHANICS

The SBA 7(a) loan has specific parameters you need to understand before you model any deal. First, the maximum loan amount is five million dollars. For most small business acquisitions in the one to five million dollar range, this ceiling is not a constraint. Second, the interest rate is variable, typically pegged to the Wall Street Journal Prime Rate plus a spread of one and a half to two and three-quarters percent. As of mid-2026, with prime sitting around eight and a half percent, you should model your all-in rate between ten and eleven percent. Third, the term for a business acquisition is ten years. Not seven, not fifteen. Ten. That is important because it determines your annual debt service, which is the single most important number in your deal model. Fourth, the SBA requires a minimum equity injection of ten percent from the buyer. This must be cash or cash-equivalent. You cannot borrow your down payment from another source and call it equity. The SBA will trace the funds. Fifth, you will personally guarantee the loan. There is no way around this. Your house, your brokerage accounts, your future earnings are all on the line. That personal guarantee is the price of admission for ninety percent leverage.

Now let us talk about how businesses are valued, because you cannot do deal math without understanding what you are paying for. Small businesses in the one to three million dollar revenue range are almost always valued as a multiple of something called Seller's Discretionary Earnings, or SDE, for owner-operated businesses, or EBITDA, which stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization, for businesses with professional management in place. EBITDA is the number you will use most often because as a surgeon, you are not going to operate the business day to day. You are going to keep the existing manager or hire one. The typical EBITDA multiple for a small business ranges from two and a half times to five times, depending on the industry, the size, the growth trajectory, customer concentration, and how clean the financials are. A boring, stable, well-run business with recurring revenue, like an HVAC company with maintenance contracts, might trade at three to four times EBITDA. A more volatile or owner-dependent business might only justify two to two and a half times.

The other critical metric is the Debt Service Coverage Ratio, or DSCR. This is simply the business's cash flow divided by the annual loan payments. Lenders want to see a DSCR of at least one and a quarter, meaning the business generates twenty-five percent more cash than needed to cover the debt. A DSCR of one and a half or higher makes a deal comfortable. Below one and a quarter, and the bank will not approve the loan, nor should you want them to.

One more mechanical detail. The SBA charges a guarantee fee, which is a one-time cost rolled into the loan. For loans over one million dollars, the fee is around three and a half percent of the guaranteed portion. On a loan of eight hundred fifty thousand, that adds roughly twenty-two thousand to your total borrowing. Factor it in. It matters.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Now let us run a real deal from start to finish. I want you to picture a residential and light commercial HVAC company in a mid-sized city. It has been in operation for eighteen years. The owner is sixty-two and wants to retire. He has a team of eight technicians, an office manager, and a dispatcher. The company does one and a half million dollars in annual revenue. After paying all employees including the owner's salary replacement of one hundred twenty thousand for a general manager, after rent, trucks, insurance, parts, marketing, and every other operating expense, the business generates two hundred seventy thousand dollars in EBITDA.

Step one, valuation. HVAC companies with a stable book of maintenance contracts, a trained workforce, and recurring revenue from service agreements typically sell at three to three and a half times EBITDA. Let us use three and a half times. Three and a half times two hundred seventy thousand gives us a purchase price of nine hundred forty-five thousand dollars.

Step two, financing structure. You apply for an SBA 7(a) loan. The bank will finance ninety percent of the purchase price. Ninety percent of nine hundred forty-five thousand is eight hundred fifty thousand five hundred dollars. Let us round to eight hundred fifty thousand for simplicity. Your equity injection is ten percent, which is ninety-four thousand five hundred dollars. That is the cash you bring to closing. You will also have transaction costs including legal fees, due diligence, the SBA guarantee fee, and working capital reserves. Budget another forty to fifty thousand for those, bringing your total out-of-pocket to roughly one hundred forty to one hundred forty-five thousand. For this model, we will focus on the equity injection of ninety-four thousand five hundred as your invested capital for return calculations.

Step three, debt service. You have an eight hundred fifty thousand dollar loan at ten and a half percent interest over ten years. Your annual debt service, principal plus interest, comes to approximately one hundred twenty thousand dollars per year, or ten thousand per month. In the early years, the interest portion is heavy. Roughly seventy-four thousand of that first year's payment is interest, with forty-six thousand going to principal. By year five, the split shifts. By year eight, you are paying down principal fast.

Step four, cash flow analysis. The business produces two hundred seventy thousand in EBITDA. Subtract one hundred twenty thousand in annual debt service. That leaves you with one hundred fifty thousand dollars in pre-tax free cash flow in year one. This is money that hits your account after every employee is paid, every bill is covered, every truck is fueled, and every loan payment is made. One hundred fifty thousand dollars. From a ninety-four thousand five hundred dollar investment.

Step five, return metrics. Your cash-on-cash return in year one is one hundred fifty thousand divided by ninety-four thousand five hundred, which equals one hundred fifty-nine percent. Let me say that again. One hundred fifty-nine percent cash-on-cash return in year one. Compare that to the stock market's historical average of ten percent per year, or a rental property's typical cash-on-cash return of eight to twelve percent. This is not even close. The leverage is doing the heavy lifting.

Step six, the seven-year projection. Assume the business grows EBITDA at a modest five percent per year. That is roughly in line with inflation plus a small amount of real growth, nothing aggressive. By year seven, EBITDA has grown from two hundred seventy thousand to three hundred eighty thousand. Your debt service stays fixed at one hundred twenty thousand, so your free cash flow grows from one hundred fifty thousand to two hundred sixty thousand. Over those seven years, you have collected cumulative free cash flow of approximately one point four million dollars. Meanwhile, the business itself is now worth three and a half times three hundred eighty thousand, which is one point three three million dollars. And you have paid down roughly four hundred thousand of the loan principal, leaving a balance of about four hundred fifty thousand. Your equity in the business is the value minus the remaining debt: one point three three million minus four hundred fifty thousand equals eight hundred eighty thousand. Add back the one point four million in cumulative cash flow you have already pocketed, and your total economic value from a ninety-four thousand five hundred dollar investment is approximately two point two eight million dollars. That is a twenty-three to twenty-four times multiple on your invested capital over seven years. Even after taxes, even after accounting for some capital expenditures along the way, you are looking at a fifteen to eighteen times return. Nothing in medicine, real estate, or the stock market comes close to this kind of leveraged return on a cash-flowing asset.

Let us pause and examine why the math is so powerful. Three forces are compounding simultaneously. First, you are collecting free cash flow every single year, and that cash flow grows as the business grows. Second, the loan is amortizing, meaning the business's own cash flow is paying down debt and building your equity for you. Third, the enterprise value of the business itself is appreciating as earnings grow. These three engines running in parallel on a ninety percent leveraged structure create exponential wealth accumulation. This is the same mathematics that made real estate fortunes, except the cash yields on small businesses are typically two to three times higher than rental properties.

RISK ANALYSIS

Now, I would be doing you a disservice if I made this sound like free money. It is not. There are real risks, and you need to understand every one of them before you write a check.

Risk number one is revenue concentration. If the HVAC company gets forty percent of its revenue from one property management company, and that company switches providers, you lose almost half your income overnight. EBITDA drops from two hundred seventy thousand to one hundred sixty-two thousand. Your debt service is still one hundred twenty thousand. Your free cash flow just collapsed to forty-two thousand and your DSCR is now one point three five, barely above the danger zone. Before you buy any business, demand a customer concentration report. No single customer should represent more than ten to fifteen percent of revenue. If they do, you need a lower purchase multiple or a larger equity cushion.

Risk number two is key employee departure. In an HVAC business, the senior technicians are the product. If two of your best techs leave and take their customer relationships with them, your revenue drops and your service quality suffers. Mitigation strategies include employment agreements with non-solicitation clauses, retention bonuses funded from deal proceeds, and building a training pipeline so no single employee is irreplaceable.

Risk number three is the economic cycle. HVAC has a significant new-installation component that is sensitive to housing starts and commercial construction. If a recession hits and new construction dries up, that revenue line can drop twenty to thirty percent. The service and maintenance side is more resilient because people still need their furnaces fixed in January. When you model the deal, stress-test it with a twenty percent revenue decline and see if the business still covers debt service. In our example, a twenty percent revenue drop takes EBITDA from two hundred seventy thousand to roughly one hundred eighty thousand, assuming some cost variability. Debt service is still one hundred twenty thousand. DSCR falls to one and a half. Tight, but survivable. If the stress test puts DSCR below one and a quarter, the deal is too thin.

Risk number four is your personal guarantee. If the business fails and cannot cover the debt, the SBA and the bank will come after your personal assets. Your surgical income, your home equity, your savings. This is not a theoretical risk. It is a contractual certainty. You need to think carefully about asset protection before you sign. Consider holding the business in an LLC or corporation, maintaining adequate insurance, and consulting with an asset protection attorney about what is shielded in your state. In some states, your primary residence is protected by homestead exemptions. In others, it is not.

Risk number five is management risk. You are a surgeon, not an HVAC manager. You are buying this business to be an owner, not an operator. That means you are entirely dependent on the quality of your general manager. A bad manager can destroy a good business in twelve months. Mitigate this by keeping the seller involved for a transition period of six to twelve months, by installing clear financial controls and reporting, and by having a backup plan if your manager quits or underperforms. Some physician-owners join a peer group like an Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition community or hire an operating partner with industry experience and give them a small equity stake to align incentives.

Risk number six is overpaying. If you pay four and a half times EBITDA instead of three and a half times, the purchase price jumps from nine hundred forty-five thousand to one point two one five million. Your loan becomes one point zero nine million, your annual debt service rises to one hundred fifty-four thousand, and your year-one free cash flow drops from one hundred fifty thousand to one hundred sixteen thousand. Your DSCR drops from two point two five to one point seven five. Still workable, but you have dramatically reduced your margin of safety and your returns. Overpaying by one turn of EBITDA cost you thirty-four thousand a year in free cash flow and significantly increased your downside risk. Valuation discipline is everything. Walk away from any deal where the seller's price expectations exceed fair market multiples by more than ten percent.

There is one more risk unique to physicians. Your medical license and clinical income are your ultimate backstop. If the business fails and you owe five hundred thousand on a personal guarantee, you can earn your way out of it in two to three years of surgical practice. That is actually a significant advantage over non-physician buyers. But it only works if you protect your license. Do not let business stress affect your clinical performance, do not cut corners on malpractice insurance to fund a deal, and do not take on so much business debt that a failure would force you into personal bankruptcy and put your license at risk through state medical board scrutiny.

ACTION STEPS

Let us close with exactly what to do next if this deal math excites you.

Step one. Build your buyer profile. You need a personal financial statement, your last three tax returns, a resume or CV, and a brief letter explaining why you want to buy a business. SBA lenders will ask for all of this. Have it ready in a folder before you start looking at deals.

Step two. Get pre-qualified with an SBA lender. Not a regular bank, an SBA Preferred Lender. Preferred Lenders can approve and close SBA loans in-house without sending the file to the SBA for review, which saves weeks. The best-known SBA lenders for acquisition deals include Live Oak Bank, Harvest Small Business Finance, and Celtic Bank. Call them, explain you are a physician looking to acquire a small business, and ask for a pre-qualification letter. This letter tells brokers and sellers you are a serious, funded buyer.

Step three. Identify your target industry. You want a business that is essential, not discretionary. HVAC, plumbing, electrical, pest control, waste management, veterinary clinics, dental practices, home health agencies, physical therapy clinics. These businesses provide services people need regardless of the economy. You also want a business with recurring revenue, whether that is maintenance contracts, subscription services, or repeat customers. Recurring revenue makes cash flow predictable and lowers your risk.

Step four. Source deals. Register on BizBuySell, BizQuest, and BusinessesForSale.com. These are the multiple listing services for small businesses. Also reach out directly to business brokers in your target geography. Tell them your criteria: industry, revenue range, EBITDA range, and geography. A good broker will bring you deals before they hit the public marketplaces. Another excellent source is the search fund and Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition community. Websites like searchfunder.com and the Stanford GSB search fund primer are goldmines of deal flow and education.

Step five. Learn to read a quality of earnings report. When you find a deal you like, you will hire an accounting firm to do a quality of earnings analysis, which is essentially a deep audit of the seller's financials. They will verify that the EBITDA the seller claims is real, that there are no hidden liabilities, and that the revenue is sustainable. This report typically costs fifteen to twenty-five thousand dollars and it is the single most important piece of due diligence you will do. Do not skip it. Ever. The quality of earnings report is to a business acquisition what an MRI is to a surgical case. You would not operate without imaging, and you should not acquire without a quality of earnings.

Step six. Model three scenarios. For every deal, build a simple spreadsheet with three columns: base case, downside case, and upside case. In the base case, assume EBITDA stays flat. In the downside case, assume a fifteen to twenty percent decline. In the upside case, assume five to ten percent annual growth. Calculate free cash flow, DSCR, and cash-on-cash return for each scenario. If the downside case still covers debt service with a DSCR above one and a quarter, the deal is structurally sound. If the downside case puts you below one, walk away.

Step seven. Assemble your team. You need four professionals: an SBA lender, a business acquisition attorney, a CPA with experience in small business transactions, and optionally a business broker representing your interests as the buyer. These four professionals will cost you twenty to forty thousand dollars in aggregate for a typical deal, but they will prevent mistakes that cost ten times that.

Step eight, and this is critical for a surgeon with limited time, set a cadence. Commit to reviewing two to three deals per week. Each review should take thirty to forty-five minutes. You pull the listing, check the revenue and EBITDA, run the basic deal math we covered today, and decide if it warrants a deeper look. Most deals will not pass your initial screen. That is fine. You are looking for one great deal, not ten mediocre ones. At a pace of two to three per week, you will review over a hundred deals in a year. Out of a hundred, you might request financials on ten, do deep diligence on three, make offers on two, and close one. That one deal, structured correctly, can generate more wealth over the next decade than five years of surgical income saved and invested in index funds.

The SBA acquisition model is not a shortcut. It requires education, diligence, professional guidance, and a clear-eyed understanding of the risks. But for a high-income physician with the capital for a ten percent equity injection, the creditworthiness to secure an SBA guarantee, and the discipline to own a business without trying to operate it, the deal math is extraordinary. A ninety-four thousand five hundred dollar investment that generates one hundred fifty thousand in year-one free cash flow and compounds into a two-point-two-million-dollar position in seven years is not a fantasy. It is arithmetic. And arithmetic does not lie.
