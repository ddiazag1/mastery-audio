CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me paint a picture for you. It is Wednesday morning. You are between cases. You have maybe ninety seconds before the next patient is wheeled in. You pull out your phone, open one screen, and in less than a minute you know exactly how your HVAC company, your plumbing franchise, your med spa, or whatever service business you own is performing. You know if revenue is up or down. You know if your techs are busy or idle. You know if cash is tight or flush. You know if customers are happy or angry. And then you put your phone away and you go back to being a surgeon. That is what a KPI dashboard does. It compresses the entire operational reality of a business into a single screen that you can read in five minutes or less, and it is the single most important tool that separates a physician who owns a business from a physician who runs a business. Because you do not want to run the business. You want to own it, oversee it, and intervene only when the numbers tell you something is wrong.

KPI stands for key performance indicator, and the word "key" is doing all the heavy lifting in that phrase. The entire point is radical selectivity. A service business generates hundreds of data points every week. Calls answered, quotes given, jobs scheduled, parts ordered, invoices sent, payments received, complaints filed, reviews posted, trucks dispatched, overtime hours logged. If you tried to track all of that, you would need a full-time analyst and you would still drown in noise. The dashboard approach says no, we are going to pick eight to ten metrics that function as vital signs for the business, and we are going to display them in a format so simple that a busy surgeon can glance at it on a phone screen and immediately know whether the business is healthy, sick, or somewhere in between. Think of it exactly like rounding on a patient. You do not re-read the entire chart every morning. You look at the vitals, you look at the labs, you check the drain output, and you make a decision. Same principle. Different patient.

The reason this matters for physician investors specifically is that your time is your most constrained resource. You are not going to spend twenty hours a week managing a service business. You should not be spending twenty hours a week. If you are, you have bought yourself a job, not an investment. The target is five hours per week of total oversight, and the dashboard is what makes that possible. It replaces lengthy phone calls with your general manager. It replaces surprise cash crunches. It replaces that sinking feeling you get when you realize nobody has been watching the numbers for three weeks. The dashboard watches the numbers every single day, and it alerts you when something needs your attention.

MECHANICS

Let me walk you through the eight metrics that belong on every service business dashboard, why each one matters, and what the red flags look like. These are not theoretical. These are the numbers that operators who run ten-million-dollar service companies actually look at every week.

Metric number one is revenue this week compared to the same week last year. Not compared to last week. Not compared to last month. Compared to the same week last year. This is critical because service businesses are seasonal. An HVAC company in Omaha is going to do three times the revenue in July that it does in March. If you compare July to June, you think you are a genius. If you compare July to March, you panic. Neither reaction is useful. But if you compare this week in July to the same week in July last year, now you have a real signal. You want to see growth of at least ten to fifteen percent year over year. If you are flat, the business is actually shrinking because your costs are going up with inflation. If you are down, something is wrong and you need to find out what. Maybe your marketing stopped working. Maybe you lost a key tech. Maybe a competitor opened up nearby. The year-over-year comparison strips out seasonality and gives you the truth. So if your business did forty-two thousand in the third week of July last year and it is doing forty-seven thousand this week, that is a twelve percent increase and you are in good shape. If it is doing thirty-eight thousand, you are picking up the phone and calling your general manager.

Metric number two is booked jobs versus completed jobs. This is a two-part number and the ratio between them tells you an enormous amount. Booked jobs tells you about demand. Are customers calling? Is your marketing working? Is the phone ringing? Completed jobs tells you about capacity. Can your team actually execute on the demand? The ideal ratio depends on lead times in your industry, but in general you want completed jobs to be running at eighty-five to ninety-five percent of booked jobs on a rolling weekly basis. If booked jobs are high but completed jobs are low, you have a capacity problem. You need more techs, more trucks, or better scheduling. If booked jobs are dropping but completed jobs are steady, your backlog is shrinking and you are about to have idle capacity. That is a demand problem, and you need to look at your marketing spend. A healthy service business with, say, a hundred and twenty booked jobs and a hundred and ten completed jobs in a week is running at about ninety-two percent execution rate. That is solid. If you see booked jobs at a hundred and forty but completed jobs stuck at ninety, you are leaving fifty thousand dollars or more on the table every week and you are making customers wait, which means you are losing them.

Metric number three is average ticket size. This is your total revenue divided by the number of completed jobs. It tells you whether you are doing high-value work or low-value work, and more importantly, whether that mix is changing. If your average ticket was four hundred and twenty dollars last quarter and it has dropped to three hundred and sixty dollars this quarter, something shifted. Maybe your techs stopped upselling maintenance agreements. Maybe you are getting more small repair calls and fewer installations. Maybe you dropped pricing to compete on a bid. Whatever the reason, a declining average ticket is a profitability killer because your fixed costs per job, the truck roll, the dispatch, the drive time, stay roughly the same whether the ticket is three hundred or five hundred dollars. You want to see average ticket size stable or growing. For a residential HVAC company, a healthy range is three hundred and fifty to six hundred dollars per job. For a commercial plumbing company, it might be eight hundred to fifteen hundred. For a med spa, it might be two hundred and fifty to four hundred per visit. The exact number matters less than the trend.

Metric number four is technician utilization rate. This is the percentage of available working hours that your technicians spend on billable work. If a tech works a forty-hour week and spends thirty-two hours on actual jobs, that is eighty percent utilization. The other eight hours went to driving between jobs, waiting for parts, doing paperwork, sitting idle, or attending meetings. Industry benchmark for a well-run service company is seventy-five to eighty-five percent utilization. Below seventy percent, you are paying people to not produce revenue. Above ninety percent, you are burning people out and you have no slack for emergencies or callbacks. This metric is also your early warning system for staffing decisions. If utilization is running above eighty-five percent for three consecutive weeks, you need to hire. If it drops below sixty-five percent, you have too many techs for your current demand and you are bleeding payroll. In a company with ten technicians averaging thirty-five dollars per hour fully loaded, every five-point drop in utilization costs you about seven hundred dollars per tech per week. That is seven thousand dollars walking out the door.

Metric number five is service agreement count. Service agreements, sometimes called maintenance contracts or membership plans, are the recurring revenue engine of a service business. A residential HVAC company might sell an annual maintenance agreement for one hundred and ninety-nine dollars that includes two tune-ups and a discount on repairs. A plumbing company might offer a monthly membership for twenty-nine dollars that includes annual inspections and priority scheduling. These agreements matter for three reasons. First, they create predictable recurring revenue that smooths out seasonality. Second, they generate high-margin work because tune-ups and inspections are fast and cheap to perform. Third, they create customer loyalty and reduce churn. You want to see this number growing every single month. If you have fifteen hundred active service agreements and that number has been flat for six months, your sales team is not selling them or your renewal rate is dropping. A mature service business should have twenty to thirty percent of its revenue coming from service agreements. If you are below fifteen percent, there is a major growth lever sitting right there waiting to be pulled.

Metric number six is cash balance and accounts receivable aging. This is the financial vital sign. You need to know two things every week. How much cash is in the operating account right now, and how much money is owed to you and how old are those receivables. Cash balance is straightforward. You need to maintain a minimum operating reserve, typically two to three months of fixed expenses. If your fixed monthly costs are one hundred and twenty thousand dollars, you want to see at least two hundred and forty thousand in the bank at all times. If you see that number dipping below your threshold, something is wrong with collections, spending, or both. AR aging is broken into buckets. Current, which is zero to thirty days. Thirty to sixty days. Sixty to ninety days. And over ninety days. In a healthy service business, eighty-five percent or more of your receivables should be in the current bucket. If you see your over-ninety-day bucket growing, you have a collections problem. Every dollar in that bucket has about a forty percent chance of becoming a write-off. If you have fifty thousand sitting in the over-ninety-day bucket, you should mentally write off twenty thousand of it and start making aggressive collection calls on the rest.

Metric number seven is customer satisfaction, which in practice means your online review score and your review velocity. For a service business, your Google review score is probably the single most important marketing asset you have. You need to know your current rating, your review count, and how many new reviews you got this week. A company with a four-point-eight rating and six hundred reviews is in excellent shape. A company with a four-point-two rating and eighty reviews is vulnerable. You want to see at least three to five new reviews per week for a company doing a hundred or more jobs per week. If your review velocity drops, it usually means your techs stopped asking for reviews or your automated follow-up system broke. If your rating drops, it means you have a service quality problem and you need to read the negative reviews carefully to find the pattern.

Metric number eight is employee headcount. Simple but important. How many people are on your payroll this week compared to last week and compared to the same time last year. Service businesses live and die by their people, and unexpected turnover is the number one operational risk. If you had twenty-two employees last week and you have twenty this week, you need to know why two people left and whether replacements are already in the pipeline. If your headcount has been slowly declining from twenty-five to twenty over six months and nobody told you, you have a management problem. Your general manager should be flagging turnover before it shows up on the dashboard, but the dashboard is your safety net.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me show you how this actually works in practice with a real scenario. You are a neurosurgeon. You acquired a residential plumbing company eighteen months ago for one point two million dollars. The business does about three point five million in annual revenue with a fifteen percent EBITDA margin, throwing off roughly five hundred and twenty-five thousand in cash flow before debt service. You have a general manager named Mike running it day to day. You have fifteen employees including eight plumbers, three apprentices, two dispatchers, a bookkeeper, and Mike. You set up a KPI dashboard using a tool like ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or even a simple Google Sheet that your bookkeeper updates every Monday morning by nine AM.

It is Monday morning. You are in pre-op. You open the dashboard on your phone. Here is what you see.

Revenue this week, fifty-eight thousand four hundred. Same week last year, fifty-one thousand two hundred. That is a fourteen percent increase. Green light.

Booked jobs, eighty-seven. Completed jobs, seventy-nine. That is a ninety-one percent execution rate. Green light.

Average ticket size, seven hundred and thirty-nine dollars. Last quarter average was seven hundred and fifteen. Trending up slightly. Green light.

Tech utilization, eighty-eight percent. That is above the eighty-five percent threshold for three weeks running. Yellow light. You need to start thinking about hiring another plumber. You text Mike, "Utilization has been above eighty-five for three weeks. Start recruiting. Let's talk Thursday about the candidate pipeline." That text took you twelve seconds.

Service agreement count, four hundred and twelve. Last month it was four hundred and five. Growing, but slowly. You make a mental note to discuss a promotion or incentive for service agreement sales at your next monthly call with Mike.

Cash balance, two hundred and eighty-one thousand. Your fixed monthly costs are ninety-five thousand, so you have roughly three months of reserve. Green light. AR aging, eleven thousand in the over-ninety bucket. That is up from six thousand last month. Yellow light. You text Mike, "AR over ninety is at eleven K. Who owes us? Let's get on the phone with them this week." Another twelve seconds.

Google reviews, four-point-seven rating, three hundred and forty-two reviews, four new reviews this week. Green light.

Employee headcount, fifteen. Same as last week, same as last month. Green light.

Total time spent, four minutes. You identified two issues that need attention, sent two texts, and you are done for the week. On Thursday you will have a fifteen-minute call with Mike about the recruiting pipeline and the AR aging. On the last Friday of the month, you will have a sixty-minute call to review the monthly financials and discuss strategy. Your total time investment in this business is about four to five hours per week, and you are completely on top of it.

Now let me show you what a bad week looks like. Same dashboard, different numbers.

Revenue this week, thirty-nine thousand two hundred. Same week last year, fifty-one thousand two hundred. That is a twenty-three percent decline. Red light.

Booked jobs, fifty-four. Completed jobs, forty-eight. Execution rate is fine at eighty-nine percent, but the absolute volume is way down. The problem is not capacity. The problem is demand.

Average ticket size, eight hundred and seventeen dollars. Actually up. The jobs you are doing are good jobs. You just are not getting enough of them.

Tech utilization, sixty-one percent. Red light. Your plumbers are sitting around.

Service agreement count, three hundred and ninety-eight. Down from four hundred and twelve. You are losing members and not replacing them.

Cash balance, one hundred and forty-two thousand. That is only one and a half months of fixed costs. Yellow light trending toward red.

AR aging, twenty-eight thousand in the over-ninety bucket. Red light.

Google reviews, four-point-three rating, three hundred and forty-five reviews, one new review this week. And the last three reviews were negative, all mentioning long wait times.

Employee headcount, thirteen. Down from fifteen. Two plumbers left.

This dashboard just screamed at you. Everything is connected. Two plumbers left, so your capacity dropped. Wait times went up. Customers got frustrated and left bad reviews. Bad reviews hurt your search ranking. Fewer calls came in. Revenue dropped. Cash is getting tight. AR is piling up because the bookkeeper has been too busy covering dispatch to make collection calls.

You are not texting Mike this week. You are calling him today. This is a thirty-minute emergency conversation, not a twelve-second text. You need to understand why two plumbers left. You need a recruiting plan with urgency. You need the AR addressed immediately. And you need to look at whether your marketing spend needs to increase to rebuild call volume while you rebuild capacity. The dashboard did not solve the problem, but it made sure you saw the problem three weeks earlier than you would have without it. Three weeks earlier might be the difference between a temporary rough patch and a death spiral.

RISK ANALYSIS

The first risk with dashboards is measuring the wrong things. I see physician owners fall in love with vanity metrics. Total revenue sounds impressive but tells you nothing about profitability. Number of calls received sounds like demand but means nothing if those calls are not converting to booked jobs. Number of employees sounds like growth but could just mean bloat. Stick with the eight metrics I just described. They are battle-tested. Resist the temptation to add more. Every metric you add dilutes your attention on the ones that matter.

The second risk is stale data. A dashboard is only useful if it is updated on a rigid schedule. If your bookkeeper updates it on Monday one week, skips a week, then updates it on Thursday, you lose the ability to compare week over week and you lose the habit of checking. The update schedule must be non-negotiable. Monday morning by nine AM, every single week, no exceptions. If your bookkeeper is on vacation, the general manager does it. If the general manager is on vacation, someone else does it. The cadence is sacred.

The third risk is dashboard-only management. The dashboard tells you what is happening. It does not tell you why. If revenue drops twenty percent, the dashboard shows you the drop but it cannot tell you whether it is because your top tech quit, your biggest commercial client switched vendors, Google changed its algorithm and your search ranking tanked, or your competitor started offering free estimates. The dashboard is a trigger for a conversation, not a replacement for a conversation. When you see a red light, you pick up the phone.

The fourth risk is ignoring yellow lights. Red lights get attention because they are dramatic. Yellow lights get ignored because they feel manageable. But yellow lights are where you actually make money as an owner. Catching tech utilization at eighty-seven percent before it hits ninety-two percent. Catching AR aging at twelve thousand before it hits thirty thousand. Catching review velocity at two per week before it hits zero. The discipline is responding to yellow lights with small, early interventions instead of waiting for red lights that require emergency responses.

The fifth risk is not benchmarking. Your dashboard numbers in isolation tell you something, but your dashboard numbers compared to industry benchmarks tell you much more. If your tech utilization is seventy-two percent, is that good or bad? If you do not know that the industry benchmark is seventy-five to eighty-five percent, you might think seventy-two is fine. It is not. Join a peer group, hire a consultant, or at minimum subscribe to industry benchmarking reports so you know where your numbers should be. ServiceTitan, for example, publishes benchmarking data for their platform users. Use it.

The sixth risk is building the dashboard yourself. You are a surgeon. You do not have time to set up integrations between your field service software, your accounting system, your CRM, and a dashboard tool. Hire someone to build it. A good operations consultant or a fractional CFO can set up your entire dashboard in two to four weeks for three to five thousand dollars. That is one of the highest-return investments you will ever make. Trying to do it yourself will take you three months of sporadic weekend work and it will be fragile and ugly and you will stop using it.

ACTION STEPS

Step one. Choose your dashboard platform. If your service business already runs on ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or Jobber, start with their built-in reporting dashboards. They are not perfect, but they are good enough and they pull data automatically. If you need something more customized, use a tool like Databox, Klipfolio, or even Google Looker Studio connected to your data sources. The platform matters much less than the discipline of using it.

Step two. Define your eight metrics and set thresholds. Write down the exact eight numbers you want to see, the green-yellow-red thresholds for each one, and who is responsible for updating them. For revenue year over year, green is above ten percent growth, yellow is zero to ten percent, red is negative. For tech utilization, green is seventy-five to eighty-five percent, yellow is eighty-five to ninety or sixty-five to seventy-five, red is above ninety or below sixty-five. Do this for all eight metrics. Write it down. Share it with your general manager.

Step three. Assign the update responsibility and make it non-negotiable. Your bookkeeper or office manager updates the dashboard every Monday by nine AM. Put it in their job description. Make it a condition of employment, not a suggestion. If the dashboard is not updated by nine AM on Monday, your general manager needs to know about it by nine-fifteen and it needs to be fixed by noon. No exceptions. No excuses.

Step four. Build the review habit. Every Monday between nine and nine-thirty AM, you spend five minutes looking at your dashboard. Put it on your calendar as a recurring event. Protect it like you would protect an OR block. If everything is green, you are done in three minutes. If there are yellow or red lights, you send a text or schedule a call. This weekly cadence is the engine of absentee ownership.

Step five. Establish a monthly deep dive. On the last Friday of every month, you have a sixty-minute call with your general manager to review the monthly financials, discuss trends in the dashboard metrics, and talk strategy. This is where you look at trailing twelve-month trends, compare against annual targets, discuss capital expenditure needs, and make decisions about hiring, marketing spend, pricing changes, and service agreement promotions. This monthly call is your board meeting. Treat it like one.

Step six. Set up automated alerts. Most dashboard platforms allow you to set up email or text alerts when a metric crosses a threshold. Set them up. If your cash balance drops below your minimum reserve, you want a text immediately, not next Monday. If a one-star review comes in, you want to know today, not next week. Alerts do not replace the weekly dashboard review, but they catch emergencies between reviews.

Step seven. Benchmark quarterly. Every ninety days, compare your dashboard metrics against industry benchmarks. Are you above or below average on tech utilization? On average ticket size? On service agreement penetration? On review ratings? This quarterly benchmarking exercise keeps you honest and identifies areas where you are leaving money on the table. It takes about thirty minutes per quarter and it is some of the most valuable time you will spend on the business.

Step eight. Iterate the dashboard every six months. Every six months, look at your eight metrics and ask whether they are still the right eight. Maybe you have solved your AR problem and collections are humming, so you swap out AR aging for a new metric like lead conversion rate. Maybe you launched a new service line and you want to track its revenue separately. The dashboard should evolve as the business evolves, but change it deliberately and infrequently. Do not tinker with it every week.

The bottom line is this. A KPI dashboard is not a nice-to-have. It is the operating system for physician-owned businesses. It is the difference between flying blind and flying on instruments. It is the difference between hoping your general manager is doing a good job and knowing whether your general manager is doing a good job. It is the difference between a business that slowly bleeds out over eighteen months because nobody was watching the numbers and a business that catches problems early, adjusts fast, and compounds value year after year. Build the dashboard. Check it every Monday. Respond to yellow lights. That is how you run a business on five hours a week from your phone, and that is how you turn a single acquisition into a portfolio of cash-flowing assets that fund the life you actually want to live.
