Let's talk about reading financial statements. This is one of those foundational skills that separates physicians who invest intelligently from physicians who get taken for a ride. You already read complex diagnostic studies every day. You look at an MRI, you see a disc herniation compressing the thecal sac, and you integrate that with the clinical picture. Reading financial statements is the same kind of pattern recognition. You are looking at a business the way you look at a patient. The income statement is the history of present illness. The balance sheet is the physical exam. The cash flow statement is the labs. And just like in medicine, you cannot make a good decision from one data source alone. You need all three, and you need to know what each one is actually telling you.

Here is why this matters specifically for you as a high-income surgeon thinking about capital deployment. When you are evaluating a business to buy, whether that is a medical device distributorship, an ambulatory surgery center, a med spa, a laundromat chain, or a small manufacturing company, the seller is going to hand you financial statements. A broker is going to hand you a confidential information memorandum loaded with charts and graphs. Your ability to actually read what is on those pages, to understand what the numbers mean, and more importantly to spot what the numbers are hiding, is the difference between buying a cash-flowing asset and buying someone else's problem. I have seen physicians write checks for seven figures based on a broker's summary page. That is malpractice in the financial world. You would never operate based solely on the referring doctor's note without looking at the imaging yourself. Same principle applies here. Let's build your fluency.

We are going to work through the three core financial statements one at a time, and then I am going to show you how they connect to each other. We will use a real example throughout so the numbers stick.

Let's start with the income statement, also called the profit and loss statement or P and L. This document tells you how much money a business made and how much it spent over a specific period of time, usually a month, a quarter, or a year. Think of it as a movie, not a photograph. It captures activity over a window.

The P and L starts at the top with revenue. Revenue is the total amount the business billed or collected. For our working example, let's say you are looking at a regional home health company. Their annual revenue is three million two hundred thousand dollars. That is the top line. Every other number on the P and L flows down from here.

The next line is cost of goods sold, often abbreviated COGS. For a service business like home health, this is the direct cost of delivering the service. That means the wages and benefits of the nurses and aides who go into patients' homes. Let's say COGS is one million nine hundred twenty thousand dollars. When you subtract COGS from revenue, you get gross profit. In our case, that is three million two hundred thousand minus one million nine hundred twenty thousand, which equals one million two hundred eighty thousand dollars. Gross margin is gross profit expressed as a percentage of revenue. One million two hundred eighty thousand divided by three million two hundred thousand gives you forty percent. That forty percent gross margin tells you that for every dollar of revenue, forty cents is left over after paying the direct costs of delivering the service. In home health, a forty percent gross margin is solid. If you saw twenty percent, you would be concerned about labor cost management. If you saw sixty percent, you might wonder if they are underreporting labor costs or if something unusual is going on.

Below gross profit, you have selling, general, and administrative expenses, often called SGA. This is everything it costs to run the business that is not directly tied to delivering the service. Office rent, administrative salaries, marketing, insurance, accounting fees, software subscriptions, the owner's car lease, the owner's cell phone, the office Christmas party. In our example, SGA is seven hundred sixty thousand dollars. This is where you need to pay very close attention, because SGA is where owners hide personal expenses inside a business. We will come back to that.

When you subtract SGA from gross profit, you land on operating income, sometimes called EBIT, which stands for earnings before interest and taxes. One million two hundred eighty thousand minus seven hundred sixty thousand gives you five hundred twenty thousand dollars in operating income. This tells you how much the core business operations generated before you account for how the business is financed or what the tax situation looks like.

Now, you will hear the term EBITDA constantly. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA takes operating income and adds back depreciation and amortization because those are non-cash expenses. The business is not writing a check for depreciation. It is an accounting entry that spreads the cost of an asset over its useful life. If our home health company has sixty thousand dollars a year in depreciation on vehicles and equipment, then EBITDA is five hundred twenty thousand plus sixty thousand, which equals five hundred eighty thousand dollars. EBITDA is the number that brokers and sellers love to talk about, because it is the number that business valuations are typically based on. If the asking price is a multiple of EBITDA, a higher EBITDA means a higher price. Remember that. The seller is financially incentivized to make EBITDA look as large as possible.

Below operating income, you subtract interest expense on any business debt, then you subtract taxes, and you arrive at net income. Net income is the true bottom line, the profit that actually belongs to the owner. If our company has forty thousand in interest on a small business loan and roughly one hundred thousand in taxes, net income comes to three hundred eighty thousand dollars. Revenue of three point two million. Net income of three hundred eighty thousand. That is an eleven point nine percent net margin.

Now let's move to the balance sheet. If the income statement is a movie, the balance sheet is a photograph. It captures the financial position of the business at a single point in time. The balance sheet has three sections: assets, liabilities, and equity. The fundamental equation is assets equal liabilities plus equity. This always balances. Always.

Assets are what the business owns. They are divided into current assets, things that can be converted to cash within a year, and long-term assets. Current assets include cash in the bank, accounts receivable, which is money that customers owe the business, and inventory if applicable. For our home health company, let's say they have one hundred twenty thousand in cash, two hundred eighty thousand in accounts receivable, and forty thousand in prepaid expenses. Total current assets are four hundred forty thousand. Long-term assets include vehicles, equipment, and maybe some real estate, let's call it two hundred sixty thousand after depreciation. Total assets are seven hundred thousand.

Liabilities are what the business owes. Current liabilities are obligations due within a year. Accounts payable, accrued wages, the current portion of any loans. Let's say current liabilities are one hundred ninety thousand. Long-term liabilities are things like the remaining balance on a business loan, say one hundred sixty thousand. Total liabilities are three hundred fifty thousand.

Equity is the residual. Assets minus liabilities. Seven hundred thousand minus three hundred fifty thousand equals three hundred fifty thousand in owner's equity. This represents the owner's accumulated investment and retained earnings in the business.

Here is a critical number from the balance sheet: working capital. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Four hundred forty thousand minus one hundred ninety thousand equals two hundred fifty thousand. Working capital tells you whether the business can pay its short-term obligations. If working capital is negative, the business may struggle to make payroll or pay vendors without taking on more debt. When you buy a business, working capital is often a negotiation point. Typically, a normalized level of working capital conveys with the sale, and any excess or deficit is adjusted at closing. If you do not understand this, you could end up buying a business and immediately needing to inject cash just to keep the lights on.

The third statement is the cash flow statement, and honestly, this is the one most people skip, and it is the one that matters most. The cash flow statement tells you where cash actually came from and where it actually went. It is divided into three sections.

Operating cash flow is cash generated by the core business. It starts with net income and then adjusts for non-cash items like depreciation and changes in working capital. If accounts receivable grew by fifty thousand during the year, that means the business earned revenue it has not collected yet. That is a use of cash. The cash flow statement adjusts for this. Our company might show net income of three hundred eighty thousand, add back sixty thousand in depreciation, subtract fifty thousand for the increase in receivables, and arrive at operating cash flow of three hundred ninety thousand.

Investing cash flow covers purchases or sales of long-term assets. If the company bought two new vehicles for eighty thousand, investing cash flow is negative eighty thousand.

Financing cash flow covers debt and equity transactions. If the company made forty thousand in loan payments and the owner took two hundred thousand in distributions, financing cash flow is negative two hundred forty thousand.

Total cash flow for the year: three hundred ninety thousand minus eighty thousand minus two hundred forty thousand equals seventy thousand. The cash balance went up by seventy thousand. You can verify this against the balance sheet.

Now here is the critical distinction you need to burn into your brain. EBITDA is not free cash flow. Our company had EBITDA of five hundred eighty thousand. But free cash flow, which is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures for maintaining the business, is three hundred ninety thousand minus eighty thousand, which is three hundred ten thousand. That is a massive difference. Two hundred seventy thousand dollars of difference. If you valued this business at four times EBITDA, you would be paying two million three hundred twenty thousand. If you valued it at four times free cash flow, you would be paying one million two hundred forty thousand. Same business, wildly different valuations depending on which number you use. Sellers and brokers will always anchor to EBITDA. Your job is to think in terms of free cash flow, because free cash flow is the actual cash you can pull out of the business after keeping it running.

Let's walk through a deal now. You are presented with this home health company. The broker sends you a confidential information memorandum. The cover page says the asking price is two point three million dollars. The broker highlights adjusted EBITDA of six hundred ninety thousand dollars, representing a three point three times multiple. That sounds reasonable for a home health company. But you need to dig into what "adjusted" means.

You open the financial statements and the broker's addback schedule. Here is what the broker is adding back to EBITDA to get from five hundred eighty thousand to six hundred ninety thousand. First, the owner's wife is on payroll for forty thousand as an office manager, but the broker says she only works ten hours a week and a replacement would cost twenty thousand, so they add back twenty thousand. Second, the owner runs a personal vehicle through the business at eighteen thousand a year. Third, the owner has a country club membership billed to the company for twelve thousand. Fourth, there is a one-time legal fee of thirty thousand for a lawsuit that has been resolved. And fifth, the broker adds back thirty thousand for "above-market owner compensation," arguing that the owner pays himself two hundred thirty thousand but a replacement general manager would only cost two hundred thousand.

So the addback schedule looks like this. EBITDA of five hundred eighty thousand, plus twenty thousand for excess spousal compensation, plus eighteen thousand for the personal vehicle, plus twelve thousand for the country club, plus thirty thousand for the one-time legal fee, plus thirty thousand for above-market owner comp. That totals six hundred ninety thousand in adjusted EBITDA.

Here is how you evaluate these addbacks. The personal vehicle and the country club are legitimate addbacks. Those are clearly personal expenses running through the business and they will go away when you take over. That is thirty thousand dollars. The one-time legal fee is arguably legitimate if you can verify the lawsuit is truly resolved and there is no ongoing exposure. That is another thirty thousand. But the spousal compensation addback is questionable. Maybe the wife actually does meaningful work and you will need to replace her with a twenty thousand dollar part-time employee, not eliminate the cost entirely. And the "above-market owner compensation" addback is one you need to scrutinize hard. If the owner is actively managing the business, doing sales, managing staff, handling compliance, and you plan to hire a manager, that manager might cost two hundred thousand or more. The addback might be zero or even negative.

Let's say after your analysis, you accept sixty thousand in addbacks instead of one hundred ten thousand. Your adjusted EBITDA is six hundred forty thousand, not six hundred ninety thousand. At a three point three multiple, that changes the purchase price from two point three million to two point one million. You just saved yourself two hundred thousand dollars by reading the financials carefully.

But you are not done. You go to the cash flow statement. You see that accounts receivable have been growing aggressively. Last year they grew by fifty thousand, the year before by seventy thousand. You ask why. It turns out the company shifted toward accepting more Medicare patients, and Medicare reimbursement timelines are longer. This means the business needs more working capital to operate. You also notice that the fleet of vehicles is aging. Average vehicle age is six years. You estimate you will need to spend one hundred fifty thousand on vehicle replacements in the next two years. That capital expenditure is not reflected in EBITDA, but it absolutely affects your actual returns.

You recalculate. Adjusted EBITDA of six hundred forty thousand. Subtract maintenance capital expenditures of seventy-five thousand per year for vehicles. Subtract the incremental working capital needed for growing receivables, approximately fifty thousand per year. Your estimated free cash flow is five hundred fifteen thousand. At a three point three multiple on free cash flow, you would offer one million seven hundred thousand, not two point three million. That is a six hundred thousand dollar difference, all because you could actually read the financial statements.

Now let's talk about what goes wrong and the red flags you need to watch for. The first major risk is revenue concentration. Go back to the income statement and ask what percentage of revenue comes from the top three clients or referral sources. If one physician group refers sixty percent of the patients, and that group gets acquired or changes their referral patterns, you just lost sixty percent of your revenue overnight. Always ask for a revenue breakout by customer or referral source.

The second risk is accounts receivable quality. A large receivables balance is not necessarily good. Old receivables, meaning invoices outstanding for ninety or one hundred twenty days or more, may never be collected. Ask for an accounts receivable aging schedule. If twenty percent of receivables are over ninety days old, you should haircut the value of those receivables in your valuation and question management's collection practices.

The third risk is trending. Never look at one year of financials. Look at three years minimum. Is revenue growing, flat, or declining? Are margins improving or compressing? Is SGA growing faster than revenue? A single year can look beautiful while the trend tells a completely different story. If revenue dropped eight percent last year and the broker is showing you adjusted EBITDA based on the best year out of three, that is a giant red flag.

The fourth risk is off-balance-sheet liabilities. The balance sheet shows you what is on the books, but it does not show you pending lawsuits, potential regulatory fines, unresolved tax audits, or deferred maintenance. This is why due diligence goes beyond the financial statements, but the statements are where you start asking questions. If you see legal fees spiking in SGA, ask why. If you see a large "other liabilities" line, ask what it includes.

The fifth risk is owner dependency. This is not on any financial statement, but the financials will give you clues. If the income statement shows zero spending on marketing because the owner brings in all the business through personal relationships, you have a problem. When that owner leaves, the referral pipeline may dry up. If the owner's compensation is low because they work eighty hours a week, your replacement cost is actually two managers, not one.

Here is what I want you to do this month. First, get a copy of Financial Intelligence by Karen Berman and Joe Knight. It is the single best book on financial statements for non-accountants. Read the first ten chapters. It will take you about four hours. Second, pull the financial statements for any business you currently have visibility into. If you own a share of a surgery center, request the annual financial statements. If you have access to your practice's financials, pull them. Read the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement using the framework we just discussed. Calculate gross margin, net margin, working capital, and compare EBITDA to operating cash flow. Third, go to the SEC's EDGAR database and pull the ten-K filing for a publicly traded healthcare company. UnitedHealth Group, HCA Healthcare, or Amedisys are good choices. Public company filings have extensive notes and disclosures that teach you how to interpret each line item. Read the financial statements section and the management discussion and analysis. Fourth, when you feel comfortable with the three statements, practice the addback analysis. Look at any business listing on BizBuySell or a similar marketplace. Pull the listing, review the seller's claimed EBITDA and addbacks, and ask yourself which addbacks you would accept and which you would challenge. The goal is to be able to look at a set of financial statements and within thirty minutes have a clear picture of how much cash the business actually generates, how healthy its balance sheet is, and where the risks are hiding. That skill alone will save you hundreds of thousands of dollars over your investing career and will make you the physician in the room who cannot be fooled by a polished broker presentation.
