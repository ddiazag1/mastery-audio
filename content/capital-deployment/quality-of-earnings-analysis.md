Quality of Earnings Analysis

CONCEPT OVERVIEW

Let me ask you a direct question. If a colleague told you they were about to spend eight hundred thousand dollars on a medical practice, and their entire basis for the purchase price was the seller's own financial statements, what would you say? You would probably say that is insane. And you would be right. Yet this is exactly what hundreds of physician-buyers do every single year. They look at the seller's tax returns, maybe glance at a profit and loss statement, see a number that looks reasonable, and write a check. This is the financial equivalent of operating on a patient based solely on their self-reported symptoms without ever ordering imaging. You would never do that in the operating room. Do not do it in a business transaction either.

This is where a Quality of Earnings analysis comes in. A Quality of Earnings analysis, which you will hear referred to as a QofE throughout this lesson, is the single most important piece of financial diligence you will perform before acquiring any business. It is not a standard audit. A standard audit tells you whether the financial statements conform to generally accepted accounting principles. That is a compliance exercise. A QofE is fundamentally different. A QofE is a forensic investigation into the sustainability and repeatability of a business's actual cash flow. It answers one question that matters more than any other: Is the money this business claims to make actually real, and will it continue to show up after you take over?

Think of it this way. The seller's financial statements are like a patient's chief complaint. The QofE is your MRI with contrast. It reveals the underlying anatomy. It shows you what is real tissue and what is artifact. It shows you where the pathology actually lives versus where the seller tells you it lives. And just like you would never skip the MRI before a complex spine case, you should never skip the QofE before writing a seven-figure check.

The core concept behind a QofE is adjusted EBITDA. EBITDA stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It is the standard metric used to value most small and mid-sized businesses. When a seller or a broker tells you a business is worth four times EBITDA or five times EBITDA, they are talking about a multiple applied to this number. The problem is that the EBITDA they are quoting you is almost always the reported EBITDA, which is the number straight off their financial statements. Reported EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA can be dramatically different numbers. A QofE firm's job is to figure out what the true adjusted EBITDA actually is, stripping away all of the noise, one-time events, owner perks, and accounting tricks that inflate or obscure the real earning power of the business.

Here is why this matters in dollar terms. If a seller tells you the business generates five hundred thousand dollars in EBITDA, and you agree to pay a four-times multiple, you are writing a check for two million dollars. But if the QofE reveals that the true adjusted EBITDA is actually three hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars, that same four-times multiple means the business is worth one and a half million dollars. You just saved yourself five hundred thousand dollars. Or more accurately, you avoided overpaying by five hundred thousand dollars. That is the power of a QofE. It is not a cost. It is a pricing tool. It is the single best investment you will make in any acquisition.

A QofE analysis typically costs between five thousand and ten thousand dollars for a business in the range most physicians would be acquiring, meaning businesses with revenues between one million and ten million dollars. For larger or more complex transactions, the cost can run to twenty or thirty thousand dollars. Regardless of the price, this is non-negotiable. You do not skip the QofE. Period. If a seller tells you they do not want to cooperate with a QofE, that is not a yellow flag. That is a red flag with flashing lights and a siren. Walk away. A legitimate seller with a real business and honest financials will welcome the scrutiny because it validates their asking price.

MECHANICS

Now let me walk you through how a QofE actually works, step by step. Understanding the mechanics will help you interpret the report when your QofE firm delivers it, and it will help you ask better questions during negotiations.

The first thing a QofE firm does is collect the raw financial data. They are going to ask the seller for at least three years of tax returns, three years of detailed profit and loss statements, three years of balance sheets, bank statements, accounts receivable aging reports, accounts payable aging reports, payroll records, and any contracts with major customers or vendors. They want everything. And they want the source documents, not summaries prepared by the seller's accountant. This distinction matters because summaries can obscure details that the source documents reveal.

Once they have the data, the QofE team begins the normalization process. Normalization is the act of adjusting reported financial results to reflect what the business would look like under a new owner operating it in a standard, arm's-length manner. There are several categories of normalization adjustments, and each one matters.

The first category is owner compensation adjustments. In most small businesses, the owner pays themselves in ways that do not reflect market-rate compensation. Sometimes the owner underpays themselves and takes distributions instead, which makes the business look more profitable than it really is. Other times the owner overpays themselves or runs significant personal expenses through the business, which makes the business look less profitable. The QofE firm will determine a market-rate salary for the owner's role and adjust EBITDA accordingly. For example, if the owner of a medical staffing company pays themselves four hundred thousand dollars per year but the market rate for a CEO of that size company is two hundred and fifty thousand, the QofE firm will add back one hundred and fifty thousand dollars to EBITDA. Conversely, if the owner pays themselves nothing and takes all the cash as distributions, the firm will subtract market-rate compensation from EBITDA.

The second category is one-time or non-recurring items. These are expenses or revenues that happened once and will not repeat. Examples include lawsuit settlements, insurance payouts, one-time consulting projects, moving expenses, major equipment repairs that are not expected to recur, and pandemic-related government grants like PPP loans. The QofE firm strips all of these out because they do not represent the ongoing earning power of the business. A business that received two hundred thousand dollars in Employee Retention Tax Credits in 2021 does not get to count that as recurring revenue. That money is gone and it is not coming back.

The third category is related-party transactions. This is where things get interesting and sometimes ugly. Many small business owners run personal expenses through the business. Country club memberships. Car payments. Family vacations disguised as business travel. Salary payments to family members who do not actually work at the company. Rent payments to a real estate LLC the owner also controls, often at above-market rates. The QofE firm identifies every single one of these and adjusts EBITDA to reflect what the business would look like without them. If the seller is paying their spouse eighty thousand dollars a year for a bookkeeping role that could be filled by a twenty-five-dollar-per-hour part-time employee, the QofE adds back roughly fifty-five thousand dollars.

The fourth category is revenue quality analysis. This is arguably the most important part of the entire QofE. The firm is not just looking at how much revenue the business generates. They are looking at the nature of that revenue. Is it recurring or one-time? Is it contractual or at-will? Is it concentrated in a small number of customers or diversified across many? Is it growing, flat, or declining? Is the revenue recognition appropriate, meaning is the business booking revenue when it is actually earned, or is it pulling future revenue forward to make current periods look better?

Customer concentration is a critical risk factor that the QofE will highlight. If one customer accounts for more than fifteen to twenty percent of total revenue, that is a significant risk. If one customer accounts for more than thirty percent, that is a potential deal-breaker unless there are long-term contracts in place with that customer. Why? Because if you buy a business and then lose that one major customer, your revenue can drop by a third overnight, and your EBITDA can drop by far more than a third because your fixed costs remain the same. A QofE firm will quantify this risk and help you understand the real vulnerability.

The fifth category is working capital analysis. Working capital is the difference between current assets and current liabilities. It represents the cash the business needs to operate on a day-to-day basis. The QofE firm will calculate the normalized working capital requirement and compare it to what the seller is proposing to leave in the business at closing. This matters because if the business needs three hundred thousand dollars in working capital to operate smoothly and the seller is planning to strip that out before closing, you are going to need to inject three hundred thousand dollars of your own cash on day one just to keep the lights on. That three hundred thousand dollars needs to be factored into your total acquisition cost.

DEAL WALKTHROUGH

Let me walk you through a realistic deal scenario so you can see how all of these concepts play out in practice. Imagine you are looking at acquiring a physician staffing company. The seller tells you the business generates one point two million dollars in annual revenue and four hundred thousand dollars in EBITDA. They want a purchase price of one point six million dollars, which represents a four-times EBITDA multiple. On paper, this looks reasonable. Four times EBITDA for a service business with decent margins is within the normal range. But you are smart, so you hire a QofE firm. You pay eight thousand dollars for their analysis. Here is what they find.

First, they discover that one hundred and twenty thousand dollars of last year's revenue came from a single large contract that has already ended. That client left three months ago and is not coming back. So recurring revenue is actually one point zero eight million, not one point two million.

Second, they find that the seller's spouse is on the payroll at seventy-five thousand dollars per year for a marketing role. When they investigate, they discover this person works roughly five hours per week and most of their duties could be handled by a part-time contractor at fifteen thousand dollars per year. That is a sixty-thousand-dollar add-back to EBITDA.

Third, they discover that the seller runs a personal vehicle lease through the business at nine hundred dollars per month, plus fuel and insurance totaling another three hundred per month. That is fourteen thousand four hundred dollars per year in personal expenses being run through the company. Another add-back.

Fourth, they find that thirty-eight percent of the company's revenue comes from a single hospital system. There is no written contract with this hospital system. It is a handshake relationship based on the seller's personal connections with the hospital's chief medical officer, who is retiring in eighteen months.

Fifth, the working capital analysis reveals that the business needs approximately one hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars in working capital to operate, but the seller's proposed deal structure would leave only ninety thousand dollars in working capital at closing. You would need to bring eighty-five thousand dollars to the table just to fund normal operations.

Now let us recalculate. The reported EBITDA was four hundred thousand dollars. The QofE firm makes the following adjustments. They subtract the lost contract revenue impact, which after accounting for the variable costs associated with that revenue, reduces EBITDA by approximately fifty-five thousand dollars. They add back the spouse's excess compensation of sixty thousand dollars. They add back the personal vehicle expenses of fourteen thousand four hundred dollars. After all adjustments, the true adjusted EBITDA is approximately four hundred nineteen thousand four hundred dollars. In this case, the adjustments roughly offset each other, and adjusted EBITDA is close to reported EBITDA. But here is the critical insight: the QofE revealed risks that the raw number does not capture.

That thirty-eight percent customer concentration with no contract and a retiring champion at the hospital is a massive risk. If you lose that relationship, your revenue drops by roughly four hundred and ten thousand dollars and your EBITDA could swing negative. A savvy buyer would use this information to negotiate either a lower purchase price, an earnout structure tied to retention of that key customer, or a requirement that the seller secure a written contract with the hospital system before closing. You might propose a purchase price of one point three million instead of one point six million, with an additional three hundred thousand payable over two years contingent on the hospital system remaining a client. That structure protects you from the downside while still giving the seller a fair price if the relationship holds.

The eighty-five thousand dollar working capital shortfall also becomes a negotiation point. Either the seller leaves adequate working capital in the business, or the purchase price is reduced by eighty-five thousand dollars to account for the cash you will need to inject.

Without the QofE, you would have paid one point six million dollars for a business with a ticking time bomb embedded in its customer base and a working capital hole you did not know about. With the QofE, you are negotiating from a position of knowledge and strength. That eight-thousand-dollar investment in the QofE just saved you potentially hundreds of thousands of dollars and possibly the entire deal.

RISK ANALYSIS

Let me now outline the specific risks that a QofE analysis is designed to surface, because understanding these risks will help you evaluate any QofE report you receive.

The first risk is earnings manipulation. Some sellers will deliberately time revenue recognition, delay expenses, or accelerate collections to make the trailing twelve months look better than they actually are. A common tactic is to pull revenue forward by billing customers early or recognizing revenue before the work is completed. Another tactic is to defer maintenance or capital expenditures to temporarily inflate cash flow. The QofE firm analyzes trends across multiple years and looks for anomalies that suggest manipulation. If revenue spiked by twenty percent in the trailing twelve months but had been flat for the three years prior, that demands explanation.

The second risk is margin degradation. Even if top-line revenue is growing, if margins are declining, the business may be earning less money on more work. This can happen when a business is competing on price to win new clients, when input costs are rising faster than prices, or when the business is taking on lower-margin work to fill capacity. A QofE will analyze gross margin and operating margin trends and flag any deterioration.

The third risk is customer or payer mix shifts. In healthcare-adjacent businesses, this is particularly important. If a medical practice's revenue is shifting from commercial insurance to Medicare and Medicaid, the same volume of patients can produce significantly less revenue per encounter. The QofE firm should analyze revenue per customer or revenue per unit of service over time to spot these trends.

The fourth risk is key person dependency. If the business's revenue depends on the seller's personal relationships, reputation, or clinical skills, there is a real question about how much of that revenue will survive the ownership transition. The QofE will note this risk, but you need to think carefully about transition planning. A staffing company whose clients work with the seller personally is different from a diagnostic lab whose clients order tests through an automated system.

The fifth risk is deferred capital expenditure. Some sellers stop investing in the business in the years before a sale to maximize short-term cash flow. They defer equipment replacement, skip facility maintenance, delay technology upgrades, and let staffing levels thin out. The business looks more profitable on paper, but the new owner inherits a backlog of capital needs that will eat into future cash flow. The QofE firm will review capital expenditure trends and flag any unusual declines.

The sixth risk is off-balance-sheet liabilities. These are obligations that do not show up on the standard financial statements. Pending lawsuits. Warranty obligations. Environmental cleanup costs. Unfunded pension liabilities. Tax disputes with the IRS or state tax authorities. Personal guarantees on leases that transfer with the business. A good QofE firm will ask about all of these and will review the footnotes to the financial statements and any correspondence with legal counsel.

The seventh risk is revenue sustainability in a changing market. The QofE firm should comment on whether the business model itself is sustainable. A medical transcription company might show strong historical earnings, but if the industry is shifting to AI-based transcription, those earnings may not be repeatable regardless of how well you manage the business. This is where industry knowledge matters, and it is also where your own expertise as a physician becomes valuable. You understand healthcare trends in ways that a generalist financial analyst may not.

ACTION STEPS

Here is exactly what you need to do when you reach the diligence phase of any acquisition.

Step one. Hire a QofE firm before you sign a letter of intent, or at minimum, make the letter of intent contingent on satisfactory completion of a QofE analysis. Do not let a seller pressure you into skipping this step. Do not let a broker tell you it is unnecessary. Do not let your own enthusiasm for the deal override your judgment. The QofE is non-negotiable.

Step two. Choose the right QofE firm. You want a firm that specializes in transactions in the size range you are pursuing. The Big Four accounting firms do QofE analyses, but they are overkill and overpriced for a two-million-dollar deal. Look for regional accounting firms with dedicated transaction advisory practices or boutique firms that specialize in QofE work for lower middle market transactions. Ask your deal attorney for referrals. Ask other physicians who have completed acquisitions. Interview at least two firms before selecting one. Ask them how many QofE reports they have completed in the last twelve months, what industries they have worked in, and whether they have experience with healthcare or healthcare-adjacent businesses.

Step three. Give the QofE firm full access. Do not filter the information flow. You want them to have direct access to the seller's financial records, and ideally direct access to the seller's bookkeeper or controller. The more access the QofE firm has, the more thorough their analysis will be. If the seller resists providing access, that is a red flag.

Step four. Review the QofE report with your deal attorney and your CPA. The QofE firm will produce a detailed report, usually thirty to sixty pages. Do not try to interpret it alone. Your deal attorney will help you understand the legal implications of the findings. Your CPA will help you understand the tax implications. Together, the three of you will determine which findings are deal-breakers, which are negotiation points, and which are acceptable risks.

Step five. Use the QofE findings to renegotiate the deal terms. This is where the QofE pays for itself many times over. Every negative finding is a lever you can pull in negotiation. Adjusted EBITDA came in lower than reported? The purchase price should decrease proportionally. Customer concentration risk identified? Propose an earnout structure. Working capital shortfall? Adjust the purchase price or require the seller to fund the gap. Deferred capital expenditure? Negotiate a hold-back or escrow to cover the catch-up spending. Key person dependency? Require a longer seller transition period with seller compensation tied to revenue retention.

Step six. If the QofE reveals fundamental problems with the business, walk away. This is the hardest step because by the time you are doing diligence, you have usually invested significant time and emotional energy into the deal. You may have told friends and family about it. You may have started imagining yourself running the business. None of that matters. If the numbers do not work, the numbers do not work. There will be other deals. There will not be another two million dollars if you lose it on a bad acquisition.

Let me leave you with one final thought. The QofE analysis is not about finding reasons to kill a deal. It is about finding the truth. Sometimes the truth is that the business is exactly as good as the seller says it is, and the QofE confirms that you are paying a fair price. That confirmation is worth the eight or ten thousand dollars all by itself because it lets you move forward with confidence instead of anxiety. Other times the truth is that the business has real problems, and the QofE gives you the information you need to either walk away or restructure the deal to protect yourself. Either way, you win. The only scenario where you lose is the one where you skip the QofE and find out the truth after you have already written the check. Do not be that buyer. Hire the QofE firm. Pay the five to ten thousand dollars. Get the truth before you buy.
