Welcome back. Today we are going to dismantle one of the most persistent illusions in how physicians study, train, and teach. The topic is interleaving versus blocked practice, and I want to warn you up front: what you are about to hear will feel counterintuitive. It will challenge the way you have organized your study sessions for boards, the way you structure your reading lists, and possibly even the way you think about building surgical skill. That discomfort is actually the point, and by the end of this session you will understand exactly why.

Let me set the frame. Blocked practice is the approach most of us default to. You sit down to study spine pathology, and you study spine pathology for three hours. You drill posterior cervical fusions in the lab until you feel fluid, and then you move on to lumbar decompressions next week. You read five chapters on cerebrovascular disease in a row before switching to anything else. The defining feature of blocked practice is repetition of a single skill or topic in a concentrated, uninterrupted block. It feels productive. It feels efficient. And by the end of a blocked session, you genuinely do perform better on that isolated task. The problem is that this improvement is largely an illusion. It reflects momentary performance, not durable learning.

Interleaving is the alternative. Instead of practicing one thing repeatedly before moving to the next, you deliberately mix different topics, skills, or problem types within a single session. You study a spine case, then a tumor case, then a vascular case, then return to spine. You practice a burr hole, then a craniotomy closure technique, then a microdiscectomy step, cycling through rather than grinding on any single one. It feels harder. It feels slower. You make more errors during the session itself. And that is precisely what makes it so powerful for long-term retention and transfer.

The research behind this distinction is extensive and remarkably consistent. The landmark studies go back decades, but the work that should matter most to you as a physician comes from Robert Bjork's laboratory at UCLA and from applied research in medical education. Bjork introduced the concept of desirable difficulties, the idea that conditions which make learning feel harder during practice often produce stronger, more flexible, more durable knowledge. Interleaving is one of the most potent desirable difficulties we know of.

Here is the core finding, replicated across dozens of studies and multiple domains. When learners use blocked practice, their performance during training is superior. They feel more confident. They rate their learning as higher. But when you test them days or weeks later, the interleaving group dramatically outperforms the blocked group. This is not a small effect. In some studies the advantage of interleaving over blocking on delayed tests is thirty to fifty percent or more. The blocked practice group essentially built a house on sand. Their fluency during practice was real, but it was supported by short-term memory and contextual cues that evaporated once the session ended.

Why does interleaving work so well? There are two primary mechanisms, and both are directly relevant to clinical medicine. The first is retrieval practice. When you interleave, every time you return to a topic after working on something else, you have to reload it from memory. That act of retrieval, that effortful reconstruction of the relevant knowledge, strengthens the memory trace in a way that simply re-reading or continuing to practice the same thing never can. Each retrieval is a rep for your memory. Blocked practice minimizes retrieval because you never leave the topic long enough to need to retrieve it.

The second mechanism is discrimination learning, and this one is arguably even more important for physicians. Discrimination learning is the process of learning to tell things apart, of recognizing what makes a posterior fossa tumor different from a cerebellopontine angle schwannoma, of distinguishing a Type I from a Type II odontoid fracture not just when they are presented in isolation but when they are mixed together and you have to figure out which is which. Blocked practice lets you avoid this challenge entirely. When you study spine for three hours, every problem is a spine problem. You never have to ask yourself, is this spine or is this something else? You never have to compare and contrast across categories in real time. But interleaving forces exactly this kind of discriminative processing. When a vascular case follows a tumor case follows a spine case, your brain is constantly asking: what kind of problem is this? What strategy applies here? How is this different from what I just saw? That ongoing comparison builds the pattern recognition architecture that expert clinicians rely on every day.

Think about what actually happens in clinical practice. Patients do not arrive pre-sorted by chapter heading. Your Monday morning does not consist exclusively of lumbar stenosis. You see a patient with a brain tumor at seven thirty, a cervical myelopathy at eight, a carpal tunnel at eight thirty, a traumatic subdural hematoma consult at nine, and a peripheral nerve case at nine forty-five. Your entire professional life is interleaved. The question is whether your study and training methods are preparing you for that reality or for a fantasy version where problems come neatly organized.

Let me go deeper on the discrimination learning piece because it has a direct analog in surgical education that I think will resonate. One of the classic interleaving studies involved teaching students to identify painting styles of different artists. In the blocked condition, students saw twelve paintings by one artist, then twelve by another, then twelve by a third. In the interleaved condition, the paintings were mixed together. On a later test with new paintings they had never seen, the interleaved group was dramatically better at identifying which artist had created each painting. They had learned to see the distinguishing features, the subtle differences in brushwork, palette, and composition, because they had been forced to compare across artists during learning. The blocked group had learned what each artist looked like in isolation but struggled when they had to tell them apart.

Now map this onto surgical pathology. When you study glioblastoma for an hour, you become temporarily fluent in the features of glioblastoma. But you have not practiced distinguishing it from metastasis, from lymphoma, from abscess. When you interleave, you see a glioblastoma MRI, then a metastatic lesion, then a lymphoma, then an abscess, and your brain is doing the critical work of asking: what features tell me this is one and not the other? That is the work that builds diagnostic expertise. That is the work that translates to three in the morning when you are looking at an MRI and you need to know what you are dealing with before you open the skull.

Now, I need to be precise here because interleaving is not universally superior, and understanding when blocked practice is still the right choice is part of mastering this framework. Blocked practice has a legitimate and important role in early skill acquisition. When you are learning a completely new motor skill, a new surgical technique you have never performed, a new instrument you have never held, you need concentrated repetition to build the basic motor pattern. A first-year resident learning to tie a surgical knot should tie that knot fifty times in a row. They need blocked practice to establish the fundamental movement sequence before they have anything to interleave. The same applies to early cognitive learning. If a concept is genuinely new and you have no existing schema for it, a period of focused blocked study to build the initial framework is appropriate.

The key insight is that blocked practice is for building, and interleaving is for strengthening, connecting, and making knowledge flexible and retrievable. Once you have the basic pattern, continuing to block is no longer helping you. It is just making you feel comfortable, and comfort during practice is almost always a signal that deep learning has stopped. The shift from blocked to interleaved practice should happen earlier than most people think. You do not need to be an expert before you start interleaving. You just need to have the basic familiarity with each component. If you can perform the skill at all, even clumsily, you are ready to start interleaving.

There is another nuance worth discussing. The spacing between interleaved items matters. If you switch topics every thirty seconds, the cognitive load becomes overwhelming and the retrieval benefit is lost because you never encode anything deeply enough to retrieve. If you switch every three hours, you are essentially doing blocked practice with a different label. The sweet spot for cognitive topics is typically switching every fifteen to thirty minutes, or every three to five problems. For motor skills, switching every five to ten repetitions tends to work well. These are guidelines, not rigid rules. The principle is that you should switch often enough that returning to a topic requires genuine effort to recall where you left off, but not so often that you never engage deeply with any single topic.

Let me talk now about how this applies specifically to your life as a surgeon and a physician leader. Start with board preparation, because this is where the implications are most immediately actionable. The way most physicians study for boards is almost purely blocked. You open your spine chapter, you read the spine chapter, you do spine questions for two hours, and you feel like you made progress. Tomorrow you do cranial. Next day you do peripheral nerve. This approach will produce the illusion of mastery during study and disappointing performance on the actual exam, which is, of course, interleaved. The questions jump from topic to topic exactly the way interleaved practice does.

Instead, structure your study sessions to mix topics. Do ten spine questions, then ten vascular questions, then ten tumor questions, then circle back. Read a section on cervical disc disease, then a section on Chiari malformation, then a section on carpal tunnel, then return to cervical topics. It will feel less productive. You will get more questions wrong during practice. You will feel like you are not making progress. Ignore those feelings. They are the desirable difficulty doing its work. When you sit for the exam and the questions are jumping between topics unpredictably, you will be the one who practiced under those exact conditions.

Now apply this to surgical skill development. If you are in a simulation lab, do not spend the entire session on one procedure. Rotate through different techniques. Practice your microsurgical anastomosis for ten minutes, then switch to endoscopic work, then switch to a craniotomy closure technique, then return to microsurgery. Your performance during the session will look worse than if you had blocked. But the retention and transfer will be dramatically better, and retention and transfer are what matter when you are in the operating room weeks later.

For continuing education and ongoing professional development, the same principle applies. Do not binge-read five papers on the same narrow topic. Read one paper on spine biomechanics, then one on glioma molecular subtypes, then one on peripheral nerve repair biology, then return to spine. Build a rotation through your reading material. Your journal club preparation, your conference review, your maintenance of certification study should all be structured as interleaved practice.

As a physician leader, this framework also has implications for how you teach residents and how you structure educational conferences. If you are running a didactic series, resist the temptation to organize it as spine month followed by cranial month followed by peripheral nerve month. Mix the topics. Monday is a spine case, Wednesday is a tumor case, Friday is a vascular case. The residents will feel like they are learning less. Their evaluations of the teaching may initially be lower because blocked instruction feels more organized and coherent. But their actual learning, measured on delayed tests and clinical performance, will be superior. This is one of those areas where doing the right thing pedagogically requires the courage to look less polished in the moment.

Here is a practice exercise I want you to implement this week. It is simple but it will concretely demonstrate the principle. Take whatever you are currently studying or reviewing, whether it is board prep material, journal articles, or clinical guidelines. For the next three study sessions, deliberately interleave. If you were going to study one topic for an hour, break it into three twenty-minute segments and fill the gaps with different topics. Keep a brief log of how the session felt, specifically noting your subjective sense of how well you learned. Then, three days after each session, quiz yourself on the material from each session. I predict you will find a disconnect between how the sessions felt and how much you actually retained. The sessions that felt hardest, most disjointed, and least productive will produce the best retention on delayed test. That experiential demonstration is more convincing than any study I can cite.

A second exercise for the surgical skills domain. The next time you are in a skills lab or even practicing knot tying at home, set a timer and rotate between three different skills every five minutes. Compare your retention of those skills a week later against the retention you typically see when you spend the full session on a single skill. Again, the blocked session will feel better in the moment, but the interleaved session will win on delayed performance.

A third exercise is for your role as a teacher. The next time you are preparing a teaching session for residents or medical students, deliberately include cases or questions from at least three different subspecialty domains. When the learners struggle with the transitions, resist the urge to simplify by focusing on one domain. Instead, use the struggle as a teaching moment. Explain the concept of interleaving. Help them understand that the difficulty they are experiencing is not a sign of poor teaching or poor learning. It is the mechanism of deep learning itself.

Let me bring this all together. The core tension in interleaving versus blocked practice is between the experience of learning and the reality of learning. Blocked practice optimizes for the experience. It feels smooth, fluent, and productive. You can see yourself improving within the session, and that feels good. Interleaving optimizes for the reality. It builds the retrieval strength, the discriminative ability, and the flexible knowledge structures that actually determine how you perform when it counts, in the clinic, in the operating room, on the board exam.

As a surgeon, you already live in an interleaved world. Your patients do not organize themselves by textbook chapter. Your call nights do not restrict themselves to a single pathology. Your career does not unfold in neat blocked segments. The question is simply whether your deliberate practice, your study, your continuing education, and your teaching are aligned with that reality or working against it.

The desirable difficulties framework, of which interleaving is a central pillar, asks you to tolerate short-term discomfort for long-term gain. That trade-off should feel familiar. It is the same trade-off you ask patients to make when you recommend surgery. The recovery is hard, but the outcome is worth it. Apply that same logic to your own learning. The study session that feels hard and disjointed is the one that is building the architecture of expertise. The session that feels smooth and easy is often the one where you are just going through the motions, building fluency that will not survive the weekend.

One final nuance. Interleaving and blocked practice are not binary. You do not have to choose one and abandon the other forever. The optimal approach is developmental. Use blocked practice when you are first encountering a skill or concept and need to build basic competence. Shift to interleaving as soon as you have enough foundational knowledge to begin the work of discrimination, retrieval, and flexible application. And as you approach mastery, increase the interleaving further, mixing not just across topics within a domain but across domains entirely. The expert surgeon who can draw on principles from biomechanics, neurophysiology, radiology, and clinical decision-making fluidly during a single case has achieved the highest form of interleaved knowledge. That integration does not happen by accident. It happens because at some point they stopped studying each of those domains in isolation and started studying them in relation to each other.

Your assignment for this week is clear. Identify your current study or practice routine. Notice where you are defaulting to blocked practice because it feels comfortable. Introduce interleaving deliberately, even in small doses. Tolerate the discomfort. Track your delayed retention. Let the results speak for themselves. The science on this is about as close to settled as learning science gets. The only question is whether you will actually implement it or whether you will keep doing what feels easier. The surgeon in you already knows the answer to that question. You do not choose the easy path. You choose the path that produces the best outcome. This is that path.
