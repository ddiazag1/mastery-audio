Welcome to this session on conflict resolution strategies. This is one of the most consequential leadership competencies you will ever develop, and I want to be direct with you from the start: your technical skill as a surgeon may get you into a leadership role, but your ability to navigate conflict is what will determine whether you thrive in that role or burn out from it. Conflict is not a sign of dysfunction. Conflict is the inevitable byproduct of intelligent, passionate people working together under high stakes conditions. The question is never whether conflict will arise. The question is whether you have a systematic framework for engaging with it productively.

Today we are going to build that framework from the ground up. We will start with the most widely validated model in organizational psychology for understanding conflict behavior, the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument. Then we will layer on interest-based resolution principles drawn from the Harvard Negotiation Project. We will cover de-escalation techniques grounded in crisis communication research. And throughout, we will anchor every concept in the specific conflicts you face as a surgeon and physician leader: interdepartmental turf wars, operating room scheduling disputes, patient and family complaints, disagreements with hospital administration, and friction within your own surgical team. By the end of this session, you will not just understand conflict resolution in theory. You will have a practical decision-making toolkit for choosing the right approach in the right moment, every time.

Let us begin with the Thomas-Kilmann framework, because it gives us a shared vocabulary for something most physicians handle purely on instinct. Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann identified five distinct modes of conflict behavior, and they organized them along two axes. The first axis is assertiveness, meaning the degree to which you try to satisfy your own concerns. The second axis is cooperativeness, meaning the degree to which you try to satisfy the other party's concerns. Every conflict behavior you have ever used falls somewhere on this two-dimensional grid, and understanding where gives you extraordinary power to be deliberate rather than reactive.

The first mode is competing. Competing is high assertiveness and low cooperativeness. You are pursuing your own concerns at the other party's expense. This is the mode most surgeons default to, and for good reason. Your training selected for it. When you are in the operating room and a complication arises, you do not convene a committee. You make a decision, you execute it, and lives are saved because of your willingness to assert authority without hesitation. The problem is that competing is a surgical instrument, not a Swiss army knife. It is devastating when misapplied. When you compete in a conflict with a colleague over resource allocation, you may win the battle but you create an adversary who will make your life harder for years. Competing is appropriate when the stakes are genuinely high, when a quick decision is essential, when you are confident you are right on a matter of patient safety or ethical principle, or when you need to protect yourself or your team from someone who is exploiting cooperative behavior. It is inappropriate when the relationship matters as much as the outcome, when you might be wrong, or when buy-in from the other party is necessary for implementation.

The second mode is collaborating. Collaborating is high assertiveness and high cooperativeness. You are trying to find a solution that fully satisfies both your concerns and the other party's concerns. This is the gold standard of conflict resolution, but it comes with a cost: it requires significant time, energy, and emotional labor. Collaborating means sitting down with the other party, genuinely understanding their interests, sharing your own interests transparently, and working together to generate creative options that neither of you would have thought of alone. In a surgical context, this is the mode you use when you and the chief of anesthesiology disagree about case scheduling protocols. You both have legitimate concerns. You need adequate block time to run an efficient service. They need flexibility to staff complex cases across multiple rooms. A collaborative approach might yield an innovative scheduling system that serves both departments better than either side's original position. Collaboration is appropriate when both parties' concerns are too important to compromise, when you need commitment rather than compliance, when the relationship is valuable and ongoing, and when you have the time to invest. It is inappropriate when the issue is trivial, when you are under severe time pressure, or when the other party has no interest in a collaborative process.

The third mode is compromising. Compromising sits in the middle of both axes, moderate assertiveness and moderate cooperativeness. You are looking for an expedient, mutually acceptable solution that partially satisfies both parties. Compromise is the workhorse of organizational life. It is not as elegant as collaboration, but it is faster and it works. When two surgical services both want the same block time on Wednesday mornings, and neither has a clearly superior claim, splitting the time or alternating weeks is a compromise that gets everyone back to work. Compromising is appropriate when the goals are moderately important but not worth the time required for full collaboration, when two equally powerful parties are at an impasse, when you need a temporary settlement under time pressure, or as a fallback when collaboration has failed. It is inappropriate when you can find a genuinely integrative solution if you invest the time, or when the issue involves a core principle that should not be split.

The fourth mode is avoiding. Avoiding is low assertiveness and low cooperativeness. You are neither pursuing your own concerns nor the other party's concerns. You are sidestepping the conflict entirely. Physicians often view avoidance as weakness, but that is a misunderstanding of the mode. Strategic avoidance is a powerful tool. When a colleague sends you an inflammatory email at the end of a long operative day, choosing not to respond until morning is avoidance, and it is exactly the right call. When two of your residents are bickering about call scheduling and both come to you to adjudicate, telling them to work it out themselves is avoidance, and it builds their own conflict resolution capacity. Avoidance is appropriate when the issue is trivial, when you have no chance of satisfying your concerns, when the potential damage of confrontation outweighs the benefits, when you need time to cool down or gather information, or when others can resolve the conflict more effectively. It is inappropriate when the issue is important and will not resolve on its own, when delay will make the situation worse, or when avoidance is being driven by fear rather than strategy.

The fifth mode is accommodating. Accommodating is low assertiveness and high cooperativeness. You are neglecting your own concerns to satisfy the other party's concerns. For surgeons, this is often the most uncomfortable mode because it can feel like losing. But accommodating is a strategic choice when used deliberately. When a colleague asks you to swap call weekends because of a family event, and it is genuinely inconvenient but not a hardship for you, accommodating builds relational capital that you can draw on later. When you are wrong in a disagreement, accommodating is simply intellectual honesty. When an issue matters far more to the other person than it does to you, accommodating is efficient generosity. Accommodating is appropriate when you realize you are wrong, when the issue is much more important to the other party, when you want to build goodwill for future conflicts that matter more, or when continued competition would damage the relationship beyond repair. It is inappropriate when it becomes a pattern, when you are accommodating out of fear rather than strategy, or when the issue involves patient safety or ethical standards that should not be yielded.

Now, here is the critical insight that separates effective leaders from everyone else. There is no single best mode. The Thomas-Kilmann framework is not a personality test that tells you which mode you are. It is a situational assessment tool that tells you which mode to deploy. The most effective physician leaders are ambidextrous. They can compete fiercely when patient safety demands it, collaborate patiently when building a new service line requires buy-in, compromise efficiently when the issue does not warrant a prolonged process, avoid strategically when timing is wrong, and accommodate graciously when the relationship outweighs the issue. Your job is to expand your repertoire so that you can match your mode to the situation rather than defaulting to whatever feels most natural.

Let us now layer on interest-based resolution, which deepens our approach beyond mode selection. Interest-based resolution comes from the work of Roger Fisher and William Ury at the Harvard Negotiation Project, and its core principle is deceptively simple: separate positions from interests. A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Positions are often incompatible. Interests usually are not.

Here is an example from surgical practice. You and an orthopedic surgeon both need the same operating room on Thursday afternoon. Your position is that you need Room Four from one to five. Their position is that they need Room Four from one to five. If you only engage at the level of positions, this is a zero-sum conflict. Someone wins, someone loses, or you split the baby with a compromise that leaves both of you inefficient. But if you dig into interests, you may discover that your interest is that you need a room with the microscope and neuromonitoring setup because you are doing a microdiscectomy. Their interest is that they need a room with a large C-arm and a fracture table for a hip pinning. Room Four has the microscope. Room Six has the fracture table. The resolution becomes obvious when you understand interests, and it was invisible when you were arguing about positions.

Interest-based resolution follows a structured process. First, you separate the people from the problem. This means acknowledging emotions without being ruled by them, building a working relationship even when you disagree on substance, and refusing to let personal grievances contaminate the negotiation. Second, you focus on interests rather than positions. Ask why. Ask why not. Ask what concern the other party's position is meant to address. Share your own underlying interests openly. Third, you generate options for mutual gain before deciding. Brainstorm without committing. Look for creative solutions that expand the pie rather than dividing it. Fourth, you insist on using objective criteria whenever possible. Base the resolution on professional standards, evidence, precedent, fair procedures, or scientific data rather than on who has more power or who yells loudest.

This interest-based approach is transformative in physician practice. Consider a common interdepartmental conflict: emergency medicine wants neurosurgery to respond to consults within thirty minutes, and neurosurgery is pushing back because many of these consults are not emergent. At the positional level, this is a fight about response time benchmarks. At the interest level, emergency medicine's interest is protecting patients with time-sensitive neurological emergencies and reducing their own medicolegal exposure. Neurosurgery's interest is not being pulled out of the operating room for non-urgent issues that could wait. An interest-based solution might involve a structured triage protocol: genuine emergencies get an immediate response, urgent cases get a thirty-minute callback, and routine consults get addressed within two hours. Both departments get their core interests met. The conflict dissolves.

Now let us talk about de-escalation, because even with the best framework in the world, you will encounter situations where emotions are running high and rational problem-solving is temporarily impossible. De-escalation is the skill of lowering the emotional temperature enough to re-engage the prefrontal cortex, both yours and the other party's. This is neurobiologically grounded. When someone is in an amygdala-driven threat response, their capacity for complex reasoning, perspective-taking, and creative problem-solving drops dramatically. You cannot collaborate with someone whose brain is in fight-or-flight mode. You have to de-escalate first.

The first de-escalation technique is managing your own physiology. Before you can calm anyone else, you need to regulate yourself. When you feel your heart rate climbing in a tense conversation, take a slow breath. Drop your shoulders. Unclench your jaw. Speak more slowly and at a lower pitch. This is not weakness. This is self-regulation, and it is the foundation of every effective de-escalation. Your calm is contagious in the same way that the other person's agitation is contagious. Choose which emotional state you want to propagate.

The second technique is active listening with validation. When someone is upset, they need to feel heard before they can hear you. This does not mean you agree with them. It means you demonstrate that you understand their experience. You might say something like, "I can see that you are frustrated about the scheduling change, and I understand why. You had planned your week around the original schedule, and this disruption creates real problems for you." Notice that you have not conceded anything. You have not agreed that the scheduling change was wrong. You have simply validated their emotional experience, and that validation alone can reduce the intensity of the conflict by half.

The third technique is reframing. Reframing takes an adversarial statement and restates it in terms that open the door to problem-solving. When a colleague says, "You always take the best cases and leave me with the garbage," you can reframe by saying, "It sounds like the case distribution feels unfair to you. Can we look at the data together and see if there is a pattern we should address?" You have taken an accusation and turned it into a shared problem to solve. This is a profound shift in the dynamics of the conversation.

The fourth technique is strategic use of time and space. Not every conflict needs to be resolved in the moment it arises. In fact, some of the worst outcomes happen because people try to resolve complex issues in the heat of the moment, in a crowded hallway, between cases. It is entirely appropriate to say, "This is important and I want to give it the attention it deserves. Can we sit down Thursday morning and work through it properly?" This is not avoidance. This is creating the conditions for effective resolution.

The fifth technique is naming the dynamic without attacking the person. When a conversation is going sideways, you can say, "I notice we are both getting entrenched in our positions. Can we step back and talk about what we are each really trying to accomplish here?" This metacognitive move lifts both parties above the conflict and turns them into collaborative observers of the process rather than adversaries within it.

Let us now apply all of this to three specific conflict scenarios you are likely to face as a surgeon and physician leader.

Scenario one: operating room scheduling disputes. These are among the most common and most emotionally charged conflicts in surgical practice because OR time is the fundamental currency of a surgeon's productivity, income, and professional identity. When you find yourself in a scheduling conflict, start by identifying the mode the situation calls for. If patient safety is at stake, such as a case being bumped that should not wait, compete assertively. If two services have legitimate needs and an ongoing relationship, collaborate to design a scheduling system that works for both. If the issue is a one-time conflict over a single slot, compromise may be the fastest path forward. Then apply interest-based thinking. Ask what each party really needs. Is it total block hours? Is it specific time slots that align with clinic schedules? Is it access to specialized equipment? Is it the ability to add urgent cases without a bureaucratic process? Often the underlying interests suggest solutions that the surface-level scheduling fight obscures.

Scenario two: interdepartmental conflicts. These arise constantly between surgical and medical services, between surgery and anesthesia, between surgery and nursing, and between different surgical subspecialties. The key challenge with interdepartmental conflict is that the parties often do not share the same mental models, incentive structures, or professional cultures. A surgeon thinks in terms of cases completed and outcomes achieved. A hospitalist thinks in terms of length of stay and readmission rates. An administrator thinks in terms of budget variances and patient satisfaction scores. When you encounter interdepartmental conflict, your first move should be to map the interests of all stakeholders. Your second move should be to identify shared interests, because they almost always exist. Both the surgeon and the hospitalist want the patient to do well. Both the surgeon and the administrator want the department to be financially viable. Start from shared interests and work outward toward the points of disagreement. This reframes the conflict from adversarial to collaborative.

Scenario three: patient and family complaints. These require a distinctly different approach because the power dynamics are asymmetrical, the emotional stakes are deeply personal, and the risk of litigation is always present. When a patient or family member is upset, your first job is not to explain, justify, or defend. Your first job is to listen. Use active listening and validation. Acknowledge the experience without admitting fault in a way that creates legal liability. Something like, "I hear you, and I understand this is not the experience you expected. Tell me more about what happened from your perspective." Then apply interest-based thinking. What does this patient or family actually need? Sometimes it is information. Sometimes it is an apology. Sometimes it is a change in the care plan. Sometimes it is simply to be heard by the person in charge. When you identify the real interest, you can often resolve the complaint in a way that strengthens the relationship rather than destroying it. Many patients who file formal complaints or lawsuits report that they did so not because of the original medical issue but because they felt dismissed, ignored, or disrespected when they tried to raise concerns. Your de-escalation and listening skills are your best malpractice prevention.

Now let us move to a practice exercise. I want you to think about a conflict you are currently facing or have recently faced. It can be with a colleague, an administrator, a patient, a member of your team, or anyone else in your professional life. Hold that specific conflict in your mind as I walk you through this exercise.

First, identify the positions. What does each party say they want? State each position as clearly and simply as you can. Do not judge the positions yet. Just name them.

Second, identify the interests behind those positions. For each party, ask: why do they want what they want? What underlying need, concern, or fear is driving their position? Try to identify at least two interests for each party that go beyond the stated position.

Third, identify shared interests. Where do the parties' interests overlap? There is almost always at least one shared interest, even if it is as broad as "we both want this department to succeed" or "we both want this patient to have a good outcome."

Fourth, select your mode. Given the stakes, the relationship, the time available, and the power dynamics, which Thomas-Kilmann mode is most appropriate for this conflict? Be honest with yourself about whether you are choosing the mode that fits the situation or defaulting to the mode that feels most comfortable.

Fifth, design your approach. Based on the mode you have selected and the interests you have identified, what specifically would you say or do in your next interaction with the other party? Draft the opening statement you would use. If you have chosen collaboration, your opening might sound like, "I would like to find a solution that works for both of us. Can I start by understanding what is most important to you in this situation?" If you have chosen compromise, it might sound like, "I think we both have valid concerns here, and I would like to propose a middle ground that addresses the most important issues for each of us." If you have chosen to compete, it might sound like, "I need to be direct about where I stand on this, and I want to explain why." Whatever your approach, draft it. Rehearse it. The gap between knowing a framework and using it is bridged by preparation.

Sixth, anticipate the response. How is the other party likely to react to your approach? What will you do if they escalate? What will you do if they stonewall? What will you do if they agree in the meeting but fail to follow through afterward? Having a contingency plan for the most likely responses dramatically increases your effectiveness.

Let us bring everything together now. Conflict resolution is not a single skill. It is an integrated competency that draws on self-awareness, emotional regulation, strategic thinking, communication skill, and genuine respect for the people you work with. The Thomas-Kilmann framework gives you a map of the five fundamental approaches to conflict, and more importantly, it gives you the ability to choose deliberately rather than react habitually. Most surgeons default to competing because their training rewarded assertiveness and decisiveness. But the operating room is a highly structured, hierarchical environment where competing often works. The rest of your professional life is not. When you step into the hallway, the conference room, or the administrator's office, you need all five modes available to you.

Interest-based resolution gives you a method for transforming zero-sum conflicts into collaborative problem-solving. When you move the conversation from positions to interests, you unlock creative solutions that are invisible at the surface level. This is not soft or idealistic. This is strategic and pragmatic. The best negotiators in the world, in business, in diplomacy, and in medicine, are interest-based negotiators.

De-escalation gives you the ability to manage the emotional dimension of conflict, which is where most physicians stumble. You can have the right framework and the right strategy, but if you cannot manage your own emotional reactivity and help the other party manage theirs, none of it works. The techniques we covered today, self-regulation, active listening, validation, reframing, strategic use of time, and naming the dynamic, are skills that improve with deliberate practice. Start using them in low-stakes situations, a minor scheduling irritation, a small disagreement with a colleague, so that they become automatic by the time you face a high-stakes conflict.

I want to leave you with one final thought. The conflicts you face as a surgeon and physician leader are not obstacles to your career. They are your career, at least the leadership dimension of it. Every conflict is a leadership moment. Every difficult conversation is an opportunity to build trust, demonstrate integrity, and model the kind of professional behavior you want to see in your department. The physicians who become truly exceptional leaders are not the ones who avoid conflict or dominate it. They are the ones who engage with it skillfully, who approach every disagreement with both conviction and curiosity, who fight hard for what matters and yield gracefully when it does not, and who leave every conflict having strengthened the relationship rather than damaged it. That is the standard we are working toward, and with deliberate practice, it is entirely within your reach.

Thank you for your time today. Review the Thomas-Kilmann modes, practice the interest-based approach on your next scheduling or interdepartmental disagreement, and notice which mode you default to so you can begin expanding your range. I will see you in the next session.
