Welcome back. Today we are going to work through one of the most powerful cognitive tools available to you as a physician leader and entrepreneur. It is called first principles reasoning, and it has the potential to fundamentally reshape how you think about your career, your compensation, your practice structure, and the entire business of medicine. This is not a trendy productivity hack. It is a disciplined method of thinking that strips away inherited assumptions and rebuilds understanding from the ground up. By the end of this session, you will have a working framework you can apply immediately to the decisions you are facing right now.

SECTION ONE. FRAMEWORK INTRODUCTION.

Let me start with a distinction that will anchor everything we discuss today. There are two primary modes of reasoning that human beings use when they encounter a problem. The first is reasoning by analogy. The second is reasoning from first principles. Most people, most of the time, use analogical reasoning. They look at what others have done, they look at how things have been done before, and they pattern-match their way to a decision. If you ask a physician how to structure a new practice, they will almost always begin by describing what other practices look like. If you ask a hospital administrator how to set a compensation model, they will pull up MGMA benchmarks and replicate the median. This is reasoning by analogy. You are borrowing conclusions from other contexts and applying them to your own.

First principles reasoning operates differently. Instead of asking what has been done before, you ask what is fundamentally true. You decompose a problem into its most basic, irreducible elements, the things that cannot be broken down any further, and then you rebuild your understanding from those foundational truths. You do not accept inherited frameworks. You do not accept conventional wisdom. You interrogate everything until you reach bedrock, and then you construct upward from there.

The term itself comes from philosophy. Aristotle described a first principle as the first basis from which a thing is known. In physics, a first principle is a foundational proposition that cannot be deduced from any other proposition. In practical terms, it means identifying the assumptions embedded in any system, testing whether those assumptions are actually true, and discarding the ones that are not.

Elon Musk has become the most visible modern advocate for this approach. When he started SpaceX, the conventional wisdom was that rockets were prohibitively expensive. The cost of a rocket launch was roughly sixty-five million dollars. Everyone in the aerospace industry accepted this as a given. Musk did not accept it. He asked a different question. What are rockets actually made of? The answer was aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, titanium, copper, and carbon fiber. He then asked what those raw materials cost on the commodity market. The answer was roughly two percent of the price of a finished rocket. That gap between two percent and one hundred percent was not physics. It was not thermodynamics. It was not some immutable law of the universe. It was business practice, inherited process, institutional inertia, and margin stacking across a supply chain that had never been seriously questioned. By reasoning from the fundamental material costs upward rather than from the existing price downward, Musk was able to build rockets at a fraction of the industry standard cost.

Jeff Bezos applies the same thinking in a slightly different way. He asks what will not change. He reasons that customers will always want lower prices, faster delivery, and wider selection. Those are first principles of retail. Rather than trying to predict what will change, he builds his entire strategy around the things that are permanently, fundamentally true about what customers want. Everything else is variable. Everything else is open to reinvention.

Now, here is why this matters to you. Medicine is one of the most tradition-bound, analogy-driven industries on earth. The way physicians are employed, compensated, organized, and governed has been shaped far more by historical accident and institutional inertia than by any rigorous analysis of what actually works. And most physicians never question it. They accept the structure they inherit because it is all they have ever seen. First principles reasoning gives you the tools to see through the structure and ask whether it is serving you or whether you are serving it.

SECTION TWO. DEEP EXPLANATION.

Let me walk you through the mechanics of first principles reasoning in greater detail, because understanding how to actually do it is more important than understanding what it is.

The process has three stages. Stage one is identification. Stage two is deconstruction. Stage three is reconstruction.

In the identification stage, you name the problem or the assumption you want to examine. This sounds simple, but it is often the hardest part, because the most powerful assumptions are the ones you do not realize you are making. They are so deeply embedded in your worldview that they feel like facts rather than assumptions. For example, a physician might say, I need to find a good employer. That statement contains an enormous assumption, which is that employment is the correct structure for delivering your services. A physician might say, I need to negotiate a higher RVU rate. That statement assumes that RVU-based compensation is the right model. A physician might say, I need to find a hospital with a good call schedule. That assumes that hospital-based call is a necessary feature of your career. The identification stage is about surfacing these hidden assumptions and making them explicit so they can be examined.

In the deconstruction stage, you break the assumption apart. You ask why. You ask it repeatedly. You ask it until you reach something that is irreducibly true. This is sometimes called the Socratic method, and sometimes called the five whys technique, but the label does not matter. What matters is the discipline of refusing to accept any intermediate answer as final.

Let me give you an example. Start with the statement: Physicians should be employed by hospitals. Ask why. Because hospitals provide infrastructure, referral networks, and administrative support. Ask why those things require employment. Because hospitals will not invest in physicians who are not employees. Ask why. Because the traditional model assumes that physician loyalty must be purchased through employment contracts. Ask why that assumption exists. Because historically, hospitals competed for physicians by offering employment security, and physicians accepted because they did not want the risk of independent practice. Ask why physicians perceive independent practice as risky. Because medical training provides zero business education, so physicians default to the structure that feels safest, which is a paycheck from a large institution.

Now you have reached something closer to a first principle. The fundamental truth is not that physicians should be employed. The fundamental truth is that physicians lack business training, and that knowledge gap drives them toward employment as a default. Once you see that, the solution space opens up dramatically. Instead of negotiating a slightly better employment contract, you might invest in business education. You might hire a practice manager. You might structure a professional services agreement that gives you the infrastructure of employment without surrendering the autonomy and upside of independence. The first principle, the knowledge gap, suggests entirely different solutions than the surface-level assumption, the need for employment.

In the reconstruction stage, you build upward from your first principles. You design a solution that is optimized for the fundamental truths you have uncovered rather than for the conventional structures you have inherited. This is where creativity enters the process. Once you have cleared away the accumulated assumptions, you have an open field. You can ask what would I build if I were starting from scratch, knowing only the fundamental truths? This is exactly the question Musk asked about rockets, Bezos asked about retail, and that you should be asking about your career and your practice.

There is a critical nuance here that I want to emphasize. First principles reasoning does not mean ignoring evidence or dismissing expertise. It does not mean being contrarian for the sake of being contrarian. Sometimes the conventional answer is the correct answer. But you should arrive at that answer through your own reasoning, not through unreflective adoption of what everyone else does. The goal is not to be different. The goal is to be right. And the only way to know whether the conventional answer is right is to test it against first principles.

There is also an important psychological dimension to this. Reasoning by analogy is comfortable. It is safe. If you do what everyone else does and it fails, you cannot be blamed, because you followed best practices. First principles reasoning is uncomfortable. It requires you to stand alone with your conclusions. It requires intellectual courage. But the upside is that it gives you access to solutions that are invisible to everyone who is trapped inside the conventional framework.

SECTION THREE. PHYSICIAN APPLICATION.

Now let me bring this directly into your world. I want to walk through three specific domains where first principles reasoning can transform how you think about your career as a physician.

The first domain is compensation. The standard model for physician compensation in the United States is built on RVUs, relative value units, which are pegged to the Medicare physician fee schedule. Most employed physicians are paid a base salary plus an RVU-based productivity bonus, or they are paid purely on production. The benchmarks come from MGMA and SullivanCotter surveys, and the entire negotiation happens within a narrow band around the median for your specialty and region.

Apply first principles. What is a physician actually selling? You are selling specialized knowledge, technical skill, judgment under uncertainty, and the ability to manage risk. You are selling outcomes. You are not selling units of activity. The RVU system does not measure the value you create. It measures the volume of coded procedures and encounters you generate. These are fundamentally different things. A surgeon who performs a complex revision case that saves a patient from paralysis generates enormous value but may produce fewer RVUs than a surgeon who does four straightforward decompressions in the same time. The RVU system does not capture this. It was designed as a Medicare reimbursement tool, not as a measure of physician value.

Once you see this, you realize that negotiating a higher RVU rate is optimizing within a broken framework. The first principles question is not how do I get more dollars per RVU, but rather how do I capture the actual value I create. This leads to entirely different structures. It leads to thinking about gainsharing arrangements where you participate in the downstream savings your surgical decisions create. It leads to thinking about professional services agreements where you set your own rate for your time rather than accepting a conversion factor applied to a government billing code. It leads to thinking about ancillary revenue streams, about building equity in the infrastructure you use, about owning rather than renting your professional life.

The second domain is practice structure. The conventional model assumes that physicians either work for a hospital, work for a large group, or run an independent private practice. These are treated as the three options. First principles reasoning asks what you actually need to practice medicine effectively. You need patients, you need a place to see them, you need equipment, you need staff, you need malpractice coverage, and you need a billing and collections infrastructure. None of these things inherently require employment. Every single one of them can be contracted, leased, or built independently. The question is not which of the three boxes do I fit into, but rather what is the most efficient way to assemble these components given my specific goals, risk tolerance, and market conditions.

This is where the PSA model, the professional services agreement, becomes interesting from a first principles perspective. A PSA lets you function as an independent entity that contracts your services to a hospital or health system. You maintain your own corporate structure, your own tax advantages, your own retirement vehicles, and your own negotiating leverage. The hospital gets what it actually needs from you, which is your clinical availability and your surgical volume. You get what you actually need from the hospital, which is patients, operating rooms, and institutional infrastructure. Both parties get the essential elements without the baggage of a traditional employment relationship. This is a first principles solution. It is built from the fundamental needs of both parties rather than from the inherited template of physician employment.

The third domain is career trajectory. The conventional path for a physician is train, get employed, work for thirty years, retire. First principles reasoning asks what you actually want from your career. If the answer is financial security, intellectual stimulation, autonomy, and the ability to help patients, then you can ask what is the most efficient path to all four of those things simultaneously. Maybe it is traditional employment for a defined period to achieve specific financial milestones, like finishing loan forgiveness, while simultaneously building the knowledge and infrastructure for a transition to a more autonomous model. Maybe it is using your clinical expertise to generate income streams that do not require your physical presence in an operating room, like medical-legal consulting, expert witness work, or clinical advisory roles. Maybe it is building a practice in a location where the supply-demand dynamics are so favorable that you have genuine leverage to design your own terms.

The point is that first principles reasoning frees you from the tyranny of the default path. It lets you see your career as a design problem rather than a compliance problem. You are not filling in the blanks on a template someone else created. You are engineering a career from the ground up based on what is fundamentally true about your skills, your market, and your goals.

SECTION FOUR. PRACTICE EXERCISE.

I want you to do something concrete right now. I want you to take a specific decision you are currently facing, whether it is a contract negotiation, a practice opportunity, a financial decision, or a career move, and run it through the first principles process. Here is exactly how to do it.

Step one. Write down the decision as you currently understand it. For example, should I accept the offer from Hospital X at a salary of Y. Write it exactly as it exists in your mind right now.

Step two. Identify every assumption embedded in that framing. Should I accept implies that the offer is fixed and your only choice is yes or no. Hospital X implies that you have already narrowed your options to this specific institution. Salary of Y implies that the compensation structure is predetermined. List every assumption you can find. Push yourself to find at least five.

Step three. For each assumption, ask why it exists and whether it is fundamentally true. Is the offer really fixed? Most terms in a physician contract are negotiable, including structure, not just numbers. Have you really narrowed your options to one institution? What about other geographies, other models, other structures entirely? Is salary really the right compensation framework? What about production bonuses, equity, ancillary ownership, PSA structures, or hybrid models?

Step four. Identify the first principles underneath. What do you fundamentally need? Financial security, clinical volume, geographic fit, professional autonomy, family considerations, career development. These are your actual decision criteria. These are your bedrock truths.

Step five. Reconstruct the decision from your first principles. Instead of should I accept this offer, the question becomes what is the optimal structure for achieving financial security, clinical autonomy, and geographic fit given my current constraints. This is a completely different question, and it opens up a completely different solution space.

Do this exercise with a real decision. Do it on paper. Do not skip the writing. The act of writing forces precision, and precision is the engine of first principles reasoning. Vague thinking produces vague results. The more precisely you can articulate your assumptions and your foundational truths, the more powerful your conclusions will be.

I also want you to practice the deconstruction technique in everyday situations this week. When someone tells you something is the way it is, ask why. When you catch yourself saying that is just how it works, stop and examine it. When you are in a meeting and someone presents a plan that is modeled on what another department or institution has done, ask whether the underlying conditions are actually the same. Build the habit of questioning inherited frameworks. It will feel uncomfortable at first. It will occasionally make you unpopular. But it will sharpen your thinking in ways that compound over months and years.

SECTION FIVE. SYNTHESIS.

Let me bring this all together. First principles reasoning is not about being smarter than everyone else. It is about being more disciplined in your thinking. It is about refusing to outsource your reasoning to convention, to benchmarks, to what your colleagues are doing, or to what your predecessors did. It is about having the intellectual honesty to say I do not actually know why we do it this way, and the courage to investigate.

For physicians specifically, this skill is transformative because the medical profession is so deeply shaped by tradition, hierarchy, and institutional inertia. The way residency training is structured, the way compensation is modeled, the way practices are organized, the way hospitals relate to their medical staffs, all of these things evolved through historical contingency, not through optimization. They are the way they are because of decisions made decades ago under conditions that no longer exist. And yet most physicians accept them without question because they have never been taught to think any other way.

You now have a different way to think. You have a method. Identify the problem. Surface the hidden assumptions. Deconstruct each assumption by asking why until you reach bedrock. Identify the irreducible truths. Reconstruct a solution from those truths upward. Test it. Refine it. Implement it.

The physicians who will thrive in the coming decade are not the ones who negotiate marginally better terms within the existing system. They are the ones who see the system itself as a variable, who understand that employment models, compensation structures, practice frameworks, and career trajectories are all human constructions that can be deconstructed and rebuilt. First principles reasoning is the tool that makes that possible.

I want to leave you with one final thought. The greatest risk in your career is not making a bold move. The greatest risk is making no move at all because you never questioned whether the path you are on is actually the right one. Analogical reasoning keeps you on the path everyone else is walking. First principles reasoning lets you look at the terrain, look at your destination, and cut your own trail.

Think from the ground up. Question everything. Build what is true.

That is your assignment. I will see you in the next session.
