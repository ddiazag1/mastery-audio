Welcome back. Today we are going to dig into a topic that sits at the intersection of everything you do as a surgeon and everything you aspire to as a leader and entrepreneur. The topic is calculated risk and decision-making under uncertainty. Now, I chose those words deliberately. Not reckless risk. Not blind gambles. Calculated risk, meaning risk that has been weighed, measured, bounded, and deliberately accepted because the expected payoff justifies the exposure. And decision-making under uncertainty, meaning the disciplined process of making consequential choices when you do not and cannot have complete information.

You already do this in the operating room. Every craniotomy, every spinal fusion, every decision to observe rather than operate is a decision made under uncertainty. You never have perfect imaging. You never have a guarantee of how the tissue will behave. You never know with certainty how a patient will heal. And yet you decide. You act. You move forward with calculated confidence, not because you have eliminated risk, but because you have assessed it honestly and judged the expected outcome to be worth the exposure. What I want to do today is take that same cognitive discipline and apply it to the professional and entrepreneurial decisions that will shape the trajectory of your career. We are going to cover four frameworks: expected value thinking, asymmetric bets, the one-way door versus two-way door framework from Jeff Bezos, and pre-mortem analysis. And then we are going to apply all four to the kinds of decisions you are actually facing: evaluating a job change, deciding whether to sign or not sign a contract, starting a side venture while employed, and timing a transition to independent practice.

Let us begin with expected value thinking, because this is the foundation on which all calculated risk assessment is built. Expected value is a concept borrowed from probability theory, but you do not need to be a mathematician to use it. The idea is simple and profound. When you face a decision with uncertain outcomes, you estimate the probability of each possible outcome, multiply each probability by the value of that outcome, whether positive or negative, and then sum them all up. The result is the expected value of that decision. It represents, on average, what you would gain or lose if you made that decision many times over.

Here is why this matters. Most people evaluate decisions based on the most likely single outcome, or worse, based on whichever scenario matches their temperament. Optimists fixate on the upside. Pessimists fixate on the downside. Neither is thinking clearly. Expected value thinking forces you to consider the full distribution of outcomes and weight them appropriately.

Let me give you a concrete example. Imagine you are considering leaving a stable position paying seven hundred thousand to pursue independent practice. You estimate a sixty percent chance of generating one point two million, a twenty-five percent chance of eight hundred thousand, and a fifteen percent chance of only four hundred thousand while you build volume. The downside scenario feels terrifying when you have a mortgage, loans, and a family. But do the math. Sixty percent times one point two million is seven hundred twenty thousand. Twenty-five percent times eight hundred thousand is two hundred thousand. Fifteen percent times four hundred thousand is sixty thousand. The sum is nine hundred eighty thousand, which is two hundred eighty thousand more than the guaranteed salary. That does not automatically mean you should move. But expected value thinking gives you a clear-eyed starting point and prevents the common error of overweighting the worst case simply because it feels scary.

There is an important refinement here called utility theory. A dollar is not always worth the same to every person. If you have two million in savings, a bad year is uncomfortable but survivable. If you have negative net worth and four children, that same shortfall might mean you cannot make your mortgage payment. The subjective value of money is not linear. The pain of losing a dollar you need is greater than the pleasure of gaining one you do not. So when you run expected value calculations, adjust for your personal circumstances. The math might favor a move, but if the downside scenario is catastrophic for your specific situation, the expected utility might be negative even when the dollar value is positive. A surgeon with zero savings and three hundred thousand in loans has a very different risk tolerance than one with a million in liquid assets and a high-earning spouse. Expected value thinking gives you the framework. Utility adjustments make it personal.

Now let us move to the second framework: asymmetric bets. This is one of the most powerful concepts in entrepreneurial thinking, and it is dramatically underutilized by physicians. An asymmetric bet is a decision where the potential upside is vastly larger than the potential downside. The key insight is that you do not need to be right most of the time if the payoff when you are right is large enough relative to the cost when you are wrong.

Think about it this way. If you invest five thousand dollars and two hundred hours over six months to explore a side venture, and the venture fails, you have lost five thousand dollars and some evenings and weekends. That is the downside. It is bounded. It is survivable. It is, frankly, negligible relative to your annual income. But if the venture succeeds, it might generate fifty thousand, a hundred thousand, or eventually five hundred thousand dollars per year in additional income. The ratio of potential upside to defined downside is ten to one, twenty to one, a hundred to one. That is an asymmetric bet, and rational actors should take asymmetric bets whenever they can find them, even if the probability of success is relatively low.

The reason physicians rarely think this way is that surgical training conditions you for a different risk profile. In the operating room, the downside of failure can be catastrophic and irreversible. So you are trained to be extraordinarily risk-averse, and that bias is absolutely appropriate in clinical medicine. But it becomes a liability when you carry it unexamined into career decisions, because most career and business decisions have bounded downsides and unbounded upsides. The worst case of starting a medical-legal consulting practice is that you spend some time and go back to where you were. The worst case of negotiating a contract is that you sign what was originally offered. These are not surgical risks. The downside is almost always recoverable.

So the discipline is to explicitly bound your downside before deciding. What is the absolute worst that can happen? Can I survive it? Can I recover? If yes and the upside is meaningfully large, you are looking at an asymmetric bet, and the rational move is to take it.

Now let us move to the third framework, which comes from Jeff Bezos and has become one of the most widely cited decision-making heuristics in business. Bezos distinguishes between what he calls Type One and Type Two decisions, or one-way doors and two-way doors. A one-way door is a decision that is irreversible or nearly so. Once you walk through, you cannot walk back. A two-way door is a decision that is reversible. You can walk through, look around, and if you do not like what you see, you can walk back through the door to where you started.

Bezos argues that most people make a critical error: they treat two-way door decisions as if they were one-way doors. They agonize, form committees, request more data, delay, and seek consensus. By the time they act, the opportunity has often passed. Most decisions in business are two-way doors, and the appropriate way to handle them is to decide quickly with about seventy percent of the information you wish you had, move through the door, and adjust.

One-way door decisions deserve the opposite treatment: deep analysis, broad input, and patience. Signing a ten-year contract with a punitive non-compete is a one-way door. Signing a one-year contract with a ninety-day termination clause is a two-way door. Moving to a new city is closer to a two-way door than most people realize, because you can move back.

The practical discipline: before any major decision, explicitly classify it. Two-way door? Decide faster. You are running an experiment, not making a permanent commitment. One-way door? Slow down. Gather more information. Model the scenarios. Sleep on it more than once.

Surgeons struggle here because surgical decisions can be one-way doors with life-and-death consequences, so you develop a cognitive habit of treating every important decision as irreversible. You carry that caution into contract negotiations and career transitions where it does not belong. The result is paralysis. You stay in a suboptimal arrangement for years because leaving feels irreversible, when most career moves are two-way doors. If you take a new position and it is not what was promised, you can leave. If you start a side venture and it flops, you shut it down. These are not craniotomies. You can undo them.

Now let us address the fourth and final framework: pre-mortem analysis. This concept was developed by psychologist Gary Klein, and it is one of the most effective debiasing tools available for high-stakes decision-making. Here is how it works. Before you commit to a decision, you imagine that you have already made the decision, that a year has passed, and that the decision turned out to be a disaster. Then you ask yourself: why did it fail? What went wrong? You write down every reason you can think of. You force yourself to generate a narrative of failure.

This may sound pessimistic, but it is actually the opposite. It is a disciplined form of optimism. By surfacing the failure modes in advance, you give yourself the opportunity to mitigate them before they happen. It counteracts the planning fallacy, which is the well-documented human tendency to underestimate risks and overestimate the likelihood of success once we have committed to a course of action. Daniel Kahneman, who won the Nobel Prize for his work on cognitive biases, has called the pre-mortem the single most effective debiasing technique he has encountered. The reason is that it legitimizes dissent. Once you have decided to do something, there is enormous social and psychological pressure to suppress doubts. The pre-mortem gives you explicit permission to voice those doubts and, more importantly, to act on them.

Let me show you how a pre-mortem works in practice. Say you are considering leaving your current group to join a hospital-employed position in another city. You have tentatively decided to do it. Now conduct the pre-mortem. It is eighteen months from now. The move has been a disaster. Why? Force yourself to generate at least seven reasons. Maybe the surgical volume was overstated and you are not generating enough RVUs to hit your compensation targets. Maybe the call coverage arrangement was worse than described and you are taking call every third night. Maybe the administration changed and the new leadership does not support your subspecialty. Maybe your spouse is miserable in the new city and it is straining your marriage. Maybe the cost of living was higher than expected and the net financial gain evaporated. Maybe you lost your referral network and it is taking longer than expected to rebuild. Maybe the non-compete clause in the new contract means you cannot leave without relocating again.

Now look at that list. Some of those risks are mitigable. You can request volume guarantees with a floor salary. You can negotiate the call schedule before signing. You can research cost of living with precision. You can negotiate the non-compete before signing or make its modification a condition of acceptance. Some risks are not mitigable but are at least now visible, which means you can build contingency plans. And some risks might be severe enough that they cause you to reconsider the decision entirely, which is also valuable. The pre-mortem does not tell you whether to make the decision. It tells you what to watch out for and what to protect against.

Now let us bring all four frameworks together and apply them to the specific decisions that physicians face at inflection points in their careers.

First, evaluating a job change. Start with expected value thinking. Model the financial outcomes of staying versus leaving across multiple scenarios, not just the best case. Include benefits, retirement, malpractice, and quality of life factors like call burden. Then look for asymmetry. Is the upside of the new position dramatically larger than the downside, or are you trading one equivalent situation for another with more uncertainty? Classify the decision. If the new contract has a short termination clause and a manageable non-compete, it is a two-way door and you can move faster. If it locks you in for three years with a hundred-mile non-compete and a repayment clause on your signing bonus, it is a one-way door, and you should deliberate carefully. Finally, run a pre-mortem. Imagine the move has failed. Why? Mitigate every risk you can before signing.

Second, signing versus not signing a contract. This is often framed as binary, but between signing and not signing, there is negotiating. And negotiation is itself an asymmetric bet. The downside of asking for better terms is almost zero. The worst they say is no, and you sign what was originally offered. The upside can be enormous: better compensation, a shorter non-compete, a guaranteed minimum income, a termination without cause clause. Physicians chronically under-negotiate because they are conflict-averse and overestimate the social cost of asking. The downside of negotiating is a mildly uncomfortable conversation. The upside is potentially hundreds of thousands of dollars over the life of the contract. Take the bet.

Third, starting a side venture while employed. This is the quintessential asymmetric bet for physicians. The downside is bounded: some time, some modest capital, some cognitive bandwidth. The upside is potentially transformational: a new income stream, diversification away from clinical dependence, and optionality for the future. Almost by definition, a side venture is a two-way door. You can start a medical-legal consulting practice, a content business, or an advisory role with minimal commitment and scale it up if it works or shut it down if it does not. There is no contract. There is no non-compete. What if it fails? You lost some evenings. You learned something about business. You go back to clinical work. That is a recoverable failure, and the lessons alone have compounding value.

Fourth, timing a transition to independent practice. This decision combines all four frameworks most intensely. Is going independent truly irreversible? No. If it does not work, you can return to employment. The market for surgeons is not going to disappear. But the transaction costs of walking back are higher: money spent on setup, credentialing, and infrastructure, potentially a damaged relationship with a former employer. So it is a two-way door with friction, deserving more deliberation than a low-friction two-way door but less agonizing than a true one-way door.

Expected value thinking applied to timing reveals something counterintuitive. Physicians focus on the risk of financial failure, but rarely calculate the opportunity cost of waiting. Every year you remain in a suboptimal employed position, earning less than your market value and building equity in someone else's practice, is a year of foregone income. Over five years, that difference can exceed a million dollars. Delay feels like safety. Expected value thinking reveals it for what it is: an invisible, compounding cost. And if you structure the transition correctly, with adequate savings, a signed facility agreement, established referral relationships, and a fallback plan, the downside is bounded while the upside is unbounded. You capture your full production value, build equity, and control your strategic direction.

The pre-mortem is most valuable here. Before committing, imagine it has failed spectacularly. Maybe volume projections were based on referral relationships that evaporated when you left. Maybe overhead was higher than modeled. Maybe a competitor captured your market share. Each failure mode is addressable in advance. You can validate referral commitments, get real quotes on malpractice and overhead, model conservative scenarios, and diversify your facility relationships. The pre-mortem does not prevent failure. It prevents preventable failure. And that distinction is everything.

Now I want to give you a practice exercise that integrates all four frameworks. This week, I want you to identify one decision you are currently facing or anticipate facing in the next six months. It does not have to be a career-defining decision. It can be anything with meaningful consequences and genuine uncertainty. A contract decision, a financial commitment, a practice change, a leadership opportunity, anything. And I want you to work through it using the four-framework protocol.

Step one: calculate the expected value. Identify the possible outcomes, estimate their probabilities, assign dollar values or utility values to each, and compute the weighted sum. Do this for both the "act" and "do not act" scenarios. Which one has the higher expected value?

Step two: identify the asymmetry. What is the maximum downside? What is the realistic upside? What is the ratio? Is this a high-asymmetry bet where bounded risk opens the door to outsized reward? Or is this a symmetric gamble where you are risking as much as you stand to gain?

Step three: classify the door. Is this decision reversible or irreversible? If reversible, how much friction is involved in reversing it? Based on your classification, how much information do you need before deciding? If it is a two-way door, can you decide this week with seventy percent confidence? If it is a one-way door, what additional information would move you from seventy to ninety percent confidence, and how long will it take to get?

Step four: run the pre-mortem. Imagine the decision has been made and it has failed. Generate at least seven specific reasons for the failure. For each one, categorize it as mitigable, monitorable, or uncontrollable. For the mitigable risks, identify a specific action you can take before committing. For the monitorable risks, define a trigger point, a specific signal that would tell you things are going wrong and prompt you to invoke your contingency plan. For the uncontrollable risks, assess whether they are survivable and what your recovery path would look like.

Write all of this down. Not in your head. On paper or on a screen. The act of writing forces clarity that mental rehearsal cannot achieve. When you are done, you will have a decision brief that is more rigorous than ninety-nine percent of the career decisions physicians make. And you will have built the muscle memory for a decision-making process that you can deploy again and again for every consequential choice you face for the rest of your career.

Let me bring this all together. The central thesis of today's session is that risk is not something to be avoided. Risk is something to be understood, measured, bounded, and deliberately accepted when the expected value justifies it. The surgeon who refuses to operate because there is a five percent complication rate is not being careful. They are being negligent, because the patient's condition will worsen without intervention. The expected value of operating is positive, and the downside has been bounded by training, technique, and preparation. The same logic applies to your career and business decisions. The physician who refuses to negotiate, refuses to explore side income, refuses to evaluate alternative practice models, and refuses to consider independence is not being prudent. They are paying an invisible tax on inaction, a tax that compounds year after year in the form of foregone income, foregone equity, and foregone autonomy.

Expected value thinking gives you the math. Asymmetric bet thinking gives you the courage. The one-way door versus two-way door framework gives you the appropriate speed. And pre-mortem analysis gives you the protection. Together, these four frameworks constitute a complete decision-making system for navigating uncertainty with discipline and confidence. You already possess the cognitive ability to use them. You exercise comparable judgment every time you stand at an operating table and decide how to proceed. What I am asking you to do is take that same rigor, that same analytical discipline, and apply it to the decisions that will determine not just your patients' outcomes, but your own.

Thank you for your time today. In your next session, we will build on this foundation and explore negotiation strategy, specifically how to apply these risk frameworks to the art of negotiating compensation, contract terms, and professional arrangements. Until then, work through the four-framework exercise, write down your analysis, and notice how much more clearly you see a decision when you have systematically dismantled the fog of uncertainty around it.
