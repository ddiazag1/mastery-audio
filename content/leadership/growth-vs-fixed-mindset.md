Welcome back. Today we are going to spend time with one of the most influential ideas in modern psychology and one that has particular relevance for anyone who has gone through surgical training: the distinction between a growth mindset and a fixed mindset. This framework comes primarily from the work of Carol Dweck, a psychologist at Stanford, and while you may have heard the terms before, I want us to go deeper than the popular summary. I want you to understand the original research, its nuances, its legitimate criticisms, and most importantly, how it applies to your daily work as a surgeon, a leader, and someone who is still developing mastery at the highest level.

FRAMEWORK INTRODUCTION

Let me start with the core idea. Dweck's research, spanning several decades, centers on a deceptively simple question: what do people believe about the nature of their own abilities? She found that people generally fall along a spectrum anchored by two implicit theories. On one end is what she calls an entity theory, or fixed mindset. This is the belief that your intelligence, your talent, and your fundamental capabilities are essentially static traits. You have a certain amount of them, and that amount does not change in any meaningful way. On the other end is an incremental theory, or growth mindset. This is the belief that your abilities are fundamentally malleable, that through effort, strategy, and learning, you can develop and expand your capabilities over time.

Now, this might sound like a trivial distinction, but the research shows that these implicit beliefs have cascading effects on nearly everything that matters for performance: how you interpret effort, how you respond to failure, what kind of challenges you seek out, how you handle feedback, and ultimately how far you develop over the course of a career.

Here is the key mechanism. If you hold a fixed mindset, then effort becomes threatening. Think about it. If you believe that ability is innate and static, then having to work hard at something is evidence that you lack natural talent. Struggle becomes a signal of inadequacy rather than a necessary part of growth. Conversely, if you hold a growth mindset, effort is not a threat. It is the very mechanism by which you get better. Struggle is not a sign that you are deficient. It is a sign that you are at the edge of your current ability, which is exactly where learning happens.

This has enormous implications for how people respond to setbacks. In a fixed mindset, failure is an identity statement. If you failed, it means you are not smart enough, not talented enough, not cut out for this work. The natural response is to avoid situations where failure is possible, to stick with what you already know, to protect your self-image at all costs. In a growth mindset, failure is information. It tells you where your understanding or skill is incomplete. It points you toward what needs to change. The natural response is to analyze what went wrong, adjust your approach, and try again.

DEEP EXPLANATION

Let me walk you through the research that established this framework, because the details matter. Dweck's foundational studies were done with children, but the principles have been replicated across ages and domains, including professional contexts. In one classic set of experiments, children were given a series of puzzles. After completing the first set successfully, they were praised in one of two ways. One group was told something like, "You did really well. You must be very smart." The other group was told, "You did really well. You must have worked really hard." Then both groups were offered a choice: try a harder set of puzzles where they might struggle but would learn a lot, or try another set at the same difficulty level.

The results were striking. Children praised for being smart, who had been given a fixed mindset cue, overwhelmingly chose the easier puzzles. They did not want to risk their "smart" label. Children praised for effort chose the harder puzzles. They saw difficulty as an opportunity rather than a threat. But the effects did not stop there. When both groups were subsequently given a very difficult set of puzzles, the "smart" group showed a sharp decline in performance and reported not enjoying the task. Many of them even lied about their scores afterward. The "effort" group maintained their performance levels, reported enjoying the challenge, and were honest about their results.

This is not just about children and puzzles. Think about what this means for any high-stakes, high-skill domain. The type of praise you receive, the way achievement is framed around you, shapes whether you approach challenges with curiosity or avoidance.

Now let me add layers to this. Dweck's work also shows that mindset affects how people interpret the success of others. In a fixed mindset, someone else's success is threatening because it implies a comparison of fixed traits, and if they are better than you, there is nothing you can do about it. In a growth mindset, someone else's success can be inspiring and instructive. You can study what they did, learn from their approach, and incorporate their strategies.

Mindset also shapes how people engage with feedback. Fixed mindset individuals tend to focus on feedback about their current ability level, seeking validation and avoiding critique. Growth mindset individuals seek feedback about how to improve, even when that feedback is uncomfortable. They are more likely to pay attention to corrective information because they see it as useful rather than as a judgment of their worth.

There is also important work on what Dweck calls "false growth mindset," which is worth understanding. This is when someone intellectually endorses the idea of growth mindset but does not actually operate from it. They say the right things about effort and learning, but when they hit a genuine setback, they revert to fixed mindset thinking. This happens more than people realize, and it is one of the reasons the concept has been oversimplified in popular culture. True growth mindset is not about positive thinking or simply believing you can do anything. It is about a genuine orientation toward learning that persists even when things are going badly.

Let me also address the legitimate criticisms, because intellectual honesty demands it. Several replication studies have found smaller effect sizes than Dweck's original work suggested. A large-scale study published in 2018, involving over sixty thousand students, found that while growth mindset interventions had statistically significant effects, they were modest. Critics have also pointed out that the framework can be used to blame individuals for systemic problems. Telling someone to "just have a growth mindset" when they face structural barriers to advancement is not helpful and can be harmful. The environment matters enormously. A growth mindset in a toxic, unsupportive environment will not magically overcome institutional dysfunction.

That said, even the critical research generally supports the core insight: implicit beliefs about the nature of ability do influence behavior, and interventions that shift those beliefs, when combined with a supportive environment, can improve outcomes. The framework is not a silver bullet, but it is a useful lens, especially when applied thoughtfully and in context.

There is one more dimension I want to highlight before we move to application: the relationship between mindset and deliberate practice. Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance shows that what separates elite performers from good ones is not innate talent but the quantity and quality of deliberate practice they engage in. Deliberate practice requires precisely the orientation that growth mindset supports: willingness to work at the edge of your ability, tolerance for struggle and error, responsiveness to feedback, and sustained effort over long periods. A fixed mindset undermines every one of those requirements. This is why mindset is not just a feel-good concept. It has direct implications for the rate and ceiling of skill development.

PHYSICIAN APPLICATION

Now let us bring this into the operating room and the clinic. Surgical training is, in many ways, a natural laboratory for studying mindset effects. Consider the structure of residency. You start as someone who cannot be trusted to do almost anything independently. Over years, through thousands of repetitions, corrections, and graduated responsibility, you develop the ability to perform complex procedures autonomously. That entire trajectory depends on a growth mindset, whether or not anyone uses that term. The residents who thrive are the ones who treat every case as a learning opportunity, who welcome attending feedback even when it stings, and who respond to a complication by asking what they can do differently next time rather than by questioning whether they belong in surgery.

But here is where it gets interesting and where I want you to think carefully about your own experience. Surgical culture has historically been dominated by fixed mindset signals. Think about the language. "He's a natural." "She just has great hands." "Some people have it and some people don't." This framing treats surgical skill as an innate trait rather than a developed capability. And it has consequences. When a resident who has been told they are "a natural" encounters a case that goes badly, they are more likely to experience that as an identity crisis rather than as a learning opportunity. When a surgeon who believes ability is fixed encounters a new technique or technology, they are more likely to resist it because learning it would require visible struggle, which threatens their self-concept as an expert.

Think about how you respond to complications. Every surgeon has them. The question is what you do with them. A fixed mindset response is to minimize the complication, blame external factors, avoid discussing it, or carry shame about it privately. A growth mindset response is to analyze the complication systematically, present it at morbidity and mortality conference without defensiveness, identify specific technical or decision-making factors that contributed, and develop a plan to mitigate similar events in the future. The first response protects ego but blocks learning. The second response is uncomfortable but drives improvement.

This extends to how you lead your team. If you hold a fixed mindset about others, you will tend to sort people into categories: good nurses, bad nurses, strong residents, weak residents. You will invest your energy in the people you have already identified as talented and write off the ones you have categorized as lacking. A growth mindset about your team means believing that most people can improve with the right support, feedback, and expectations. It means investing in developing the struggling resident rather than simply labeling them. It means giving your scrub tech specific, actionable feedback rather than just sighing in frustration when they hand you the wrong instrument.

Here is a practical distinction that matters enormously in surgical leadership. When you give feedback, are you praising or criticizing the person, or are you praising or criticizing the process? "You're a great surgeon" is a fixed mindset compliment. It sounds good, but it creates pressure to maintain the label and fear of disproving it. "Your tissue handling in that case was excellent, and the way you adjusted your approach when you encountered the adhesions showed strong intraoperative decision-making" is a growth mindset compliment. It identifies specific behaviors and strategies, which reinforces them and makes them repeatable. The same applies to criticism. "You're not ready for this" is fixed mindset. "Your knot-tying speed needs work, and here is how I want you to practice it" is growth mindset. One labels the person. The other gives them a path forward.

I also want you to think about this in the context of your career beyond training. Many surgeons settle into a fixed mindset after they complete residency and fellowship. They have their techniques, their preferred approaches, their established practice patterns, and they stop actively seeking to develop. They attend conferences but do not change their practice. They read journals but filter out anything that challenges their current approach. This is not because they are lazy or unintelligent. It is because the fixed mindset tells them that they have already become what they are going to become, and any struggle to learn something new would be evidence of inadequacy rather than growth.

The surgeons who continue to develop throughout their careers are the ones who maintain a growth orientation. They seek out proctored cases when they want to learn a new approach. They review their own outcome data critically. They ask colleagues for feedback. They stay genuinely curious about better ways to do things. They accept that being an attending does not mean being finished.

PRACTICE EXERCISE

Here is what I want you to do over the next week. This is a mindset audit, and it has three parts.

First, pay attention to your self-talk after difficult cases. When something does not go as planned, notice the narrative that runs through your head. Is it about who you are, or is it about what happened and what you can learn? Write down the specific thoughts. Do not judge them. Just notice them. You might be surprised to find fixed mindset language in places where you thought you had a growth orientation.

Second, monitor how you give feedback to others. Every time you give a compliment or a correction to a resident, a nurse, a PA, anyone on your team, notice whether you are speaking about the person or about the behavior. "You're great" versus "That was a well-executed closure." "You need to be better" versus "Here is the specific thing I want you to work on." At the end of each day, reflect on the ratio. How much of your feedback was person-oriented versus process-oriented?

Third, identify one area of your practice where you have been avoiding challenge. Maybe it is a technique you have been meaning to learn but keep putting off. Maybe it is a type of case you refer out when you could be developing the skill to handle it. Maybe it is a leadership responsibility you have been ducking because you are not sure you would be good at it. Whatever it is, I want you to articulate it clearly and then ask yourself: what is the belief that is keeping me from engaging with this challenge? If the answer is some version of "I'm just not someone who is good at that," you have found a fixed mindset belief that is constraining your development. Then make a concrete plan for one small step you can take to begin developing in that area. Not a massive commitment. Just one step that moves you from avoidance toward engagement.

This exercise is not about forcing yourself to feel a certain way. It is about building awareness. Mindset is not a binary switch you flip once. It is a practice, an ongoing orientation that requires monitoring and maintenance. Even Dweck herself has said that everyone has a mixture of fixed and growth mindset beliefs, and the goal is not to eliminate fixed mindset thinking entirely but to notice it, understand its triggers, and choose to respond from a growth orientation when it matters most.

SYNTHESIS

Let me pull this together. Dweck's growth versus fixed mindset framework, while sometimes oversimplified in popular culture, offers a genuinely useful lens for understanding your own behavior and the behavior of those you lead. The core insight is that your implicit beliefs about the nature of ability shape how you engage with effort, failure, and feedback, and those engagements in turn shape how much you develop over time. In a domain like surgery, where the learning curve is steep, the stakes are high, and the need for continuous improvement never ends, the difference between a fixed and growth orientation is not academic. It is practical and consequential.

The fixed mindset tells you that struggle means inadequacy, that failure reveals your limits, that other people's success diminishes you, and that feedback is judgment. The growth mindset tells you that struggle means you are at the edge of your current ability, that failure is data, that other people's success is a source of learning, and that feedback is a gift even when it is uncomfortable.

But remember the nuances. Growth mindset is not about blind optimism or ignoring real constraints. It is about maintaining an accurate belief that you can develop and improve through focused effort and good strategy, even when that development is difficult. It is also not an individual solution to systemic problems. The environment you create as a leader matters as much as the mindset you personally hold. If you tell your team to embrace a growth mindset but then punish them for mistakes, you are sending contradictory signals. The environment must support the mindset.

As a surgeon, you have already demonstrated growth mindset in action. You spent years being bad at things that you are now good at. You tolerated the discomfort of learning under pressure. You absorbed criticism from attendings and used it to get better. The question is whether you continue to operate from that orientation now that you have achieved a level of competence that makes it comfortable to stop. The best surgeons, the ones who are still getting better ten and twenty years into practice, are the ones who never stop treating their skill as something under development.

Finally, think about your role as a mindset architect for your team. Every time you react to a complication, give feedback, respond to a question, or model your own learning process, you are shaping the implicit beliefs of the people around you. When you talk about what you learned from a case rather than just what you did well, you normalize the idea that even experienced surgeons are still learning. When you praise specific techniques rather than innate talent, you reinforce the connection between effort and excellence. When you respond to a team member's mistake with coaching rather than condemnation, you create an environment where people are willing to take the risks necessary for growth rather than playing it safe to avoid blame.

This is leadership at its most fundamental level. You are not just managing tasks and schedules. You are shaping the beliefs that determine whether the people around you are growing or stagnating. And the first person whose beliefs you need to manage is yourself.

That is our session on growth versus fixed mindset. In our next session, we will explore psychological safety in team environments, which connects directly to everything we discussed today. The mindset you hold about your team determines the environment you create, and the environment you create determines the mindset your team can sustain. Until then, run the mindset audit. Notice your self-talk, monitor your feedback, and identify your avoidance. Awareness is the first step toward choice, and choice is the first step toward growth.
