Welcome back. Today we are going to explore one of the most important and, frankly, most underestimated dimensions of surgical leadership: team building and psychological safety. This is not soft material. This is not the kind of feel-good organizational theory that looks nice on a hospital brochure but has no bearing on outcomes. The research is clear, and the stakes in our world could not be higher. When surgical teams lack psychological safety, people stay silent. When people stay silent, errors go unrecognized. When errors go unrecognized, patients suffer. That is the chain of causation, and it runs through your operating room every single day.

The concept we are going to anchor this entire session around comes from the work of Amy Edmondson, a professor at Harvard Business School who has spent more than two decades studying teams in high-stakes environments, including hospitals. Her foundational insight is deceptively simple: psychological safety is a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Notice the precision of that definition. It does not mean comfort. It does not mean the absence of conflict. It does not mean everyone is nice to each other. It means that when a team member takes a risk, when they speak up about a concern, admit a mistake, ask a question that might seem naive, or challenge a plan proposed by someone more senior, they believe they will not be punished, humiliated, or marginalized for doing so.

Edmondson originally developed this concept while studying medication errors in hospital units. She expected to find that the best-performing units would have the lowest error rates. Instead, she found the opposite. The highest-performing units reported more errors, not fewer. The explanation was psychological safety. In units where nurses and pharmacists felt safe to speak up, errors were caught, reported, and addressed. In units where the culture was punitive or hierarchical, the same errors occurred but were buried, hidden, or never verbalized. The errors did not disappear. They simply became invisible to leadership. That finding should make every surgeon pause and ask a difficult question: How many concerns are my team members not voicing because they do not feel safe doing so?

This brings us to the deep explanation. Let us examine the architecture of psychological safety and what the research tells us about how it functions in teams.

Google's Project Aristotle, one of the largest studies of team effectiveness ever conducted, analyzed over one hundred and eighty teams across the organization to determine what separates high-performing teams from mediocre ones. The researchers examined every variable they could measure: team composition, individual intelligence, personality types, educational backgrounds, social connections outside of work. What they found was that who was on the team mattered far less than how the team worked together. And the single most important factor, the one that predicted team success above all others, was psychological safety. Teams with high psychological safety outperformed teams composed of individually brilliant people who lacked it. This is a finding that should resonate deeply with anyone who has worked in surgery, because we have all seen technically excellent teams fall apart under pressure when communication breaks down.

Project Aristotle identified five key dynamics of effective teams, and they form a hierarchy. At the base is psychological safety. Above it sits dependability, the confidence that team members will complete their work on time and at a high standard. Then comes structure and clarity, meaning everyone understands their role and the plan. Then meaning, the sense that the work matters personally to each individual. And finally, impact, the belief that the team's work makes a difference. But here is the critical point. None of the higher-level dynamics function well without the foundation of psychological safety. You cannot have dependability if people are afraid to admit when they are falling behind. You cannot have structure and clarity if no one dares to ask clarifying questions. The entire pyramid rests on the willingness to be vulnerable with one another.

Now let us examine the specific mechanisms through which psychological safety operates in teams. Edmondson identifies three core behaviors that signal psychological safety within a group. The first is voice. Team members speak up with ideas, concerns, and questions. The second is experimentation. The team tries new approaches without excessive fear of failure. The third is reflection. The team regularly pauses to examine its own processes and outcomes. When all three of these behaviors are present, a team becomes a learning system. It improves continuously. When any of them are suppressed, the team becomes brittle, repeating the same patterns and accumulating unaddressed problems.

The opposite of psychological safety is not danger in the physical sense. It is social threat. It is the fear of being seen as ignorant, incompetent, negative, or disruptive. In surgical environments, this fear is amplified by steep authority gradients. The attending surgeon stands at the top of a hierarchy that includes fellows, residents, nurses, scrub techs, anesthesiologists, and support staff. Every one of those individuals is making real-time judgments about whether speaking up is worth the social risk. A scrub tech who notices the surgeon picking up the wrong instrument. A circulating nurse who questions whether the timeout was properly completed. A junior resident who thinks the anatomy does not look right. Each of these moments is a decision point, and in the absence of psychological safety, silence wins.

Research in aviation safety has demonstrated this phenomenon clearly. The concept of Crew Resource Management, or CRM, was developed after investigators found that a significant percentage of airline accidents were caused not by mechanical failure or pilot incompetence, but by failures of communication within the cockpit. First officers who noticed problems but did not speak up because the captain had not invited their input. Flight engineers who deferred to authority even when they had critical information. The parallels to surgical teams are exact. The operating room is our cockpit, and the authority gradient between the surgeon and the rest of the team can be just as steep and just as dangerous.

Let us now turn to physician application and discuss how to build psychological safety in the environments where you actually work.

The first and most important tool is the surgical briefing. A good briefing is not a perfunctory recitation of the patient's name and procedure. It is a deliberate invitation for every team member to participate in the plan and raise concerns. Before the first incision, every person in the room should understand the plan, anticipate potential complications, and know that their input is expected, not merely tolerated. The best briefings I have observed include explicit language such as, "I want everyone to speak up if something does not look right at any point during this case. If you see something, say something, and I promise I will listen." That kind of language, spoken by the person with the most authority in the room, is a powerful signal. It resets the default from silence to voice.

The second tool is the surgical debriefing. This is the most underutilized mechanism for team learning in operative surgery. After the case, before the team disperses, take sixty to ninety seconds to ask three questions. What went well? What could we improve? Was there anything that felt unsafe or unclear? That third question is the one that matters most for psychological safety, and it is the one most surgeons never ask. When you ask it, and when you respond non-defensively to whatever answer you receive, you are building trust at a rate that no team-building retreat or motivational poster will ever match.

The third tool is your response to mistakes and near-misses. This is where psychological safety is either built or destroyed, and it happens in real time. When a team member makes an error, your immediate response sets the tone for every future interaction. If you respond with anger, sarcasm, or blame, you have just taught every person in that room that the cost of admitting a mistake is humiliation. They will not make that mistake again in front of you, but they also will not tell you about the next one. If instead you respond with curiosity, if you say something like, "Thank you for catching that. Let us figure out how to prevent it next time," you have just taught everyone that honesty is valued more than perfection. That single moment of response is worth more than a hundred policy memos about patient safety culture.

The fourth tool is what Edmondson calls "framing the work." This means explicitly naming the uncertainty, complexity, and interdependence of what you are doing together. When you say to your team, "This is a complex case and I am going to need everyone's eyes on this," you are doing two things simultaneously. You are accurately describing the reality of surgical work, and you are lowering the threshold for speaking up. You are telling your team that vigilance is expected, not because they need to catch your mistakes, but because the work itself demands collective attention.

The fifth tool, and this one is specific to training environments, is creating what I call graduated disclosure. When you are working with residents, one of the most powerful things you can do is model vulnerability yourself. Share a case where you made an error and what you learned from it. Describe a moment where you were uncertain and how you managed that uncertainty. When the attending surgeon, the person with the most experience and the most authority, demonstrates that fallibility is a normal part of being human, it gives every junior member of the team permission to be honest about their own limitations. This does not undermine your authority. It strengthens it. Because authority that depends on the illusion of infallibility is fragile. Authority that is grounded in competence, honesty, and a commitment to improvement is resilient.

Let us also address a common objection. Some surgeons hear the phrase "psychological safety" and worry that it means lowering standards. They fear that if they stop being demanding, if they stop holding people accountable, performance will decline. This is a fundamental misunderstanding. Edmondson has been explicit about this: psychological safety and high standards are not opposites. They are independent dimensions that combine to create four distinct zones. Low safety with low standards produces apathy. Low safety with high standards produces anxiety, which is where many surgical programs currently operate. High safety with low standards produces a comfortable environment where no one grows. But high safety combined with high standards produces what Edmondson calls the learning zone, and this is where the best surgical teams operate. People feel safe enough to take risks and push themselves because they know that mistakes will be treated as learning opportunities, not career-ending events. The goal is not to be easier on your team. The goal is to be demanding and supportive at the same time.

Now let us move to the practice exercise. I want you to try something concrete during your next operating day.

Before your first case, conduct a briefing that includes the following elements. First, introduce yourself and ask every team member to state their name and role. Even if you know everyone, this ritual resets the dynamic and signals that every person's identity matters. Second, state the plan clearly and then ask an open-ended question: "What concerns does anyone have about this case?" Pause for a full five seconds after asking. Silence is uncomfortable, but it is where courage finds its footing. If no one speaks, do not move on immediately. Say, "I really do want to hear if anyone has a concern. It helps me be a better surgeon." Third, at the end of the case, before anyone leaves the room, ask the team to debrief. What went well, what could we improve, and was there any moment where someone felt uncomfortable or unsure but did not speak up?

After your operating day, I want you to reflect on the following questions. How did the briefing feel compared to your usual routine? Did anyone raise a concern during the case that they might not have raised without the explicit invitation? During the debrief, did you learn anything about your team's experience that you did not already know? And most importantly, when a mistake or near-miss occurred, how did you respond? Were you curious or were you critical?

Here is a second exercise for training environments. If you are working with residents, try this during your next case together. At a natural pause point, perhaps while waiting for a specimen result or during a routine portion of the procedure, ask your resident to describe what they are thinking about the case so far. Ask them what concerns they have. Ask them what they would do differently if they were the attending. Then listen without correcting. You are not looking for the right answer. You are looking for honest engagement. And when they offer it, reinforce it. Say, "That is a great observation" or "I had not thought about it that way." These micro-moments of affirmation accumulate. Over weeks and months, they transform the training relationship from one based on fear into one based on trust, and residents who trust their attendings learn faster, retain more, and become better surgeons.

A third exercise is what I call the thirty-day authority gradient audit. For the next thirty days, pay attention to every interaction you have with a team member who is junior to you in the hierarchy. Count the number of times someone disagrees with you, asks you a challenging question, or brings you bad news. If that number is very low, it does not mean your judgment is perfect. It means your team does not feel safe challenging you. A healthy team should generate frequent, constructive friction. If you are not experiencing that friction, the problem is not that your team agrees with everything you say. The problem is that they have learned that disagreement is not safe.

Now let us bring this all together in the synthesis.

Psychological safety is not a personality trait. It is not something you either have or you do not. It is a climate that you create through specific, repeatable behaviors. Every time you invite input, you build it. Every time you respond to a mistake with curiosity instead of contempt, you build it. Every time you admit your own uncertainty, you build it. And every time you shut someone down, dismiss a concern, or react with anger, you erode it. The compound effect of these micro-interactions over weeks, months, and years determines whether your operating room is a learning environment or a silent one.

The evidence is overwhelming. Teams with high psychological safety have fewer preventable adverse events, higher rates of error reporting, better communication during critical moments, and stronger retention of talented team members. In training environments, psychological safety predicts resident well-being, learning outcomes, and the likelihood that trainees will speak up when they see something wrong. In the broader organizational context, psychological safety is the foundation upon which every other dimension of team effectiveness is built.

As a surgeon, you have disproportionate power to shape this climate. Your position at the top of the hierarchy means that your behavior is watched, interpreted, and amplified by everyone around you. A single dismissive comment from you carries more weight than a dozen encouraging comments from a peer. This is not fair, but it is the reality of positional authority. The good news is that this same dynamic means your positive behaviors are also amplified. One surgeon who consistently models psychological safety can transform the culture of an entire operating room, and over time, an entire department.

The framework we discussed today, Edmondson's psychological safety combined with the practical tools of briefings, debriefings, framing the work, and graduated disclosure, gives you a concrete set of behaviors to practice. You do not need to overhaul your personality. You do not need to become a different kind of surgeon. You simply need to be intentional about how you respond to the people around you, especially in moments of stress, uncertainty, and error. Those are the moments that define your team's culture. Those are the moments that determine whether the next concern goes voiced or silent. And in surgery, silence can be the most dangerous sound in the room.

Thank you for spending this time on a topic that matters deeply. The next time you walk into your operating room, remember that you are not just performing a procedure. You are leading a team. And the way you lead that team, the safety you create or fail to create, shapes not only the outcomes for today's patient but the habits and courage of every person who works alongside you. That is the real work of surgical leadership, and it starts with a single question: Does my team feel safe enough to tell me the truth?
