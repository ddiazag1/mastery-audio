Welcome to this session on giving effective feedback using the SBI model. This is one of the most practical leadership skills you will ever develop, and it is especially critical for surgeons who operate in high-stakes environments where clear communication directly affects patient outcomes. Whether you are coaching a resident through their first craniotomy closure, debriefing with a scrub tech after a difficult case, or having a candid conversation with a colleague whose behavior in the operating room is creating friction, the ability to deliver feedback that actually lands and actually changes behavior is what separates competent surgeons from true surgical leaders.

Most of us received almost no formal training in how to give feedback. We learned by absorbing whatever models our attendings demonstrated, which ranged from thoughtful mentorship to outright verbal abuse. The culture of surgical training has shifted enormously, and for good reason, but many surgeons still feel uncertain about how to deliver feedback that is honest without being harsh, specific without being nitpicking, and constructive without being demoralizing. The SBI model gives you a repeatable, structured framework that eliminates that uncertainty. SBI stands for Situation, Behavior, Impact, and once you internalize it, you will find that giving feedback becomes not only easier but dramatically more effective.

Let us begin with the framework itself. The SBI model was developed by the Center for Creative Leadership and has become one of the most widely adopted feedback frameworks in professional development worldwide. Its power lies in its simplicity. Every piece of feedback you deliver, whether positive or constructive, follows three steps. First, you identify the Situation, which is the specific context in which the behavior occurred. Second, you describe the Behavior, which is what the person actually did or said, described in observable, factual terms. Third, you explain the Impact, which is the effect that behavior had on you, on the team, on the patient, or on the workflow. That is the entire model. Situation. Behavior. Impact. Three components, delivered in sequence, creating a feedback statement that is specific, objective, and actionable.

Now let us go deeper into each component and understand why each one matters so much.

The Situation is your anchor point. It grounds the feedback in a specific time and place, which prevents the conversation from drifting into vague generalizations. When you say something like "you always seem distracted" or "you need to be more careful," you have given someone nothing to work with. They cannot act on a generalization because they do not know which moment you are referring to, and they are likely to become defensive because the word "always" feels like a character indictment rather than a behavioral observation. By contrast, when you say "during the posterior fossa case this morning" or "in our team huddle last Tuesday," you have placed the feedback in a concrete, retrievable moment. The person can recall that moment. They can reconstruct what was happening. They are already mentally engaged with the specifics, which means they are far more likely to hear the rest of what you have to say.

The Behavior component is where most people stumble, and it is the most important part of the model to get right. Behavior means what the person observably did or said. Not what you think they were feeling, not what you assume their intention was, not your interpretation of their character. Observable behavior. There is an enormous difference between saying "you were disrespectful" and saying "you interrupted the anesthesiologist twice during the pre-op briefing." The first statement is a judgment. The second is a fact. When you describe behavior in factual, observable terms, you remove the subjective layer that triggers defensiveness. The person may argue about whether they were being "disrespectful," but they cannot argue about whether they interrupted someone twice. You saw it. It happened. By staying at the level of observable behavior, you keep the conversation grounded in reality rather than in competing interpretations.

This is particularly important in surgical environments because the operating room is a pressure cooker. Emotions run high. Stress distorts perception. If you tell a resident "you seemed nervous and it made everyone uncomfortable," you have made two interpretive leaps. You assumed they were nervous, and you assumed everyone was uncomfortable. Maybe they were not nervous at all. Maybe they were intensely focused and their silence was concentration, not anxiety. But if you say "when you hesitated for about fifteen seconds before making the dural incision and looked at me twice without proceeding," you have described exactly what happened. Now you can have a productive conversation about what was going on in that moment and what they might do differently next time.

The Impact component is what gives feedback its emotional weight and its practical relevance. Impact answers the question: why does this matter? When you explain the impact of someone's behavior, you help them understand the consequences of their actions in a way that connects to something they care about. Impact can be personal, meaning how the behavior affected you. It can be team-oriented, meaning how it affected the functioning of the group. It can be patient-centered, meaning how it affected care quality or safety. And it can be systemic, meaning how it affected workflow, efficiency, or culture.

Here is a complete SBI statement bringing all three components together. "During the lumbar fusion yesterday afternoon, when you handed me the pedicle screw driver without confirming the screw size verbally, I had to stop and verify it myself, which added time to the case and broke my concentration during a critical step." Situation: the lumbar fusion yesterday afternoon. Behavior: handing the screw driver without verbal confirmation of screw size. Impact: added time, broke concentration during a critical step. That statement is specific, factual, and clearly explains why the behavior matters. There is no accusation of laziness or incompetence. There is no character judgment. There is simply an observation and its consequence, delivered in a way that invites correction without provoking shame.

Now let us talk about the two major categories of feedback and how the SBI model applies differently to each.

Positive feedback is chronically underdelivered in surgical culture. Many surgeons operate under the assumption that competence is the baseline expectation and therefore does not need to be acknowledged. This is a leadership failure. Positive feedback, when delivered with specificity using the SBI model, serves a critical function. It reinforces the exact behaviors you want to see repeated. If you simply say "good job today," the person feels momentarily appreciated but has no idea which specific behavior earned the praise. They cannot reliably repeat it because they do not know what "it" was. But if you say "during the cervical case this morning, when you anticipated the need for the Kerrison and had it ready before I asked, it kept the case flowing smoothly and saved us several minutes," you have done something powerful. You have identified a specific behavior and connected it to a meaningful outcome. That resident or that scrub tech now knows exactly what to do again next time. Positive feedback using the SBI model is not flattery. It is precision reinforcement of high-performance behavior.

Make it a discipline to deliver at least one piece of specific positive feedback per day. Not because it makes you a nicer person, although it does, but because it is the single most effective tool for shaping the performance of the people around you. People repeat behaviors that are noticed and valued. If you only give feedback when something goes wrong, you are training your team to associate your attention with punishment, and they will learn to avoid your attention rather than seek your guidance.

Constructive feedback is where the SBI model truly proves its worth, because it creates a structure that separates the behavior from the person. This distinction is not just a communication technique. It is a psychological principle. When people feel that their identity or character is under attack, they enter a defensive state in which learning becomes nearly impossible. The amygdala activates, cortisol floods the system, and the prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain responsible for rational thought and behavioral change, essentially goes offline. You have seen this happen. You give a resident critical feedback and their eyes glaze over, they start making excuses, or they shut down entirely. That is not a character flaw. That is a neurobiological response to perceived threat. The SBI model minimizes that threat response by keeping the feedback anchored in observable facts rather than personal judgments.

When delivering constructive feedback, there is an important extension to the SBI model that many practitioners add, which is the question. After you deliver your Situation, Behavior, and Impact statement, you pause and ask a genuine question. "What was going on for you in that moment?" or "How did you see that situation?" or "What would you do differently next time?" This transforms the feedback from a one-directional transmission into a two-way conversation. It demonstrates respect for the other person's perspective, and it often surfaces information you did not have. Maybe the resident hesitated before the dural incision because they noticed an anatomical variant and were reconsidering their approach. Maybe the scrub tech did not verbally confirm the screw size because the room was so loud they did not think you would hear them. The question is where learning happens, for both of you.

Let us now turn to the specific environments where surgeons give feedback most frequently and examine how the SBI model applies in each.

Resident education is the most obvious application. As a teaching surgeon, you are giving feedback constantly, often in real time during cases, and the quality of that feedback directly shapes the next generation of surgeons. The SBI model is especially valuable here because residents are in a perpetual state of evaluation anxiety. They know they are being watched and judged at all times, and this awareness can make them hypersensitive to criticism. When you use SBI, you signal that your feedback is about specific moments and specific behaviors, not about their overall worth or potential as a surgeon. Over time, this builds what psychologists call psychological safety, which is the belief that one can take interpersonal risks without fear of punishment or humiliation. Residents who feel psychologically safe ask more questions, admit mistakes earlier, and seek feedback proactively rather than avoiding it. This directly improves patient safety.

Peer feedback is harder, and most surgeons avoid it entirely. Giving feedback to a colleague of equal or senior rank feels awkward and potentially threatening to the relationship. But the SBI model makes it manageable because it strips away judgment and focuses on facts. If a colleague is consistently running late and it is affecting the OR schedule, you do not need to say "you are always late and it is disrespectful to the team." You can say "on Tuesday and Thursday this week, when the first case started twenty minutes behind schedule, the entire day's cases were pushed back and the staff had to stay late." That is a factual statement. It describes a situation, a behavior, and an impact. It does not accuse. It does not moralize. It simply states what happened and why it matters. This approach is far more likely to produce a productive conversation than a confrontational one.

Operating room coaching is the third critical application. The OR is unique because feedback often needs to be delivered in real time, under pressure, and in front of an audience. This is where the SBI model needs to be adapted for brevity and immediacy. You may not have time for a full three-part statement during a critical moment. In those situations, you can use a compressed version. "Right there, when you angled the retractor laterally, you are putting pressure on the nerve root." That is SBI compressed into a single sentence. Situation: right there, meaning this exact moment. Behavior: angling the retractor laterally. Impact: pressure on the nerve root. After the case, you can expand on the feedback in a more detailed debrief, but in the moment, even a compressed SBI statement is far more useful than a vague "be careful" or an alarming "stop, stop, stop."

Let us now move into a practice exercise. I want you to take a recent interaction, something that happened in the last week, where you either gave feedback or wished you had given feedback. It can be positive or constructive. Now, mentally reconstruct that moment using the SBI framework.

First, define the Situation. When and where did this happen? Be specific. Not "the other day" but "during the eight o'clock case on Wednesday" or "at the department meeting last Friday."

Second, identify the Behavior. What did the person actually do or say? Strip away your interpretations and judgments. Do not say what they seemed to be thinking or feeling. Describe only what a camera in the room would have recorded.

Third, articulate the Impact. What was the consequence of that behavior? How did it affect you, the team, the patient, or the workflow? Be honest and specific. Did it slow the case down? Did it create confusion? Did it improve efficiency? Did it make you feel confident in their abilities?

Now put those three pieces together into a single, clean statement. Say it out loud if you can. Notice how different it feels compared to the vague feedback most of us default to. Notice how grounded it is. Notice how it focuses entirely on what happened and why it matters, without attacking anyone's character or making assumptions about their motivations.

If you want to push yourself further, practice delivering one SBI feedback statement every day this week. Aim for a mix of positive and constructive. Start with positive feedback because it is lower stakes and will help you build the muscle memory for the framework. Then gradually introduce constructive feedback as you become more comfortable with the structure. Pay attention to how people respond. You will likely notice that they engage more deeply with your feedback than they did before, because the specificity signals that you were actually paying attention, and the lack of judgment signals that you are trying to help, not to punish.

There is one more dimension to effective feedback that the SBI model supports but does not explicitly address, and that is timing. The best feedback is timely. The closer you can deliver it to the moment it references, the more powerful it will be. This is because the memory is fresh, the emotional context is still accessible, and the behavioral pattern has not yet hardened. If you wait three weeks to tell someone that their instrument handling was creating safety concerns, the moment has lost its specificity and its emotional charge. They may not even remember the case you are referring to. Aim to deliver feedback within twenty-four hours when possible, and ideally on the same day. For operating room feedback, the immediate post-case debrief is the gold standard. Take two minutes after every case to offer one piece of specific feedback, positive or constructive, and you will see a measurable improvement in team performance within weeks.

Timing also applies to the environment. Positive feedback can be delivered publicly. In fact, public positive feedback is often more powerful because it signals to the entire team what behaviors are valued. Constructive feedback should almost always be delivered privately. Correcting someone in front of their peers triggers shame, which is the single most destructive emotion in a learning environment. It does not matter how well-structured your SBI statement is. If you deliver it in front of an audience, the person will hear it as humiliation, and the learning will be lost. Pull them aside. Close the door. Start by signaling your intent. "I want to talk about something from the case this morning because I think it will help you improve, and I want to make sure I am being clear about what I observed."

Let us bring everything together now. The SBI model is a deceptively simple framework that solves a genuinely difficult problem. Most feedback fails because it is vague, judgmental, poorly timed, or all three. The SBI model forces you to be specific by anchoring feedback in a defined situation. It forces you to be objective by describing only observable behavior. And it forces you to be relevant by articulating the concrete impact of that behavior. When you add a genuine question at the end, you transform a monologue into a dialogue, and you open the door to mutual learning.

For surgeons, this framework is not a soft skill. It is a clinical skill. The quality of your feedback directly affects the performance of every person who works with you, from residents to nurses to scrub techs to colleagues. It affects how quickly trainees develop competence. It affects whether your OR team anticipates your needs or simply follows orders. It affects whether your colleagues trust you enough to have honest conversations about patient care. And ultimately, it affects patient outcomes, because every error that goes uncorrected due to poor feedback is an error that will be repeated.

Your action items coming out of this session are straightforward. First, memorize the three components: Situation, Behavior, Impact. Write them on an index card and keep it in your white coat pocket until they become automatic. Second, deliver one specific piece of positive feedback per day for the next seven days. Use the full SBI structure. Third, identify one constructive feedback conversation you have been avoiding and prepare an SBI statement for it. Write it out. Practice it. Then deliver it within the next week, privately, with a genuine question at the end. Fourth, after each feedback conversation this week, take thirty seconds to reflect on how it went. What worked? What felt awkward? What would you adjust next time?

The SBI model is not the only feedback framework available, but it is the one best suited to surgical environments because of its precision, its brevity, and its emphasis on observable behavior over subjective interpretation. Master it, practice it daily, and you will become the kind of leader that people seek out, not because you are easy on them, but because your feedback actually helps them get better. That is the highest compliment a surgical leader can earn.
