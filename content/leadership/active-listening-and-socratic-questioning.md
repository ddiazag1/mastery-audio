Welcome back. Today we are going to explore two of the most powerful communication tools available to you as a surgeon-leader, and they are tools that, ironically, have almost nothing to do with what you say. We are talking about active listening and Socratic questioning. These two practices are deeply intertwined, and when you develop real facility with them, you will find that your ability to lead teams, teach residents, navigate difficult patient conversations, and resolve conflicts transforms in ways that are almost hard to believe. So settle in. Let us begin with where these ideas come from and why they matter.

The concept of active listening was first formally described by Carl Rogers and Richard Farson in a 1957 paper of the same name. Rogers, as you may know, was one of the founders of humanistic psychology, and his entire therapeutic framework was built on the idea that people heal, grow, and change not when they are told what to do, but when they feel genuinely heard. Rogers called this "unconditional positive regard," and he believed that the act of truly listening to someone, without judgment, without an agenda, without mentally preparing your rebuttal, was itself a transformative act. Farson, his collaborator, helped translate this insight from the therapy room into the world of organizations and leadership. Together, they argued that most of what we call listening is not really listening at all. It is waiting. It is scanning for the pause where we can insert our own point. It is filtering what another person says through our own assumptions and experiences so rapidly that we never actually receive what they are trying to communicate.

Now, Socratic questioning comes to us from an entirely different tradition, but it shares a surprising amount of DNA with active listening. Socrates, as described in Plato's dialogues, did not teach by lecturing. He taught by asking questions. His method, sometimes called the elenchus, involved drawing out what a person believed, then gently and persistently probing those beliefs with further questions until the person arrived at deeper understanding on their own. There was a second dimension to this, which the Greeks called maieutics, from the word for midwifery. Socrates said he was a midwife of ideas. He did not put knowledge into people. He helped them deliver knowledge that was already forming within them. This is a profound reframe for anyone in a teaching role, and it is especially relevant for surgeons who train residents.

The core principle that unites active listening and Socratic questioning is this: the most powerful thing you can do in a conversation is create space for the other person to think more deeply. Not to impress them with your knowledge. Not to correct them. Not to demonstrate your authority. But to help them access their own understanding, express their own concerns, and arrive at their own insights. When you do this well, you do not lose authority. You gain it. People trust leaders who listen. They follow leaders who make them feel intelligent and capable. And they remember, often for years, the attending who asked them a question that changed how they thought about a problem.

Let us now go deeper into each of these components, starting with active listening. There are three core skills within active listening that you need to understand and practice: paraphrasing, summarizing, and reflecting. These are not just techniques. They are disciplines of attention.

Paraphrasing is the act of restating what someone has just said in your own words, not to show you were paying attention like a student reciting back a lecture, but to confirm that you actually understood what they meant. This is harder than it sounds. When a resident says to you, "I am not sure I am ready to do this case," the temptation is to immediately respond with reassurance or a plan. But if you pause and paraphrase, you might say, "So you are feeling some uncertainty about whether your preparation is sufficient for this procedure." Notice what happens. You have not agreed or disagreed. You have not solved anything. But you have done something enormously important: you have shown the resident that you heard them, and you have given them the chance to clarify. They might say, "No, it is not that I do not feel prepared. It is that I had a bad outcome on a similar case last month and I am still processing it." Now you are in an entirely different conversation, a much more important one, and you would never have gotten there if you had jumped straight to your response.

Summarizing is related to paraphrasing but operates at a larger scale. When you summarize, you pull together the key themes from a longer stretch of conversation. This is particularly useful in team meetings and in complex patient discussions. For example, after a multidisciplinary tumor board where several specialists have weighed in, you might say, "So what I am hearing is that there is agreement on the diagnosis, but there are two schools of thought on timing. The oncology team favors neoadjuvant treatment before surgery, while the surgical perspective is that the window for resection may narrow if we wait. And the patient has expressed a strong preference for moving quickly." A summary like this does several things simultaneously. It demonstrates that you have been tracking the entire conversation. It organizes the discussion so that everyone is oriented to the same map. And it creates a natural pivot point for decision-making. Edgar Schein, the organizational psychologist at MIT who wrote extensively about process consultation and humble inquiry, argued that this kind of summarizing is one of the most underused leadership skills in professional settings. Leaders who summarize well are perceived as fair, thoughtful, and in command of complexity, even when they have not yet offered their own opinion.

Reflecting is the third skill, and it is the deepest. When you reflect, you name the emotion or the underlying concern that you sense beneath what someone is saying. This requires a kind of emotional attunement that many surgeons have not been trained in, but that all of us are capable of developing. Reflecting sounds like this: "It sounds like you are frustrated that the team did not follow the protocol, and maybe underneath that, you are worried about what it means for patient safety on your service." Or, in a patient encounter: "I can hear that this diagnosis is frightening, and it seems like part of what is making it harder is that you have been through something similar with your father." Reflecting is powerful because it validates the emotional reality of the person you are speaking with. Daniel Goleman, whose work on emotional intelligence has been widely applied in leadership development, found that leaders who demonstrate this kind of empathic accuracy, the ability to correctly identify what someone is feeling, build significantly higher levels of trust and psychological safety on their teams. And Amy Edmondson's research at Harvard Business School on psychological safety has shown that teams with high psychological safety perform better, report errors more readily, and innovate more effectively. So when you reflect well, you are not just being kind. You are building the conditions for high performance.

Now let us turn to Socratic questioning. The Socratic method, as practiced in its original form, involved several distinct types of questions, and understanding these types will make you far more effective as a teacher, a leader, and a communicator.

The first type is clarifying questions. These are questions that ask someone to elaborate or specify what they mean. "What do you mean when you say the patient is not doing well?" "Can you be more specific about what you observed on your exam?" These questions seem simple, but they serve a critical function. They force precision. In surgery, imprecision kills. When a resident says, "The patient looks septic," a clarifying question like, "What specifically are you seeing that makes you think sepsis rather than a localized infection?" trains them to ground their clinical reasoning in observable data rather than pattern-matching heuristics that they cannot articulate.

The second type is probing assumptions. These are questions that surface the hidden premises beneath someone's reasoning. "What are you assuming about the blood supply to that region?" "What would have to be true for your treatment plan to be the best option?" These questions are not challenges. They are invitations to think more carefully. When you ask a resident what they are assuming, you are teaching them the habit of examining their own reasoning, which is the foundation of good clinical judgment. Richard Paul and Linda Elder, who developed the framework for critical thinking at the Foundation for Critical Thinking, identified probing assumptions as one of the six essential types of Socratic questions, and they argued that it is the type most directly connected to intellectual humility, the willingness to recognize that your thinking might be incomplete.

The third type is probing evidence and reasoning. "What evidence supports that diagnosis?" "How did you arrive at that conclusion?" "What is the strongest argument against your current plan?" These questions teach residents to build cases, not just have opinions. They also model a culture where decisions are expected to be grounded in evidence, which is essential in surgical practice.

The fourth type is exploring alternatives and perspectives. "What would a neuroradiologist say about this imaging?" "If you were the patient, what would matter most to you in this decision?" "What would we do differently if the patient were twenty years younger?" These questions expand the frame. They train residents to see clinical problems from multiple angles, which is exactly the kind of thinking that prevents tunnel vision during complex cases.

The fifth type, and this is the one most people associate with Socrates, is questioning the question itself. "Why do you think I am asking you this?" "What is the real question we should be asking about this patient's care?" This metacognitive move teaches residents to think about their own thinking, which is the highest order of clinical reasoning.

The key to all of these is tone. Socratic questioning done poorly feels like an interrogation. Done well, it feels like a collaboration. The difference is not in the questions themselves but in the spirit behind them. If you are asking questions to demonstrate that the resident does not know something, they will feel it, and they will shut down. If you are asking questions because you genuinely want to help them discover what they already partly know, or because you are genuinely curious about their reasoning, they will feel that too, and they will open up. This distinction maps directly onto what Carol Dweck at Stanford has described as the difference between a fixed mindset and a growth mindset in teaching. Teachers who use questions punitively reinforce a fixed mindset, where the student believes intelligence is static and mistakes are shameful. Teachers who use questions generatively foster a growth mindset, where the student believes ability is developed through effort and mistakes are data.

Let us now talk about how all of this applies specifically in your world as a surgeon-leader. I want to walk through three concrete scenarios.

Scenario one: teaching in the operating room. You are midway through an anterior cervical discectomy, and your senior resident is at the table with you. You have just completed the discectomy and you are preparing to place the graft. Instead of narrating what you are doing and why, which is the default teaching mode for most attendings, you pause and ask, "Before I place this graft, walk me through what you are assessing about the endplate preparation." The resident begins to answer. They mention removing the cartilaginous endplate. You paraphrase: "So you are thinking about getting down to bleeding bone for fusion." Then you probe: "What is the risk if we are too aggressive with the endplate preparation?" The resident hesitates, then says, "Subsidence." You reflect: "Good. And you hesitated there, which makes me think you might be connecting this to a case where you saw that happen." The resident nods and describes a previous case. In ninety seconds, you have accomplished more teaching than a thirty-minute lecture would have. The resident has retrieved knowledge from memory, applied it to the case in front of them, connected it to a prior experience, and felt respected in the process. This is maieutics in action. You midwifed an insight.

Scenario two: a difficult patient conversation. You are in clinic with a patient who has been told they need a lumbar fusion, and they are anxious. They have read things online. They have a friend who had a bad outcome. The temptation is to go into education mode, to explain the procedure, cite your success rates, and address their concerns point by point. And there is a time for that. But first, you listen. You say, "Tell me what is on your mind about this." And then you are quiet. The patient talks for two minutes. They mention the friend. They mention their job. They mention their kids. When they finish, you summarize: "So it sounds like you are weighing the pain you are in now against the fear of something going wrong, and the stakes feel very high because your ability to work and take care of your family depends on the outcome." The patient's shoulders drop. They nod. They feel heard. Now, and only now, you begin to address their specific concerns. Michael Balint, the Hungarian psychoanalyst who pioneered the study of the doctor-patient relationship in the 1950s, described this as the "drug doctor," the idea that the physician themselves, through the quality of their attention, is a therapeutic agent. When you listen to a patient this way, you are not wasting time. You are building the therapeutic alliance that leads to better adherence, better outcomes, and fewer callbacks.

Scenario three: a team conflict. Two members of your OR staff are in a disagreement about workflow. One believes the current turnover process is inefficient. The other feels that the proposed changes add unnecessary complexity. In your leadership role, the temptation is to make a decision and move on. But you try something different. You ask each person to describe their perspective while the other listens. Then you ask each person to paraphrase what the other said. This alone often resolves about half of the conflict, because most disagreements persist not because the positions are irreconcilable but because neither side feels heard. Then you ask a Socratic question: "What would the ideal turnover process look like if we could design it from scratch, without any constraints?" This question reframes the conversation from adversarial to collaborative. It moves both parties from defending their positions to imagining a shared future. Roger Fisher and William Ury, in their foundational negotiation text Getting to Yes, called this "inventing options for mutual gain," and it is one of the most reliable techniques for moving past impasse.

Now, let us turn to your practice exercise for this week. I want you to choose three conversations over the next seven days where you deliberately practice these skills. One should be a teaching interaction with a resident or medical student. One should be a patient encounter. And one should be a team or administrative conversation.

In each of these conversations, I want you to set a simple internal goal: listen for ninety seconds before you respond. That is it. Ninety seconds. When the other person is speaking, resist the urge to formulate your reply. Instead, focus entirely on what they are saying, and what they might be feeling beneath what they are saying. When they finish, use one of the three active listening skills. Paraphrase what they said. Or summarize the key themes. Or reflect the emotion you sensed. Then, before you offer your own perspective, ask one Socratic question. Just one. It could be a clarifying question: "Can you say more about that?" It could be a question that probes their reasoning: "What led you to that conclusion?" It could be a question that explores alternatives: "What would you do differently if you had more time or resources?"

After each of these three conversations, take sixty seconds to reflect. Ask yourself three questions. First, what did I learn that I would not have learned if I had responded immediately? Second, how did the other person's demeanor or engagement change when they felt heard? And third, what was the hardest part of staying in listening mode? This last question is important because it reveals your own patterns. Many surgeons find that the hardest part is tolerating the silence. We are trained to act, to decide, to move. Listening feels passive. But as you practice, you will discover that it is anything but passive. It is one of the most demanding and generative things you can do with your attention.

If you want to go further, try keeping a brief note after each conversation. Just two or three sentences. Over time, these notes will reveal patterns in how you listen, what triggers your urge to interrupt, and what kinds of questions open people up versus shut them down. This is the kind of self-awareness that separates competent surgeons from true surgical leaders.

Let us close by connecting all of this to the bigger picture of who you are becoming as a surgeon-leader. Surgery selects for decisiveness, speed, and technical precision. These are virtues, and you should never abandon them. But leadership requires a complementary set of capabilities, and active listening and Socratic questioning sit at the heart of those capabilities. When you listen deeply, you gather better information, which leads to better decisions. When you ask Socratic questions, you develop the people around you, which builds a stronger team. When you reflect emotions and summarize perspectives, you create psychological safety, which enables your team to speak up when something is going wrong before it becomes a complication, before it becomes a never event, before it becomes a lawsuit or a tragedy.

The philosopher Epictetus, who was a Stoic and who influenced much of the thinking about self-mastery that we value in surgery, said that we have two ears and one mouth so that we can listen twice as much as we speak. That is a simple formulation, but there is a lifetime of practice in it. The surgeons who are remembered not just for their technical brilliance but for the teams they built, the residents they trained, and the patients who trusted them, those are the surgeons who learned to listen.

Rogers said it best. He said that when someone really hears you, without judging you, without trying to take responsibility for you, without trying to mold you, it feels damn good. That is the experience you can create for the people you lead. And when you combine that quality of listening with the disciplined curiosity of Socratic questioning, you become the kind of leader that people seek out, not because you have all the answers, but because being in conversation with you helps them find their own.

Practice this week. Ninety seconds of listening. One paraphrase or summary or reflection. One Socratic question. Three conversations. That is your assignment. I will see you in the next session.
