Welcome back. Today we are going to talk about one of the most underappreciated administrative processes in all of medicine, and one that has an outsized impact on your revenue from the very first day you start practicing. We are talking about credentialing and privileging. Now, I know this is not the most exciting topic. Nobody goes into otolaryngology because they love filling out paperwork and chasing down verification letters. But here is the reality. If you do not understand this process, if you do not manage it proactively, you will leave tens of thousands of dollars on the table, and in some cases you will find yourself unable to bill for the work you are already doing. That is real money walking out the door, and it is entirely preventable.

So let us start with the basics. Credentialing and privileging are two related but distinct processes, and conflating them is one of the most common mistakes I see new physicians make. Credentialing is the process by which an insurance payer, a hospital, or a healthcare organization verifies your qualifications. They are confirming that you are who you say you are, that you went to the medical school you claim, that you completed the residency you listed, that your board certification or board eligibility is legitimate, that your DEA license is active, that your state medical license is current, and that you do not have a history of malpractice actions or disciplinary sanctions that would disqualify you. This is fundamentally a verification and vetting process. Privileging, on the other hand, is the process by which a specific hospital or surgical facility grants you the authority to perform particular procedures at that institution. You can be fully credentialed with every payer in the state, but if you have not been granted surgical privileges at the hospital where you want to operate, you cannot take a patient to that operating room. These two tracks run in parallel but they serve different masters. Payer credentialing determines whether you get paid. Hospital privileging determines whether you can practice.

Now let us talk about why this matters so much financially. The timeline for initial credentialing with a commercial payer typically runs between ninety and one hundred eighty days. Some payers are faster, and Medicare is often on the quicker end at around sixty to ninety days, but many commercial plans, especially regional ones, can take the full six months. During that window, if you are seeing patients covered by a plan where you are not yet credentialed, you generally cannot bill that payer. Some payers will retroactively process claims back to your application date, but many will not. And even those that do allow retroactive billing often impose strict filing deadlines that create an administrative nightmare for your billing team. The practical impact is this. According to MGMA data, the median revenue per physician in an otolaryngology practice is approximately six hundred fifty thousand to seven hundred fifty thousand dollars annually. If you divide that across twelve months, you are looking at roughly fifty-four thousand to sixty-two thousand dollars per month in collections. If your credentialing is delayed by even sixty days with a major payer that represents, say, thirty percent of your payer mix, you could be looking at thirty-two thousand to thirty-seven thousand dollars in lost or delayed revenue. And that is a conservative estimate. In a busy ENT practice performing procedures like septoplasty, tonsillectomy, thyroidectomy, and sinus surgery, the per-case revenue can be substantial, and every week of delay compounds the problem.

Let us now take a deep dive into the mechanics of each process, starting with payer credentialing. The foundation of payer credentialing in the modern era is your CAQH ProView profile. CAQH, which stands for the Council for Affordable Quality Healthcare, maintains a universal credentialing database that the vast majority of commercial payers use as their primary source of provider information. Think of it as your master credentialing file. You fill it out once, and then payers pull from it rather than requiring you to complete separate applications for each one. Your CAQH profile includes your personal information, education and training history, work history, malpractice insurance details, hospital affiliations, license numbers, DEA registration, board certification status, and practice location information. Here is the critical thing about CAQH that catches people off guard. It requires re-attestation every one hundred twenty days. That means every four months you need to log in and confirm that your information is still accurate. If you miss that re-attestation window, your profile goes inactive, and payers can and will terminate your participation or deny new claims. I have seen this happen more times than I can count. A busy surgeon forgets about the CAQH re-attestation, the profile lapses, and suddenly claims start getting denied. By the time someone notices, there are weeks of claims in limbo. Set a recurring calendar reminder ninety days out from every attestation. Better yet, assign this to a specific staff member and build it into your practice's compliance calendar.

Beyond CAQH, each payer will have its own credentialing application. For the major national payers like UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, Blue Cross Blue Shield plans, and Humana, you will typically submit your application either online through their provider portal or through a delegated credentialing arrangement if you are joining a larger group or health system. The documentation you will need to have ready includes your current curriculum vitae, copies of your medical school diploma and residency completion certificate, your board certification or board eligibility letter from the American Board of Otolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery, your current state medical license, your DEA certificate, your malpractice insurance face sheet showing coverage dates and limits, a completed W-9 for tax identification, your National Provider Identifier or NPI number, and often a signed attestation questionnaire addressing things like malpractice history, criminal history, substance abuse history, and any gaps in practice. One thing I want to flag specifically for ENT. If you perform procedures that cross into other specialties, for example sleep surgery, facial plastics, or allergy testing and immunotherapy, make sure your credentialing applications reflect the full scope of your practice. Some payers will credential you for general otolaryngology but then require a separate review or additional documentation before they will reimburse for allergy services billed under codes like 95165 for antigen immunotherapy supervision or 95004 for percutaneous allergy testing. If you are running an allergy practice within your ENT office, which many of you are, this is a revenue stream that can easily generate one hundred fifty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand dollars annually, and a credentialing oversight can shut it off entirely.

Now let us talk about hospital privileging. This is the other track, and it has its own timeline and requirements. When you apply for privileges at a hospital, the medical staff office will conduct their own primary source verification of your credentials, independent of what any payer has done. They will verify your training, check the National Practitioner Data Bank for any reports, review your malpractice history, and often require letters of recommendation from physicians who have directly observed your clinical and surgical competence. For an ENT surgeon, your privilege request will include a specific list of procedures you want to be authorized to perform. This is where it gets granular. You are not just asking for "ENT privileges." You are requesting privileges for specific categories like otologic surgery, rhinologic and sinus surgery, laryngeal surgery, head and neck oncologic surgery, facial plastic and reconstructive surgery, pediatric otolaryngology, and so on. Each hospital has its own delineation of privileges form, and you need to go through it carefully. Do not just check every box. Request privileges for procedures you are actually trained to perform, that you have sufficient case volume to demonstrate competency in, and that the facility has the equipment and support staff to handle. For example, if you are requesting privileges for cochlear implant surgery, the hospital will want to see evidence of your training volume and may require that the facility has the appropriate audiological support and intraoperative monitoring capabilities.

The privileging process typically takes sixty to ninety days at most hospitals, but it can take longer if the medical executive committee meets infrequently or if there are questions about your application. Some hospitals have a provisional privilege process that allows you to start operating while your full application is being reviewed, often under the supervision or sponsorship of an existing medical staff member. Ask about this option if timing is critical. One important nuance here is that privileges are institution-specific. If you operate at two hospitals and an ambulatory surgery center, you need separate privileges at each facility. And if you are doing outreach clinics, which is very common in ENT where you might travel to a rural hospital one or two days a month, you need privileges at those locations as well. Each one is a separate application, a separate review process, and a separate set of ongoing requirements.

Let us talk about re-credentialing now, because this is not a one-time event. Both payer credentialing and hospital privileging require periodic renewal. For payers, re-credentialing typically occurs every two to three years, depending on the plan. For hospital privileges, re-appointment to the medical staff is usually every two years. During re-credentialing, you will need to provide updated documentation, and the reviewing body will look at your practice since the last credentialing cycle. For hospital re-privileging specifically, they will review your case volume, your complication rates, your outcomes data, any peer review actions, any malpractice claims, and your compliance with continuing medical education requirements. Many hospitals now use the Ongoing Professional Practice Evaluation, or OPPE, and Focused Professional Practice Evaluation, or FPPE, frameworks mandated by the Joint Commission. OPPE is the continuous monitoring of your performance between re-credentialing cycles, while FPPE is a more intensive review triggered by specific concerns or when you request new privileges. Keep a running log of your case volumes by procedure type. This makes re-privileging dramatically easier and helps you demonstrate competency, especially for lower-volume procedures.

Now let me walk you through a realistic case scenario to bring all of this together. Imagine you are Dr. Sarah Chen, a fellowship-trained rhinologist who has just signed a contract to join a two-physician ENT group in a mid-sized city. Your start date is July first. You have completed a rhinology fellowship and your training included advanced sinus surgery, anterior skull base surgery, and orbital decompression. You are board eligible, planning to sit for your oral boards in the fall. Your new practice sees patients at two office locations, operates at the main community hospital and at a freestanding ambulatory surgery center, and does outreach one day per month at a critical access hospital forty-five miles away. That means you need hospital privileges at three separate facilities and payer credentialing with every insurance plan in the region.

Here is where things go wrong if you are not proactive. Dr. Chen signs her contract in April, giving her roughly ninety days before her start date. She assumes the practice's office manager will handle credentialing. The office manager starts her CAQH profile in mid-April and submits payer applications in early May. But the office manager is also handling billing, scheduling, and a dozen other tasks, and some applications sit for a week or two before being submitted. One application is returned because the malpractice insurance face sheet listed the wrong effective date. Another is delayed because the fellowship program was slow to respond to a verification request. By July first, Dr. Chen is credentialed with Medicare and one commercial payer, but she is still pending with three others, including the largest commercial plan in the area, which represents about thirty-five percent of the practice's patient volume.

Now Dr. Chen is seeing patients, but every time a patient with that pending payer comes in, the practice has a difficult choice. They can see the patient and not bill, essentially providing free care and hoping to file retroactively once credentialing is complete. They can see the patient and bill the payer, knowing the claim will be denied and planning to resubmit later. Or they can reschedule the patient, which damages the patient relationship and slows the ramp-up of the new physician's panel. None of these options is good. Meanwhile, at the hospital, Dr. Chen's privileges were submitted in May but the medical executive committee does not meet until the third Thursday of each month, and the June meeting agenda was already full. Her application is scheduled for the July meeting, which means she will not have privileges until mid-July at the earliest. For the first two weeks of her employment, she cannot operate. She is seeing clinic patients, building her schedule, but every surgical case has to wait. If she averages three surgical days per week with a typical ENT surgical mix, those two weeks of lost OR time could represent twelve to fifteen cases. At an average reimbursement of two thousand to three thousand dollars per case across a mix of sinus surgery billed under CPT 31267 for maxillary antrostomy, 31276 for frontal sinus exploration, and 31287 for nasal or sinus endoscopy with sphenoidotomy, plus tonsillectomies at CPT 42826, and septoplasties at CPT 30520, she is looking at twenty-four thousand to forty-five thousand dollars in delayed surgical revenue.

The total financial impact of poor credentialing planning for Dr. Chen over her first ninety days could easily exceed one hundred thousand dollars in delayed or lost revenue. And some of that money may never be recovered. Claims filed outside of timely filing deadlines are gone forever. Now contrast this with the right approach. In the ideal scenario, the credentialing process begins the moment the contract is signed, or even before. The practice has a dedicated credentialing coordinator, or they outsource to a credentialing service, which typically costs between three hundred and five hundred dollars per payer per application, or a flat monthly fee of around one thousand to two thousand dollars for full-service management. The CAQH profile is completed and attested within the first week. All payer applications are submitted within two weeks of the signed contract. Hospital privilege applications are submitted simultaneously. The credentialing coordinator follows up with each payer every two weeks, escalating any delays. Provisional privileges are requested at each facility to allow Dr. Chen to start operating on day one under sponsorship. By the time July first arrives, Dr. Chen is credentialed with most payers, has provisional or full privileges at all facilities, and can see and bill patients from day one.

Let us now move into the specific action items you should take to get your own credentialing house in order. First, audit your current CAQH profile right now. Log in, review every section, and make sure all information is current and accurate. Check your re-attestation date and set a calendar reminder for ninety days before it expires. Confirm that your practice addresses, phone numbers, and accepting-new-patients status are correct. This information feeds directly into provider directories, and inaccurate directory listings are one of the top patient complaints and a common reason for payer audits. Second, create a credentialing master document. This is a single file, whether digital or physical, that contains current copies of every document you will ever need for a credentialing application. Include your CV, diplomas, training certificates, board certification or eligibility letter, all state medical licenses, DEA certificate, malpractice insurance face sheet, NPI confirmation, W-9, professional references with current contact information, a chronological work history with no gaps, and explanations for any gaps in training or practice. Keep this updated in real time. When you renew your medical license, put the new copy in the file immediately. When your malpractice policy renews, update the face sheet immediately. This single habit will save you hours of scrambling every time a credentialing application comes due.

Third, know your payer mix and prioritize accordingly. Pull a report from your billing system showing the percentage of revenue from each payer. Rank them from highest to lowest. When you are doing initial credentialing or when you are adding a new practice location, submit applications to your top payers first. If Blue Cross represents forty percent of your revenue, that application goes out on day one, not day thirty. Fourth, build a credentialing timeline into every major practice change. Whether you are hiring a new physician, opening a satellite office, adding a new service line like in-office balloon sinuplasty billed under CPT 31295 and 31296, or expanding to a new surgical facility, map backward from your target start date. If you want to be operational by a certain date, payer applications need to go out at minimum one hundred twenty days before that date, and one hundred eighty days is safer. Hospital privilege applications should go out at minimum ninety days before. Fifth, consider investing in a credentialing tracking system or service. For a solo practitioner or small group, a simple spreadsheet tracking each payer, the application submission date, the current status, the follow-up date, and the effective date may be sufficient. For larger groups, there are dedicated credentialing software platforms like Modio Health, CredentialMyDoc, and Medallion that automate much of the tracking and follow-up. The return on investment is almost always positive when you consider the revenue at risk from credentialing delays.

Sixth and finally, do not forget about delegated credentialing if it is available to you. If you are joining a large health system or a physician group that has delegated credentialing agreements with major payers, this can significantly shorten your timeline. Under delegated credentialing, the health system has been authorized by the payer to credential providers on the payer's behalf. This can reduce the typical ninety to one hundred eighty day timeline down to thirty to sixty days because you are eliminating the back-and-forth between the payer and the group. Ask about this during your contract negotiations.

Let me close with the key takeaways you need to remember from today's discussion. Credentialing is about getting paid, privileging is about getting access. They are parallel processes with different timelines and different stakeholders, and you need to manage both proactively. The number one rule is to start early. Begin credentialing the moment you have a signed contract, ideally one hundred twenty to one hundred eighty days before your start date. Every week of delay is real revenue lost. Your CAQH profile is the foundation of everything. Keep it current, re-attest on time every one hundred twenty days, and treat it as a living document that requires regular maintenance. Maintain a credentialing master file with current copies of every document you need. This single organizational habit eliminates the most common cause of application delays. Know your payer mix and prioritize your highest-volume payers first. Not all credentialing applications are equally urgent, so triage based on revenue impact. And finally, track everything. Whether you use a spreadsheet, a software platform, or an outsourced service, have a system for monitoring the status of every credentialing and privileging application. The cost of tracking is trivial compared to the cost of a credentialing gap. Credentialing is not glamorous, and no one goes into ENT because they love administrative paperwork. But the physicians who manage this process well protect their revenue from day one, avoid unnecessary stress, and set their practices up for financial success. Master this process early in your career and it will pay dividends for decades. That is all for today, and I will see you in the next session.
