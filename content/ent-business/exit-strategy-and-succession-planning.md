Welcome to this session on exit strategy and succession planning for your ENT practice. This is one of the most consequential decisions you will ever make as a practice owner, and yet it is also one of the most commonly neglected. Most ENT surgeons spend decades building a thriving practice, only to realize far too late that they have no coherent plan for leaving it. The result is often a rushed sale at a fraction of the practice's true value, disrupted patient relationships, and a painful transition that could have been avoided entirely with proper planning. Today we are going to walk through every dimension of this topic, from the timeline you should be working on, to how you maximize value before a sale, to the mechanics of structuring the deal itself. Whether you are five years from retirement or twenty, the principles we cover here will serve you well. Let us get started.

At its core, exit strategy and succession planning is about answering a deceptively simple question: what happens to your practice when you stop practicing? That question branches into dozens of sub-questions. Who takes over your patients? Who buys the assets? What happens to your staff, your lease, your reputation in the community? How do you extract the maximum financial value from what you have built without burning bridges or creating legal exposure? And critically, how do you time all of this so that each piece falls into place in the right sequence?

The reason this matters so much for ENT specifically is that our specialty sits at an interesting intersection. Unlike primary care, where patients can relatively easily transition to a new provider, ENT practices often have deep surgical relationships. Patients who have had sinus surgery, tympanoplasty, or thyroidectomy with you have a long-term bond of trust. They followed up with you for years. They referred their family members. That patient panel has real, measurable economic value. According to MGMA data, a well-established ENT practice generating around two million dollars in annual collections with a single physician can be valued anywhere from sixty to one hundred percent of one year's collections, depending on the payer mix, the geographic market, and the strength of the referral base. That means you could be looking at a practice value of one point two to two million dollars, or you could be looking at a fire sale for three hundred thousand if you wait too long and let things deteriorate.

The planning horizon you should be working with is three to five years at a minimum. Five years is ideal. That gives you time to address every major lever that drives practice value, and it gives you room to recruit and develop a successor without the desperation that comes with a compressed timeline. Let me walk you through the major components of that five-year window.

In years five and four before your planned exit, the focus should be on what I call structural optimization. This is where you take a hard look at the financial infrastructure of your practice. You want clean books. You want to eliminate any personal expenses that have been running through the practice. That country club membership, the vehicle lease, the family cell phone plan billed to the practice. These are common in physician-owned businesses, and they are perfectly legal, but they suppress your reported earnings and make the practice look less profitable than it actually is. A buyer, whether that is a private equity group, a hospital system, or a young associate, is going to value your practice based on reported earnings. Specifically, they are going to look at your adjusted EBITDA, which stands for earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. For a solo ENT practice doing two million in collections, you want your adjusted EBITDA to be in the range of five hundred thousand to seven hundred fifty thousand dollars. That is twenty-five to thirty-seven percent of collections, which aligns with MGMA benchmarks for well-run ENT practices. If your EBITDA is sitting at three hundred thousand because you have been running personal expenses through the business, you are literally leaving hundreds of thousands of dollars on the table when it comes time to sell.

During this same period, you should be auditing your payer contracts. Many ENT practices are sitting on contracts that have not been renegotiated in years. If your commercial payer reimbursement for a septoplasty, CPT 30520, is sitting at eight hundred dollars when the Medicare rate is around six hundred and the commercial average in your market should be closer to eleven hundred, you are underperforming. Renegotiating even two or three major contracts can increase collections by ten to fifteen percent without seeing a single additional patient. That increase flows directly to your bottom line and directly to your practice valuation.

You also want to look at your coding and documentation practices. Are you capturing all the complexity of what you do? A functional endoscopic sinus surgery, CPT 31256 for maxillary antrostomy, should almost always be billed with appropriate additional codes when you address the frontal recess, the sphenoid, or the ethmoid. CPT 31267 for frontal sinus exploration, CPT 31287 for sphenoidotomy, CPT 31255 for anterior ethmoidectomy. Many ENT surgeons under-code out of habit or conservative anxiety. A coding audit at this stage can identify tens of thousands of dollars in annual revenue that you have been leaving on the table. And again, that revenue improvement translates directly into a higher practice valuation.

Now let us talk about years three and two before exit. This is when succession recruitment becomes the primary focus. Finding the right associate to eventually take over your practice is not something you can do in six months. You need to identify, recruit, vet, and onboard someone who not only has the clinical skills but also the temperament and business orientation to run a practice. The typical timeline for an associate-to-partner track in ENT is two to three years. During that period, the associate builds their own patient panel, learns your referral network, develops relationships with your referring primary care doctors and pediatricians, and proves that they can generate revenue at a level that justifies an ownership stake.

From a financial standpoint, you should expect to guarantee the associate a salary in the range of three hundred fifty thousand to four hundred fifty thousand dollars in year one, depending on your market. MGMA median total compensation for ENT in 2024 was around five hundred ten thousand, so you need to be competitive. The associate should be generating collections of at least three times their compensation by the end of year two. If they are not, you either have a productivity problem or a market problem, and both need to be addressed before you can hand over the keys.

One critical nuance here is the concept of a buy-in structure. You want to create an arrangement where the associate buys into the practice over time, typically through a combination of sweat equity and actual capital contributions. A common structure is to offer a twenty-five percent ownership stake after year two, with an option to purchase the remaining seventy-five percent over the following three to five years. The valuation at each step should be based on a formula agreed upon in advance, usually a multiple of trailing twelve-month EBITDA. For ENT practices, that multiple typically ranges from point eight to one point five, depending on the market, the payer mix, and the ancillary revenue streams.

Speaking of ancillary revenue, this is a major value driver that deserves its own discussion. ENT practices with in-office CT scanning, audiology services, allergy testing and immunotherapy, and hearing aid dispensing command significantly higher valuations than practices that are purely surgical. In-office CT, which typically bills under CPT 70486 for sinus CT or 70480 for temporal bone CT, can generate one hundred fifty to two hundred fifty thousand dollars in annual revenue with relatively low overhead once the equipment is paid off. Allergy immunotherapy, billed under CPT 95165 for antigen preparation and 95117 for injection administration, can generate two hundred to four hundred thousand per year with proper volume. These ancillary streams are attractive to buyers because they are recurring, predictable, and less dependent on the individual surgeon's hands. A practice doing two million in surgical and clinical collections plus five hundred thousand in ancillary revenue is going to command a much higher multiple than a practice doing two million in pure surgical work.

Now in the final year before your exit, the focus shifts to deal structure and legal mechanics. This is where the distinction between an asset sale and a stock sale becomes critically important, and I want to make sure you understand this clearly because it has enormous tax implications.

In an asset sale, the buyer purchases specific assets of the practice. That includes the patient charts, the equipment, the accounts receivable, the name and goodwill, and the payer contracts. The corporate entity itself, whether it is an S corp, a C corp, or an LLC, remains with you. This is the structure most buyers prefer because they get a stepped-up tax basis in the assets they acquire, which means they can depreciate those assets all over again, generating tax deductions. For you as the seller, however, an asset sale can be less favorable because a portion of the proceeds will be taxed as ordinary income rather than capital gains. Specifically, any amount allocated to personal goodwill, which is the value associated with your name and reputation, will typically be taxed at capital gains rates, currently fifteen to twenty percent for most physicians. But amounts allocated to equipment and supplies will be subject to depreciation recapture at ordinary income rates, which could be thirty-two to thirty-seven percent. And any amount allocated to a non-compete agreement or consulting arrangement will be taxed as ordinary income.

In a stock sale, the buyer purchases your ownership interest in the corporate entity itself. Everything transfers, including liabilities. This is generally more favorable for sellers from a tax perspective because the entire gain is typically treated as long-term capital gains. However, buyers are less enthusiastic about stock sales because they inherit any unknown liabilities, including potential malpractice claims, tax issues, or compliance problems. In practice, most ENT practice sales end up as asset sales, with the seller negotiating for a larger allocation to personal goodwill to minimize their tax burden.

Let me give you a concrete example. Suppose your practice is valued at one point five million dollars. In an asset sale, the allocation might look something like this: four hundred thousand to equipment and tangible assets, six hundred thousand to personal goodwill, three hundred thousand to a covenant not to compete, and two hundred thousand to a consulting agreement for a two-year transition period. Under this allocation, the six hundred thousand in personal goodwill would be taxed at capital gains rates, saving you roughly one hundred twenty thousand in taxes compared to ordinary income treatment. The equipment portion would be subject to depreciation recapture, and the non-compete and consulting portions would be taxed as ordinary income. Your after-tax proceeds might end up around one point one million. In a stock sale with the same one point five million valuation, nearly the entire gain could be taxed at capital gains rates, potentially saving you an additional fifty to one hundred thousand dollars. But getting a buyer to agree to a stock sale usually requires significant representations and warranties, indemnification provisions, and sometimes an escrow holdback of ten to fifteen percent of the purchase price for twelve to eighteen months after closing.

Let us now walk through a realistic case scenario that ties all of these concepts together. Imagine Dr. Sarah Chen, a fifty-seven year old ENT surgeon in a mid-sized Midwestern city. She has been in solo practice for twenty-two years. Her practice generates two point three million in annual collections. She has two audiologists, an allergy nurse, and a staff of eight. Her payer mix is thirty percent Medicare, forty-five percent commercial, fifteen percent Medicaid, and ten percent self-pay. She has in-office CT and a robust allergy immunotherapy program. Her lease has six years remaining on a building she does not own. Her adjusted EBITDA, after normalizing for personal expenses, is about six hundred eighty thousand dollars. She wants to retire at sixty-two.

Dr. Chen starts her exit planning at fifty-seven, giving herself a full five-year runway. In year one, she hires a healthcare accountant to clean up her books. They identify eighty-five thousand dollars in personal expenses running through the practice, including a vehicle lease, a home office deduction that is questionable, and some travel that was more personal than professional. Removing those increases her reported EBITDA to seven hundred sixty-five thousand. She also renegotiates her three largest commercial payer contracts, resulting in a twelve percent increase in commercial reimbursement. That adds approximately one hundred twenty thousand in annual collections, bringing her total to two point four two million and her adjusted EBITDA to approximately eight hundred forty thousand.

In year two, she begins recruiting for an associate. She works with a physician recruitment firm and after four months identifies Dr. James Park, a fellowship-trained rhinologist completing his training at a university program. She offers him a two-year guarantee of four hundred thousand dollars with a productivity bonus above one point two million in collections, plus a clear path to partnership. Dr. Park starts in July and begins building his panel. By the end of his first year, he is generating eight hundred thousand in collections. The practice total is now three point two million.

In year three, Dr. Park hits one point three million in collections. Dr. Chen offers him a twenty-five percent buy-in at a price based on the agreed-upon formula, which is one times trailing twelve-month practice EBITDA. The practice EBITDA is now approximately one point one million with two providers. Twenty-five percent of one point one million is two hundred seventy-five thousand. Dr. Park finances this through a physician practice loan from a regional bank at six percent interest over seven years. He is now a twenty-five percent partner.

In year four, Dr. Chen begins reducing her clinical schedule from five days to four, then to three. She transitions her established patient relationships to Dr. Park, introducing him personally in joint clinic sessions. She sends letters to her top fifty referring physicians, introducing Dr. Park as her successor and endorsing his clinical abilities. She also brings in a practice management consultant to prepare a formal valuation for the remaining seventy-five percent sale.

In year five, the final transaction occurs. The remaining seventy-five percent of the practice is valued at eight hundred twenty-five thousand dollars, reflecting a slight discount for the transition risk. The sale is structured as an asset sale with the following allocation: two hundred thousand to equipment, four hundred thousand to personal goodwill, one hundred fifty thousand to a two-year non-compete covering a thirty-mile radius, and seventy-five thousand to a one-year consulting agreement where Dr. Chen will be available by phone for clinical questions and will see patients one day per week during the first six months. After taxes, Dr. Chen nets approximately six hundred forty thousand from this final transaction, plus the two hundred seventy-five thousand from the earlier twenty-five percent buy-in, for a total of approximately nine hundred fifteen thousand dollars. Combined with her retirement savings, this provides a solid financial foundation for retirement.

Notice what made this work. Five years of planning. Clean financial records. A recruited successor who had time to build relationships. A phased transition that protected patient continuity. And a deal structure that balanced tax efficiency with the buyer's needs.

Now let us talk about post-sale non-compete considerations, because this is where many physicians get tripped up. Non-compete agreements in the context of a practice sale are fundamentally different from employment non-competes. When you sell a practice, the non-compete is part of what the buyer is paying for. They are paying for your patient panel and your referral relationships, and they need assurance that you are not going to set up shop across the street and take everything back. Courts generally enforce sale-related non-competes much more strictly than employment non-competes because there is clear consideration, meaning the purchase price, exchanged for the restriction.

Typical non-compete terms in ENT practice sales are two to three years in duration and fifteen to thirty miles in geographic scope. Some states, like California, have historically been hostile to non-competes, but even California courts have enforced non-competes tied to the sale of a business. You need to be realistic about this. If you are selling your practice in a city of two hundred thousand people, a thirty-mile non-compete effectively means you cannot practice ENT in that market for two to three years. If you are planning to do locum tenens work or part-time practice in retirement, you need to negotiate the non-compete terms carefully. Consider carving out specific activities, such as medical mission work, expert witness testimony, teaching, or locum tenens outside the restricted area.

Let me now give you specific action items you should be working on, regardless of where you are in your career timeline. First, get a formal practice valuation done. Even if you are fifteen years from retirement, knowing your baseline value gives you a target to work toward. A qualified healthcare valuation firm will charge between eight and fifteen thousand dollars for a comprehensive appraisal. This is money well spent. The valuation will identify your strengths and weaknesses and give you a roadmap for value enhancement.

Second, start normalizing your financial statements now. Remove personal expenses from the practice books. If you want to run personal items through the business, maintain a clear addback schedule so that any future buyer or appraiser can easily see what the true operating earnings are. Your goal is to have at least three years of clean, normalized financial statements by the time you go to market.

Third, diversify your revenue streams. If you do not have ancillary services, investigate adding them. In-office CT, allergy immunotherapy, audiology with hearing aid dispensing, and even sleep medicine services can all add significant revenue and practice value. Each of these takes twelve to eighteen months to establish and become profitable, so start early.

Fourth, protect your referral relationships. Your referring physician base is one of the most valuable assets in your practice, but it is also one of the most fragile. Document your top referring sources. Maintain those relationships actively. When the time comes to transition, those relationships need to transfer smoothly to your successor. A new graduate cannot build a two-million-dollar referral network overnight. They need your active introduction and endorsement.

Fifth, engage a healthcare attorney early in the process. Practice sales involve complex legal issues including corporate structure, tax planning, payer contract assignment, lease assignment or termination, employee transition, and regulatory compliance. You need an attorney who specializes in healthcare transactions, not your general business lawyer or your estate planning attorney. The legal fees for a practice sale typically run twenty to forty thousand dollars, but the cost of getting the structure wrong can be many multiples of that.

Sixth, have an honest conversation with your staff. Your employees will be anxious about a transition. Good staff are hard to replace, and if they leave before the sale, your practice value drops. Consider retention bonuses for key employees conditional on staying through the transition. A twenty to thirty thousand dollar retention bonus for your practice manager or lead audiologist is a small price to pay to keep the operation running smoothly during the most critical period.

Let me close with the key takeaways you need to remember from this session. Exit strategy and succession planning is a five-year process, not a five-month scramble. The single biggest driver of practice value is your adjusted EBITDA, so clean up your books and optimize your revenue well before you go to market. Recruiting a successor associate two to three years before your exit gives that person time to build relationships and gives you a built-in buyer who already knows the practice. The structure of your sale, whether asset or stock, and the allocation of the purchase price among goodwill, equipment, non-compete, and consulting fees, has a direct and significant impact on your after-tax proceeds. Non-competes tied to practice sales are enforceable and should be negotiated carefully with your post-retirement plans in mind. And finally, this is not something you do alone. You need a team that includes a healthcare accountant, a healthcare attorney, a practice valuation expert, and ideally a practice management consultant who can help you see blind spots you have been living with for years.

The physicians who get the most value from their life's work are the ones who plan deliberately and execute methodically. Do not be the surgeon who operates with precision in the OR but wings it when it comes to the business of leaving practice. Your exit deserves the same level of planning, preparation, and attention to detail that you bring to every procedure you perform. Start now, wherever you are in your career, and future you will be grateful.
