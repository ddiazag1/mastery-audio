Welcome back. Today we are tackling a topic that has fundamentally reshaped how ENT practices deliver care and generate revenue: telehealth regulations and billing. If you have been in practice for more than a few years, you remember a time when telehealth was a niche curiosity, something used mainly by rural health systems connecting patients to distant specialists. Then the COVID-19 pandemic arrived, and within weeks, telehealth went from a fringe service to a lifeline. Regulations were relaxed almost overnight, reimbursement parity was temporarily mandated, and patients discovered that many of their medical concerns could be addressed through a screen. Now, several years later, we are living in the aftermath of that massive experiment. Some of those emergency flexibilities have been made permanent, others have expired, and the regulatory landscape continues to shift. For an ENT surgeon building or growing a practice, understanding telehealth is no longer optional. It is a core competency in practice management. Done well, telehealth expands your reach, improves patient retention, reduces no-show rates, and opens revenue streams that did not exist a decade ago. Done poorly, or done without attention to the regulatory and billing details, it becomes a compliance liability and a financial drain. So let us get into the specifics.

The first thing to understand is which ENT services are actually eligible for telehealth delivery. Not everything we do translates to a virtual visit, and that is obvious when you think about it. You cannot perform a flexible laryngoscopy through a screen. You cannot examine the tympanic membrane with an otoscope over video. But a surprising number of our encounters do not require hands-on examination. Think about postoperative follow-ups after sinus surgery, where the patient is healing well and you are reviewing their symptom trajectory. Think about allergy management visits where you are adjusting immunotherapy protocols based on symptom diaries. Think about new patient consultations for straightforward complaints like chronic rhinosinusitis, where you are taking a history, reviewing outside imaging, and formulating a plan that may or may not include an in-person visit later. Medication management for conditions like vertigo, tinnitus, or chronic ear infections can often be handled virtually. Sleep apnea follow-ups, particularly CPAP compliance reviews, are natural telehealth encounters. Pre-surgical consultations where you are reviewing risks, benefits, and alternatives can be conducted effectively over video. And counseling visits, whether for hearing aid adjustment discussions or cancer surveillance check-ins where imaging has already been reviewed, all fit the telehealth model. The key principle is this: if the clinical decision you need to make does not depend on a physical examination finding that can only be obtained in person, the visit is a candidate for telehealth.

Now let us talk about the regulatory framework, because this is where many practices stumble. The foundational rule you must understand is state licensure. In the United States, the practice of medicine is regulated at the state level, and telehealth does not change that. When you provide a telehealth visit, you are practicing medicine in the state where the patient is physically located at the time of the encounter, not where your office is. So if you are licensed in Iowa and your patient drives across the border to Nebraska to visit family and calls you from Omaha for a telehealth visit, you need a Nebraska license to legally provide that care. This catches people off guard, but it is the law in most states. Before the pandemic, this was an absolute barrier. During the pandemic, many states issued emergency waivers allowing out-of-state providers to deliver telehealth. Most of those waivers have expired. What has emerged in their place is the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact, often called the IMLC. This is a voluntary agreement among participating states that creates an expedited pathway to obtaining licenses in multiple states. As of now, over forty states and territories participate in the compact. It does not give you a single license that works everywhere. Instead, it streamlines the application process so that you can obtain individual state licenses much faster and with less paperwork. For an ENT practice near a state border, and many practices are in exactly that situation, the compact is enormously valuable. The cost of obtaining additional state licenses through the compact is typically in the range of five hundred to one thousand dollars per state, plus annual renewal fees. Compare that to the revenue potential of retaining patients who move across state lines or capturing new patients in adjacent markets, and the return on investment is clear. My recommendation is that any ENT practice within fifty miles of a state border should seriously evaluate IMLC participation for at least the bordering states.

Let us move to originating site rules, which is one of the areas where the post-pandemic landscape has changed most significantly. Before COVID, Medicare had strict rules about where the patient had to be physically located during a telehealth visit. The patient had to be at a designated originating site, which was typically a clinic, hospital, or rural health center, and that site had to be in a designated rural area or a health professional shortage area. The patient's home was generally not an eligible originating site. This effectively killed telehealth for most suburban and urban ENT practices. During the pandemic, CMS waived these restrictions entirely, allowing patients to receive telehealth from their homes regardless of geographic location. Congress has extended these flexibilities multiple times, and as of the current legislative environment, patients can receive Medicare telehealth services from their home through at least the end of 2025, with strong bipartisan support for making this permanent. However, and this is critical, you must stay current on this because the authorization has been extended in short increments and could theoretically lapse. For commercial payers, the situation is more favorable. Most major commercial insurers adopted home-based telehealth during the pandemic and have maintained it. Many states have passed telehealth parity laws requiring commercial insurers to cover telehealth services at the same rate as in-person services. But parity laws vary significantly by state. Some require payment parity, meaning the same dollar amount. Others require only coverage parity, meaning the insurer must cover the service but can pay a lower rate. You need to know which type of law applies in your state, because it directly impacts your revenue per visit.

Now let us get into the billing mechanics, because this is where the money is made or lost. The single most important thing to understand is the place of service code. When you bill a telehealth visit, you use place of service code 02, which designates telehealth provided other than in the patient's home, or place of service code 10, which designates telehealth provided in the patient's home. Before 2022, the distinction mattered less, but CMS has since clarified that place of service 10 applies when the patient is in their home. Here is why this matters financially. When you bill with place of service 02, Medicare pays the facility rate, which is lower. When you bill with place of service 10, Medicare pays the non-facility rate, which is higher, because it assumes you are bearing overhead costs similar to an office visit. The difference is not trivial. For a level four established patient visit, CPT 99214, the Medicare non-facility rate is approximately one hundred and ten dollars, while the facility rate is roughly seventy-five dollars. That is a thirty-five dollar difference per visit. If you are conducting even ten telehealth visits per week, that adds up to over eighteen thousand dollars per year in lost revenue if you are using the wrong place of service code. So make sure your billing team understands: when the patient is at home, use place of service 10 and collect the non-facility rate.

The GT modifier is another billing element you need to know about. Historically, Medicare required the GT modifier to be appended to telehealth claims to indicate that the service was delivered via interactive audio and video telecommunications. CMS has since relaxed this requirement for Medicare claims, stating that the place of service code alone is sufficient to identify a telehealth service. However, and this is important, many commercial payers still require the GT modifier, and some state Medicaid programs do as well. The 95 modifier, which indicates synchronous telemedicine service rendered via real-time interactive audio and video, is used by payers who follow the AMA's CPT guidelines rather than CMS conventions. Your billing team needs a payer-by-payer reference sheet that specifies which modifier each payer requires. Getting this wrong does not just delay payment. It can trigger denials that require appeals, and appeals cost staff time, which is money.

Let us talk about audio-only visits, because this is a nuanced area. During the pandemic, CMS allowed audio-only telephone visits to be billed using standard E and M codes with the place of service code for telehealth. This was a game-changer for patients without reliable internet or video-capable devices, which is a larger population than many physicians realize. Post-pandemic, Medicare has retained audio-only coverage for certain services, but with limitations. Audio-only visits are typically reimbursed at a lower rate than video visits. For established patients, you can use CPT codes 99441 through 99443 for telephone E and M services. Code 99441 covers five to ten minutes of medical discussion, reimbursing at roughly fifteen to twenty dollars. Code 99442 covers eleven to twenty minutes, reimbursing at approximately forty to fifty dollars. Code 99443 covers twenty-one to thirty minutes, reimbursing at roughly sixty-five to seventy-five dollars. Compare those rates to a standard 99214 at one hundred and ten dollars, and you see the financial trade-off. Audio-only visits serve an important access function, but they are not a revenue growth strategy. Use them judiciously for situations where video is genuinely not feasible for the patient, and document clearly why audio-only was medically appropriate. Some payers have stricter rules, requiring an established relationship with the patient before audio-only visits are permitted or limiting the diagnoses eligible for audio-only encounters. Know your payers' policies.

For ENT-specific CPT codes in the telehealth context, you should be aware of which evaluation and management codes are most commonly used. The bread and butter of telehealth billing is the standard outpatient E and M codes: 99202 through 99205 for new patients and 99211 through 99215 for established patients. These are billed the same way as in-person visits, with the place of service code and any required modifier being the only differences. Time-based billing, which became the dominant method after the 2021 E and M documentation changes, works particularly well for telehealth because you can count the entire time spent on the patient's care on the date of the encounter, including chart review, order entry, and care coordination, not just face-to-face time. This means a twenty-five minute telehealth encounter that includes ten minutes of pre-visit chart review, a twelve-minute video visit, and three minutes of post-visit documentation and ordering can be billed as a 99214, which requires twenty to twenty-nine minutes of total time. That is a legitimate one hundred and ten dollar encounter that required no exam room, no medical assistant rooming the patient, and no physical overhead beyond your computer and internet connection.

Now let us walk through a case scenario to make all of this concrete. Imagine you are an ENT surgeon in a mid-sized city near a state border. Your practice has been growing steadily, and you have noticed a pattern. About fifteen percent of your postoperative patients miss their two-week and six-week follow-up appointments. When your staff calls to reschedule, the most common reasons are work conflicts, transportation issues, and the hassle of driving forty-five minutes each way for a ten-minute visit. You also have a cohort of allergy patients on sublingual immunotherapy who need quarterly check-ins but frequently cancel. Your no-show rate for these visits is running at twenty-two percent, which is above the MGMA median of around eighteen percent for otolaryngology. You decide to implement a telehealth program.

First, you identify your target patient populations. Postoperative follow-ups for uncomplicated sinus surgery, tonsillectomy, and thyroidectomy at the two-week and six-week marks are your primary candidates. You also include your sublingual immunotherapy patients for their quarterly management visits and your CPAP patients for compliance reviews. You estimate this represents about thirty visits per week that could shift to telehealth, roughly twenty percent of your total visit volume.

Next, you address the regulatory requirements. Your practice is twenty miles from the state border, and about twelve percent of your patients have home addresses in the neighboring state. You confirm that both states participate in the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact. You apply for licensure in the bordering state through the compact, which costs seven hundred dollars and takes about six weeks to process. You also verify that both states have telehealth parity laws. Your state has full payment parity for commercial insurers, meaning they must reimburse at the same rate as in-person visits. The neighboring state has coverage parity only, meaning insurers must cover telehealth but can set their own rates. You check your top five payers and find that four of them are paying at parity, while one pays telehealth visits at eighty-five percent of the in-person rate. That is still acceptable.

You invest in a HIPAA-compliant video platform. You have several options. Your EHR may have an integrated telehealth module, which simplifies workflow and documentation. Standalone platforms like Doxy.me offer a free tier that is adequate for low-volume use and a paid tier at around thirty-five dollars per month per provider for additional features. Enterprise solutions like Teladoc Health or Amwell cost more but offer scheduling integration, patient reminders, and analytics. You choose your EHR's integrated module because it minimizes workflow disruption and keeps the documentation in one place. The additional cost is two hundred dollars per month added to your EHR subscription.

You train your billing team on the specifics. Place of service 10 for patients at home. The 95 modifier for your two commercial payers that require it. No modifier needed for Medicare claims. Time-based documentation for all telehealth E and M encounters. You create a pre-visit checklist for your medical assistants to verify the patient's physical location at the start of each telehealth visit, because remember, you need to confirm they are in a state where you are licensed.

After six months, you review the data. Your telehealth program is now handling twenty-five visits per week, slightly below your initial estimate but growing. Your no-show rate for the patient populations you shifted to telehealth has dropped from twenty-two percent to nine percent. That is a dramatic improvement. Let us do the math on what that means financially. Previously, out of thirty potential visits per week in these categories, you were losing about seven to no-shows, so you were completing twenty-three visits. Now, with twenty-five telehealth visits per week and a nine percent no-show rate, you are losing about two, completing twenty-three visits via telehealth. But here is the key: you have freed up those in-person slots. The seven slots per week that were previously lost to no-shows are now available for new patients or procedures. If you fill even five of those seven slots with new patient visits averaging one hundred and sixty dollars each, that is eight hundred dollars per week in incremental revenue, or over forty-one thousand dollars per year. Add in the retained revenue from patients who would have been lost entirely due to the inconvenience of in-person follow-up, and the total financial impact of your telehealth program easily exceeds sixty thousand dollars annually. Your costs are the two hundred dollar per month platform fee, the seven hundred dollar one-time licensure fee, and roughly forty hours of staff training time. The return on investment is exceptional.

But there is a cautionary element to this scenario as well. Three months into your program, you receive a denial from a commercial payer for a batch of telehealth claims. The reason code indicates invalid modifier. Your billing team had been applying the GT modifier to this payer's claims, but this particular payer switched to requiring the 95 modifier at the start of the new year and sent a bulletin that your team missed. You now have thirty-two claims to resubmit with corrected modifiers. Each resubmission takes your billing staff about eight minutes, so that is over four hours of staff time, plus the cash flow delay of waiting for reprocessing. This is exactly the kind of operational detail that separates practices that succeed with telehealth from those that find it more trouble than it is worth. You implement a quarterly payer policy review process to catch these changes proactively going forward.

Let us now move to your action items, the specific steps you should take to evaluate and implement telehealth in your own practice. Step one: conduct a visit-type audit. Pull a report from your practice management system showing all visit types over the past six months. Identify which visits did not involve a procedure or a physical examination finding that required in-person assessment. Calculate what percentage of your total volume these represent. If it is less than ten percent, telehealth may not justify the setup investment. If it is fifteen percent or more, you have a strong business case.

Step two: map your patient geography. Run a report of patient home zip codes. Identify what percentage of your patients live in a different state. If that number is above five percent, prioritize obtaining licensure in that state through the Interstate Medical Licensure Compact. Also identify your patients who travel more than thirty minutes to reach your office, because these are the patients most likely to embrace telehealth and most at risk of being lost to attrition.

Step three: create a payer matrix. List your top ten payers by volume. For each one, document their telehealth policy: which services they cover via telehealth, what place of service code they require, which modifier they require, whether they pay at parity with in-person rates, and whether they have any restrictions on audio-only visits. Assign a staff member to update this matrix quarterly. This single document will prevent the majority of telehealth billing errors.

Step four: calculate your break-even point. Add up all the costs of implementing telehealth: platform fees, licensure costs, staff training time, and any equipment purchases like webcams or ring lights. Then estimate your revenue per telehealth visit based on your payer mix and the appropriate reimbursement rates. Divide your total costs by your net revenue per visit to determine how many telehealth visits per month you need to break even. For most practices, this number is surprisingly low, often fewer than twenty visits per month.

Step five: start with a pilot. Do not try to shift all eligible visits to telehealth at once. Choose one visit type, such as postoperative follow-ups for sinus surgery, and one provider if you are in a group practice. Run the pilot for three months. Track your completion rates, patient satisfaction, billing accuracy, and revenue per visit. Use this data to refine your workflows before expanding to other visit types and providers.

Step six: address the compliance fundamentals. Ensure your telehealth platform is HIPAA compliant, meaning it offers end-to-end encryption and signs a business associate agreement with your practice. Train all providers and staff on telehealth-specific documentation requirements, particularly confirming and recording the patient's physical location at the start of each visit. Establish a protocol for situations where a telehealth visit reveals the need for an in-person examination, so that patients are not left without appropriate follow-up.

Now let us wrap up with the key takeaways you need to remember. First, telehealth is a practice growth strategy, not just a convenience. It reduces no-shows, retains patients who might otherwise leave your practice, and frees up in-person capacity for higher-value services like new patient visits and procedures. Second, place of service code 10 for patients at home gets you the non-facility rate, which is significantly higher than the facility rate you get with code 02. This single billing detail can mean eighteen thousand dollars or more per year in a moderately active telehealth program. Third, state licensure follows the patient, not the provider. You must be licensed in the state where the patient is physically sitting during the visit. The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact makes multi-state licensure manageable, and any practice near a state border should invest in it. Fourth, audio-only visits serve an access purpose but reimburse at substantially lower rates than video visits. Use them when video is not feasible for the patient, but do not build your telehealth program around them. Fifth, payer policies on modifiers, covered services, and reimbursement rates vary and change frequently. A quarterly payer matrix review is essential to maintaining clean claims and maximizing revenue. And sixth, start small with a defined pilot program, measure everything, and expand based on data rather than assumptions. Telehealth done right is one of the highest return-on-investment initiatives an ENT practice can undertake, but it requires the same operational discipline as any other aspect of your practice. Take the time to get the details right, and the financial and clinical returns will follow.
