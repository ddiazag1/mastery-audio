Welcome back. Today we are going to tackle one of the most important financial metrics in your ENT practice, and that is accounts receivable management. If you have ever looked at your monthly financials and felt a vague sense of unease about how much money is supposedly owed to you but has not actually landed in your bank account, this episode is going to give you the framework to understand exactly what is going on, how to fix it, and what benchmarks you should be measuring yourself against. Accounts receivable, or A/R, is simply the total dollar amount of charges that have been billed but not yet collected. It sounds straightforward, but the management of A/R is where a huge number of ENT practices either thrive or slowly bleed revenue without ever realizing it.

Let me put this in perspective for you. According to MGMA data, the median otolaryngology practice collects somewhere around one point two to one point five million dollars per physician per year. If your days in A/R creep from thirty-five days up to fifty-five days, you are essentially floating an extra seventy-five to one hundred thousand dollars in uncollected revenue per physician at any given moment. That is money you have already earned by seeing patients, performing surgeries, and doing procedures, but it is sitting in limbo instead of funding your operations, paying your staff, or contributing to your take-home pay. Worse, the longer a claim sits in A/R, the less likely it is to ever be collected. Industry data consistently shows that collectability drops by roughly fifteen to twenty percent once a claim passes ninety days, and by the time you hit one hundred twenty days, you are looking at a fifty percent or greater chance of never seeing that money at all.

So accounts receivable management is not just an accounting exercise. It is a direct determinant of your practice's cash flow, profitability, and long-term financial health. And the good news is that the tools to manage it well are not complicated. They just require discipline, consistent processes, and an understanding of the key metrics. So let us dive in.

The foundation of A/R management is understanding aging buckets. Think of your total accounts receivable as a large pool of money, and aging buckets are how you sort that pool by how old each charge is. The standard buckets are zero to thirty days, thirty-one to sixty days, sixty-one to ninety days, and ninety-plus days. Some practices further break out the ninety-plus bucket into ninety-one to one hundred twenty and over one hundred twenty, which I actually recommend because the strategies for working those two sub-buckets are quite different.

Here is what a healthy ENT practice A/R aging distribution should look like. You want at least sixty-five to seventy percent of your total A/R sitting in the zero to thirty day bucket. That is normal and expected. Claims take time to process, and most commercial payers have a fourteen to twenty-one day adjudication cycle. The thirty-one to sixty day bucket should hold no more than fifteen to twenty percent. This bucket represents claims that may need a single follow-up or are just slightly behind normal processing. The sixty-one to ninety day bucket should be under ten percent. If claims are sitting here, something went wrong, whether it is a denial that was not worked promptly, a missing authorization, or a coding issue. And the ninety-plus bucket should absolutely be under ten percent, and ideally closer to five percent. Anything sitting here is at serious risk of becoming uncollectable, and every dollar in this bucket deserves active, aggressive attention.

Now let us talk about the single most important metric in A/R management, which is days in A/R, also called days sales outstanding. This number tells you, on average, how many days it takes from the date you bill a charge to the date you collect payment. The formula is simple. You take your total accounts receivable, divide it by your average daily charges, and that gives you your days in A/R. Your average daily charges are calculated by taking your total charges over the past three months and dividing by ninety. The MGMA benchmark for otolaryngology is around thirty to thirty-five days. If you are at thirty-five or below, you are performing well. If you are between thirty-five and forty-five, there is room for improvement but you are not in crisis. If you are above forty-five days, you have a significant revenue cycle problem that is costing you real money and needs immediate attention.

Let me make this concrete with ENT-specific numbers. Say your practice generates forty thousand dollars per day in charges across all providers. If your days in A/R is thirty-five, your total A/R balance is one point four million dollars. If your days in A/R creeps up to fifty, your total A/R balance is now two million dollars. That extra six hundred thousand dollars is money you have earned but not collected. Some of it you will eventually collect, but a meaningful percentage, perhaps ten to fifteen percent of the aged balance, will turn into write-offs. That is sixty to ninety thousand dollars in lost revenue simply because claims were not worked in a timely fashion.

Now, the single biggest driver of inflated A/R in ENT practices is denial management, or more accurately, the failure to manage denials effectively. Let me walk you through how a proper denial management workflow should function. When a claim is denied, the first thing that needs to happen is categorization. Your billing team should be categorizing every denial by type. The most common denial categories in ENT are prior authorization issues, which are especially prevalent for sinus surgery codes like CPT 31267 for maxillary antrostomy, 31276 for frontal sinus exploration, and 31287 for sphenoidotomy. These frequently require prior authorization, and if your front desk or surgical scheduler did not obtain it, or obtained it with the wrong diagnosis code or wrong CPT code, the claim will be denied. The second major category is coding errors, which includes unbundling issues where you billed separately for procedures that should be bundled, incorrect modifier usage such as failing to append modifier 50 for bilateral sinus procedures or modifier 59 for distinct procedural services, or simply using the wrong CPT code. The third category is eligibility and coverage denials, meaning the patient's insurance was not active on the date of service or the specific service is not a covered benefit. The fourth category is timely filing denials, which should be essentially zero if your billing processes are working properly, because most payers give you ninety days to file and there is no reason to miss that window.

Once denials are categorized, each one needs a clear workflow. Authorization denials should trigger a retroactive authorization request if the payer allows it, and many do within thirty days of the denial. Coding denials should go back to the provider for clarification and be resubmitted with corrected codes. Eligibility denials need coordination with the patient to obtain correct insurance information and resubmit. And timely filing denials need to be escalated immediately because if you can prove the claim was submitted on time, through electronic submission records or clearinghouse reports, you can often overturn these.

The key metric for denial management is your denial rate, which is the percentage of claims denied on first submission. The benchmark for a well-run ENT practice is under five percent. If your denial rate is above eight to ten percent, you have a systemic issue in your front-end processes, whether it is eligibility verification, prior authorization, or coding accuracy. And here is an important point. You should be tracking not just your overall denial rate but your overturn rate, which is the percentage of denied claims that you successfully appeal and get paid. A good billing team should be overturning at least fifty to sixty percent of denied claims. If your overturn rate is below forty percent, your team either is not appealing denials aggressively enough or does not have the knowledge to craft effective appeals.

Let me now address write-off policies, which are an essential and often misunderstood component of A/R management. There are two types of write-offs. Contractual write-offs, which are the difference between your billed charge and the allowed amount under your contract with each payer, are automatic and expected. These are not a problem. The write-offs you need to monitor carefully are bad debt write-offs, which represent money you expected to collect but ultimately could not. Your practice needs a clear, written write-off policy that defines when and how balances can be written off. A common and reasonable framework is that patient balances under ten dollars can be written off after a single statement cycle. Balances between ten and fifty dollars can be written off after three statement cycles and one phone call attempt. Balances between fifty and two hundred dollars should go through at least three statements, two phone call attempts, and one final notice letter before write-off. And balances over two hundred dollars should go through your full collection process, including potential referral to a collection agency, before being written off. You should also set a small balance write-off threshold for insurance balances. If a payer underpays by less than five dollars, it is often not worth the staff time to appeal. The cost of working a claim appeal, when you factor in staff time, phone hold time, and administrative overhead, is typically twenty-five to thirty-five dollars. So appealing a three-dollar underpayment is not a good use of resources.

Now let me talk about a topic that does not get enough attention, which is credit balance resolution. A credit balance exists when you have collected more than you should have for a particular service, either because of duplicate payments, overpayments from insurance, or patient overpayments. Credit balances are not free money. They represent a liability on your books, and both federal and state regulations require you to refund overpayments in a timely manner. The federal False Claims Act and the sixty-day rule under the Affordable Care Act require that identified overpayments from government payers be returned within sixty days. Failure to do so can result in significant penalties. Your practice should be running a credit balance report at least monthly and resolving every credit balance within thirty days of identification. This means either issuing a refund if the overpayment is confirmed, or reclassifying the credit if it is the result of a posting error. Sitting on credit balances is not only legally risky but it also distorts your A/R data and makes it harder to get an accurate picture of your financial position.

Now let us walk through a realistic case scenario so you can see how all of these concepts come together in practice. Imagine you are an ENT surgeon who just joined a two-physician ENT practice. You have been there for six months, and the practice manager shows you the monthly financial report. Your days in A/R is fifty-two, which is well above the thirty-five day target. Your A/R aging shows that forty-eight percent of your total A/R is in the zero to thirty day bucket, twenty percent is in the thirty-one to sixty bucket, fourteen percent is in the sixty-one to ninety bucket, and eighteen percent is in the ninety-plus bucket. Your denial rate is twelve percent, and your total A/R balance is one point eight million dollars across both physicians.

This practice has a significant A/R problem. Let us break down what is happening and how you would address it. First, the fact that only forty-eight percent of A/R is in the zero to thirty bucket, compared to the target of sixty-five to seventy percent, tells you that claims are not being submitted promptly. You dig into the data and discover that the practice is averaging five to seven days from date of service to claim submission. That is too slow. Best practice is forty-eight hours or less. The delay is happening because the physicians are not completing their operative reports and office notes promptly, which delays coding, which delays billing. The fix here is straightforward. Implement a forty-eight hour documentation completion policy and track compliance weekly.

Second, the twelve percent denial rate is more than double the benchmark. You pull a denial report and find that forty percent of denials are for prior authorization issues, predominantly for sinus surgery. The practice is booking sinus cases and assuming the surgical scheduler will handle the prior auth, but there is no tracking system and no verification that the auth was obtained before the surgery date. The fix is to implement a prior authorization tracking log, require documented proof of authorization at least forty-eight hours before any scheduled surgery, and hold the case if the auth is not confirmed. Another thirty percent of denials are for coding errors, specifically for unbundling issues on sinus surgery. The practice has been billing CPT 31267 and 31256, which is maxillary antrostomy with removal of antral mucosal lining, together on the same claim without modifier 59, and payers are denying 31256 as bundled. You need to review your coding practices with your billing staff and potentially bring in an ENT-specific coding consultant for a half-day training session. This would cost you perhaps one thousand to fifteen hundred dollars but could recover tens of thousands in denied claims.

Third, the eighteen percent of A/R in the ninety-plus bucket represents approximately three hundred twenty-four thousand dollars. You run an aging detail report and find that about one hundred forty thousand dollars of this is from claims that were denied and never appealed. Another ninety thousand dollars is patient balances that have been through the statement cycle but never escalated to collections. And the remaining ninety-four thousand dollars is a mix of old credits, small balances, and claims in various stages of limbo. You need to do an A/R cleanup project. Assign your best billing person to work the ninety-plus bucket exclusively for the next four to six weeks. Set a goal of touching every account over one hundred dollars with a specific action, whether that is an appeal, a corrected claim, a patient phone call, or a write-off with proper documentation. For the small balances under twenty-five dollars that are over one hundred twenty days old, batch-write them off according to your write-off policy. For the credit balances, issue refunds within thirty days.

Let me tell you what happens when you do this work. In a real practice that implemented these changes, days in A/R dropped from fifty-one to thirty-six over a four-month period. The denial rate dropped from eleven percent to four point five percent. And the practice collected an additional two hundred ten thousand dollars in the first six months, most of which came from working the aged A/R bucket and reducing denials. That two hundred ten thousand dollars was money that had already been earned. It was sitting there waiting to be collected. It just needed a systematic process to go get it.

Now let me give you your action items. These are specific steps you can take in your own practice starting this week. Action item number one. Ask your practice manager or billing manager for three reports. Your current days in A/R, your A/R aging by bucket, and your denial rate by category for the past six months. If they cannot produce these reports, that itself is a problem that needs to be addressed, because you cannot manage what you cannot measure. Action item number two. Compare your days in A/R to the thirty-five day benchmark. If you are above forty days, request a meeting with your billing team to understand the root causes. Ask specifically about claim submission lag time, denial follow-up processes, and patient collection procedures. Action item number three. Review your write-off policy. If your practice does not have a written, documented write-off policy, create one using the thresholds I described earlier. Every write-off should require documentation of the reason and appropriate approval. Write-offs over a certain threshold, say five hundred dollars, should require physician or administrator approval. Action item number four. Run a credit balance report. If your practice has more than ten thousand dollars in unresolved credit balances, prioritize resolving those within the next thirty days. Remember, government payer overpayments have a sixty-day refund requirement, and the penalties for non-compliance are severe. Action item number five. If your denial rate is above seven percent, consider bringing in an ENT-specific revenue cycle consultant for an assessment. A good consultant will cost you three to five thousand dollars for a comprehensive review but can identify tens of thousands in recoverable revenue and process improvements that will pay dividends for years.

Let me wrap up with your key takeaways for today. First, your target for days in A/R is thirty-five or below. Every day above thirty-five represents real money sitting uncollected, and the longer it sits, the less likely you are to ever see it. Second, your A/R aging should show at least sixty-five percent of balances in the zero to thirty day bucket and less than ten percent in the ninety-plus bucket. If your aging distribution is inverted or skewed toward older buckets, you have a systemic collection problem. Third, denial management is not optional. A twelve percent denial rate versus a five percent denial rate on a practice generating three million dollars in annual charges is the difference between three hundred sixty thousand dollars and one hundred fifty thousand dollars in denied claims. Even if you recover sixty percent of those denials, you are still leaving tens of thousands of dollars on the table with a high denial rate. Fourth, every practice needs a written write-off policy with defined thresholds, required documentation, and appropriate approval levels. Ad hoc write-offs without documentation are a compliance risk and a revenue leak. And fifth, credit balances are a legal liability, not a windfall. Resolve them monthly, issue refunds promptly, and keep your books clean.

Accounts receivable management is not glamorous work, but it is the difference between a practice that is financially healthy and one that is slowly losing money it has already earned. Master these fundamentals, hold your billing team accountable to the benchmarks we discussed, and you will see a measurable impact on your bottom line. I will see you in the next session.
