Let's master magnetic resonance sequences and their selection for the oral boards.

The examiner will hand you an image and ask what sequence it is. Then the harder question: what would you order next, and why. Fluency here separates the candidate who understands imaging from the candidate who memorized a few pictures. Let's build the physics lightly, then the practical decision rules that actually get tested.

Begin with identifying the sequence, because you cannot discuss anything until you can name what you are holding. The fastest discriminator is the cerebrospinal fluid. On T1 weighted images, cerebrospinal fluid is dark and white matter is bright relative to grey matter. On T2 weighted images, cerebrospinal fluid is bright and grey matter is brighter than white matter. On fluid attenuated inversion recovery, cerebrospinal fluid is suppressed and therefore dark, while pathologic fluid such as edema remains bright. So the rule is simple: dark fluid with bright white matter is T1, bright fluid is T2, and dark fluid with bright lesions is fluid attenuated inversion recovery. Say this to yourself before you say anything to the examiner.

A useful second check is fat. Fat is bright on T1 and intermediate to bright on conventional T2. So look at the orbital fat and the marrow of the clivus. If the fat is bright and the fluid is dark, you are looking at T1. This is reliable even when the brain itself looks unfamiliar.

Have a minimal amount of physics ready, because a good examiner will probe whether you understand the pictures or merely recognize them. A magnetic resonance image is built from the behaviour of hydrogen protons placed in a strong magnetic field and then tipped by a radiofrequency pulse. T1 is the longitudinal relaxation time, the recovery of magnetization along the main field; tissues with short T1, such as fat, recover fast and appear bright. T2 is the transverse relaxation time, the decay of coherence in the transverse plane; tissues with long T2, such as water, stay coherent longer and appear bright. Weighting is controlled by the repetition time and the echo time: short repetition and short echo times give T1 weighting, long repetition and long echo times give T2 weighting, and long repetition with short echo gives proton density weighting. If asked why a lesion is bright, answer in those terms rather than by pattern alone.

Now go sequence by sequence and understand what each one is for.

T1 weighted imaging is your anatomy sequence. It gives the crispest grey white differentiation and is the substrate for contrast enhanced imaging. Intrinsically bright material on a non-contrast T1 is a short differential worth memorizing: fat, subacute blood in the methemoglobin phase, melanin, high protein content, and certain mineralization such as calcium in some concentrations and manganese. So a bright lesion on pre-contrast T1 should immediately prompt you to say dermoid, subacute hemorrhage, melanoma metastasis, or proteinaceous cyst such as a Rathke cleft cyst or a colloid cyst. That single list wins points.

Distinguish spin echo from gradient echo while you are here, because it explains several findings you will be asked about. Spin echo uses a refocusing pulse that cancels out static field inhomogeneity, so it is relatively insensitive to susceptibility effects and gives clean anatomy. Gradient echo omits that refocusing pulse, so local field distortions persist and blood products, calcium and metal bloom into exaggerated signal voids. That is precisely why gradient echo and its modern descendant susceptibility weighted imaging are the sequences you request when hunting microhaemorrhage, and why they are the worst sequences near the skull base, the sinuses and any metallic hardware.

T2 weighted imaging is your pathology sequence. Nearly everything abnormal in the brain contains more water than normal tissue, and more water means brighter on T2. Edema, gliosis, infarct, demyelination, and most tumors are T2 hyperintense. The important exception is the T2 hypointense lesion, which is a genuinely useful discriminator because it implies high cellularity, hemorrhage, calcification, or flow. A densely cellular tumor such as a lymphoma or a medulloblastoma is often relatively dark on T2 because the high nuclear to cytoplasmic ratio leaves little free water. Say that out loud when you see it.

Fluid attenuated inversion recovery is T2 with the cerebrospinal fluid signal nulled. This matters enormously at interfaces. A periventricular or cortical lesion that is buried in adjacent bright fluid on T2 becomes conspicuous on fluid attenuated inversion recovery. Use it for periventricular disease, cortical lesions, subarachnoid pathology, and for defining the non-enhancing infiltrative margin of a glioma. Bright subarachnoid space on fluid attenuated inversion recovery is abnormal and means blood, infection, high protein, carcinomatosis, or supplemental oxygen, and that differential is a frequent examiner question.

Diffusion weighted imaging measures the Brownian motion of water. Restricted diffusion is bright on the diffusion weighted image and dark on the corresponding apparent diffusion coefficient map. You must always state both, because the diffusion weighted image alone carries T2 signal through, producing the T2 shine through artifact where a bright diffusion weighted image simply reflects underlying T2 brightness rather than true restriction. So the correct phrasing is: there is restricted diffusion, bright on diffusion weighted imaging with corresponding low signal on the apparent diffusion coefficient map. The differential for true restriction in neurosurgical practice is short and high yield: acute infarct, the purulent contents of an abscess, epidermoid cyst, hypercellular tumor such as lymphoma or a high grade small round blue cell tumor, and some subsets of acute hemorrhage. The abscess point is the one the examiner cares about, because it is how you distinguish an abscess from a necrotic glioblastoma or a necrotic metastasis: the abscess cavity restricts, the necrotic tumor cavity generally does not.

Susceptibility weighted imaging and gradient echo detect the magnetic field inhomogeneity produced by blood products, calcium, and metal. These sequences bloom, meaning the signal void appears larger than the actual lesion. Use them to find microhemorrhage, cavernous malformations, diffuse axonal injury, superficial siderosis, and calcification. If you are asked how you would screen a patient for multiple cavernomas in a familial syndrome, the answer is susceptibility weighted imaging, because it will reveal lesions invisible on every other sequence. Susceptibility weighted imaging can also separate calcium from blood using the phase data, which is a sophisticated point worth having available.

Post contrast T1 imaging demonstrates breakdown of the blood brain barrier. Enhancement means the barrier is disrupted or the tissue has no barrier to begin with. Normal structures that enhance because they lack a blood brain barrier include the pituitary gland and stalk, the choroid plexus, the dura, the pineal gland, and the cavernous sinus. Knowing this prevents you from calling a normal enhancing structure a lesion. The pattern of abnormal enhancement carries the diagnosis: a thin, smooth, regular ring suggests an abscess, whereas a thick, irregular, nodular ring suggests a high grade tumor. A dural tail suggests a meningioma. Leptomeningeal enhancement following the sulci suggests infectious meningitis or carcinomatosis. Always request fat suppression when imaging the orbit, the skull base, or the spine after contrast, because otherwise bright fat will mask or mimic enhancement.

Know the specialised sequences by name, because asking for the right one is itself a scored behaviour. Short tau inversion recovery suppresses fat by exploiting its short T1 and is the sequence for marrow oedema, for the posterior ligamentous complex in spinal trauma, and for the orbit. Constructive interference in steady state and its equivalents produce sub-millimetre heavily T2 weighted images in which cerebrospinal fluid is bright and cranial nerves stand out as dark filaments, which is how you assess the internal auditory canal, neurovascular conflict in trigeminal neuralgia and hemifacial spasm, and the floor of the third ventricle before an endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Magnetic resonance angiography comes in time of flight form, which needs no contrast and exploits inflowing unsaturated spins but overestimates stenosis and loses slow flow, and contrast enhanced form, which is faster and better for the neck. Magnetic resonance venography interrogates the dural sinuses. Cine phase contrast imaging quantifies cerebrospinal fluid flow through the aqueduct or across a stoma, and is how you demonstrate that an endoscopic third ventriculostomy is patent.

Now the practical decision rules, which is where the examination actually lives.

For a suspected acute stroke, you want diffusion weighted imaging with the apparent diffusion coefficient map, plus a vascular study. Diffusion becomes positive within minutes, far earlier than any CT finding. For a suspected brain tumor, you want a full sequence set with and without gadolinium, including fluid attenuated inversion recovery to assess the infiltrative margin, susceptibility weighted imaging to assess for hemorrhage and calcification, and diffusion to assess cellularity. For a suspected abscess, the decisive sequence is diffusion, as discussed. For a suspected pituitary lesion, you need a dedicated pituitary protocol with thin sections through the sella in coronal and sagittal planes, dynamic contrast acquisition to catch a microadenoma that enhances later than the normal gland, and you should say the words dynamic and thin section because that is what distinguishes a pituitary protocol from a routine brain study. For epilepsy, you need a dedicated protocol with thin coronal T2 and fluid attenuated inversion recovery sequences angled perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampus, plus a volumetric T1 for morphometry, hunting for mesial temporal sclerosis or a focal cortical dysplasia. For suspected cerebrospinal fluid leak, you want high resolution heavily T2 weighted sequences and thin section CT of the skull base. For a suspected epidermoid versus an arachnoid cyst, the answer is diffusion: the epidermoid restricts, the arachnoid cyst follows cerebrospinal fluid on every sequence and does not restrict. For vestibular schwannoma, you want thin section heavily T2 weighted imaging through the internal auditory canal along with post contrast T1.

Know the timeline of hemorrhage on magnetic resonance, because it is asked constantly and it is pure memorization. Hyperacute, within the first day, is oxyhemoglobin, which is isointense to slightly bright on T1 and bright on T2. Acute, roughly one to three days, is deoxyhemoglobin, which is isointense on T1 and dark on T2. Early subacute, roughly three to seven days, is intracellular methemoglobin, bright on T1 and dark on T2. Late subacute, roughly one to several weeks, is extracellular methemoglobin, bright on both T1 and T2. Chronic, beyond several weeks, is hemosiderin and ferritin, dark on both. The memory device many candidates use tracks the T1 then T2 brightness as it goes from dark dark, to dark bright, to bright bright, to bright dark, to dark dark across the phases. Whatever device you use, be able to place a lesion in time from its signal, because the examiner will show you a bright T1 lesion and ask how old the blood is.

Understand the safety constraints, because they generate real oral board scenarios. Ask about implanted devices, particularly older pacemakers and defibrillators, cochlear implants, certain aneurysm clips, neurostimulators, and retained metallic foreign bodies, especially intraocular metal in a patient with a history of metalworking, which warrants orbital radiographs first. Know that gadolinium is relatively contraindicated in severe renal impairment because of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, so you check the glomerular filtration rate, and that macrocyclic agents have a substantially lower risk than the older linear agents. Know that gadolinium crosses the placenta and is generally avoided in pregnancy, whereas non-contrast magnetic resonance is considered acceptable. And know that the unstable patient in the intensive care unit may simply be too sick to go into the scanner, which is a legitimate and often correct answer: the examiner may be testing whether you will move a herniating patient to a magnet instead of to an operating room.

Be ready for the intraoperative and postoperative timing question, which is a favourite because it catches people out. After a craniotomy for tumour, surgically induced enhancement along the resection margin appears within days and can persist for months, so it will be mistaken for residual tumour unless you image early. The convention is to obtain the postoperative magnetic resonance within twenty four to forty eight hours of surgery, before reactive enhancement develops, and to include diffusion, because a rim of restricted diffusion around the cavity represents peri-operative ischaemic injury that will later enhance and mimic recurrence. Say the forty eight hour window explicitly. For spine surgery, the analogous problem is distinguishing recurrent disc herniation from postoperative epidural fibrosis: scar enhances homogeneously and early, whereas recurrent disc material does not enhance centrally, so contrast is mandatory in the reoperation workup.

Know the practical failure modes of the magnet as well. The examination is long, so a confused, agitated or paediatric patient may need sedation or general anaesthesia, and you should say who will manage the airway. Claustrophobia, body habitus and inability to lie flat all limit the study. Field strength matters: three tesla gives better signal and resolution, which is why epilepsy protocols favour it, but it suffers worse susceptibility artefact near bone and metal. And remember that magnetic resonance is comparatively slow and remote from resuscitation facilities, which is a clinical argument as much as a radiological one.

Finally, a word on how to handle the sequence you cannot identify. Do not guess. Describe the contrast characteristics you can see and reason forward. Say that the cerebrospinal fluid is bright and the lesion is brighter still, that this is therefore a T2 weighted or fluid attenuated inversion recovery image, and that you would want the corresponding post contrast T1 and diffusion to characterize it further. Reasoning out loud from signal characteristics to a differential is exactly the behavior being graded.

Know the artefacts, because an examiner will sometimes hand you one to see whether you chase it. Susceptibility artefact from metal, blood or air distorts and drops signal, worst on gradient echo and echo planar sequences and therefore worst on diffusion. Chemical shift artefact produces bright and dark bands at fat water interfaces along the frequency encoding direction. Motion produces ghosting along the phase encoding direction. Aliasing or wrap around folds anatomy outside the field of view onto the opposite side. Truncation or Gibbs artefact produces parallel bands near sharp interfaces and can mimic a syrinx in the cervical cord, which is a classic trap. Flow related artefact in the dural sinuses mimics thrombosis. In each case, the right answer is to name it as an artefact and say which sequence or parameter change would resolve it.

To summarize. Identify the sequence from the cerebrospinal fluid signal first. T1 is anatomy and the substrate for contrast, and intrinsic T1 brightness means fat, subacute blood, melanin, or protein. T2 is pathology, and T2 darkness means cellularity, blood, calcium, or flow. Fluid attenuated inversion recovery unmasks lesions next to fluid. Diffusion distinguishes abscess from necrotic tumor and detects stroke within minutes, and you must always cite the apparent diffusion coefficient map. Susceptibility weighted imaging finds blood, calcium, and cavernomas. Post contrast T1 shows barrier breakdown, and the ring pattern tells you the diagnosis. Match the protocol to the question being asked, and state the safety screen before you order anything.
