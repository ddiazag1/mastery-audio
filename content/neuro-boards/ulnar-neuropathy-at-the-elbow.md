  - label: Clumsy hand in a builder
    stem: A 44-year-old bricklayer reports six months of numbness in the small and ring fingers and increasing clumsiness with fine tasks. He sleeps with his elbows bent. Let's master ulnar neuropathy at the elbow for the oral boards.

This is the second most common entrapment neuropathy and it is a better examination topic than carpal tunnel syndrome because the anatomy is harder, the localization is genuinely challenging, and the operative decision is contested. The examiner can ask you to localize the lesion along the entire course of the nerve, and they can push you on the choice between simple decompression and transposition, where reasonable surgeons disagree. Let's build the anatomy first, because localization is the whole game.

The ulnar nerve arises from the medial cord of the brachial plexus, carrying fibers principally from the eighth cervical and first thoracic roots. In the upper arm it runs medially, then pierces the medial intermuscular septum at roughly eight centimeters above the medial epicondyle, passing from the anterior to the posterior compartment, sometimes beneath a fibrous band called the arcade of Struthers. Note carefully: the arcade of Struthers is a fascial band over the ulnar nerve in the distal arm, and it is entirely distinct from the ligament of Struthers, which is associated with a supracondylar process and median nerve compression. Examiners enjoy this confusion, so keep them separate.

The nerve then passes behind the medial epicondyle in the retrocondylar or ulnar groove, which is the most common site of compression and the site where the nerve is most superficial and most vulnerable to external pressure. It then enters the cubital tunnel proper, whose roof is the cubital tunnel retinaculum, also called Osborne ligament or the arcuate ligament, spanning between the medial epicondyle and the olecranon, and whose floor is the medial collateral ligament of the elbow. Distally the nerve passes between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris, where the aponeurosis between the humeral and ulnar heads forms another potential compressive structure. So you have at least five potential sites at the elbow: the arcade of Struthers, the medial intermuscular septum, the retrocondylar groove, Osborne ligament, and the flexor carpi ulnaris aponeurosis. Naming these in order earns substantial credit.

Understand the mechanism. With elbow flexion, the cubital tunnel narrows because the retinaculum tightens and the medial collateral ligament bulges, and the nerve elongates and experiences markedly increased intraneural pressure. This is why symptoms are provoked by sustained flexion, such as talking on the phone or sleeping with the elbow bent, and it is also the rationale for transposition, which places the nerve anterior to the axis of rotation and shortens its course.

Now the innervation, which is the key to localization. In the forearm the ulnar nerve supplies flexor carpi ulnaris and the ulnar half of flexor digitorum profundus, that is, the profundus tendons to the ring and small fingers. Then two important sensory branches arise in the distal forearm, proximal to the wrist: the dorsal ulnar cutaneous branch, which supplies the dorsal ulnar aspect of the hand, arising roughly five to eight centimeters proximal to the wrist, and the palmar cutaneous branch. These pass outside Guyon canal. In the hand, the ulnar nerve supplies the hypothenar muscles, the interossei, the third and fourth lumbricals, adductor pollicis, and the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis.

Here is the localization rule you must state. If the dorsal ulnar cutaneous distribution is numb, the lesion is proximal to the wrist, meaning at the elbow or above, because that branch leaves before Guyon canal. If dorsal sensation is spared and only the palmar ulnar digits are involved, the lesion is at the wrist in Guyon canal. This is the exact counterpart of the thenar sparing rule in carpal tunnel syndrome, and it is the highest yield fact in the topic.

Know Guyon canal and its zones, because the examiner may take you there. The canal is bounded by the pisiform medially, the hook of the hamate laterally, the transverse carpal ligament as the floor, and the volar carpal ligament as the roof. Zone one is proximal to the bifurcation and produces mixed motor and sensory deficits. Zone two involves the deep motor branch and produces pure motor deficits with intrinsic wasting and preserved sensation. Zone three involves the superficial sensory branch and produces pure sensory deficits. Causes include ganglion cysts, which are the most common, hook of hamate fractures, ulnar artery thrombosis in the hypothenar hammer syndrome, and repetitive pressure as in cyclists.

Now the clinical presentation at the elbow. Patients report numbness and tingling in the small finger and the ulnar half of the ring finger, elbow pain radiating distally, weakness of grip and of fine manipulation, and clumsiness. Ask specifically about elbow trauma, prior fracture, arthritis, prolonged leaning on the elbow, occupational posture, and sleeping position.

Examine systematically. Sensation in the ulnar digits, both palmar and dorsal, is the localizing step. Inspect for intrinsic wasting, most visible as guttering of the first dorsal interosseous space and hypothenar flattening. Test the intrinsics.

Know the eponymous signs and what they mean. The Froment sign: ask the patient to pinch a piece of paper between thumb and index finger. With a weak adductor pollicis, the patient compensates by flexing the thumb interphalangeal joint using the median innervated flexor pollicis longus, producing visible thumb flexion. The Jeanne sign is the compensatory hyperextension of the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint during the same maneuver. The Wartenberg sign is persistent abduction of the small finger due to weakness of the third palmar interosseous, which cannot counteract the unopposed pull of the extensor digiti minimi. Ulnar claw hand, or the benediction posture at rest, results from unopposed extrinsic flexion of the ring and small fingers with loss of the lumbrical contribution, producing metacarpophalangeal hyperextension and interphalangeal flexion.

Now state the ulnar paradox, because it is one of the most satisfying facts in peripheral nerve and it is frequently asked. A more distal lesion at the wrist produces a more pronounced claw than a proximal lesion at the elbow. The reason is that the proximal lesion also denervates the ulnar half of flexor digitorum profundus, so the deep flexors to the ring and small fingers are weak and cannot generate the interphalangeal flexion that creates the claw. The distal lesion spares those extrinsic flexors, which remain strong and unopposed, producing a dramatic deformity. So the paradox is that the more proximal, more extensive injury looks less deformed. Say it in those terms.

Provocative tests include the elbow flexion test, holding maximal elbow flexion with the wrist extended for sixty seconds, and Tinel sign at the retrocondylar groove. Also assess for nerve subluxation by palpating the nerve as the elbow is flexed and extended, because a subluxating nerve changes the operation: if the nerve dislocates over the epicondyle, simple decompression may destabilize it further and transposition is favored.

Electrodiagnostics are essential. The standard technique is the inching study or short segment incremental study across the elbow, looking for focal slowing, with a conduction velocity drop across the elbow segment of more than roughly ten meters per second compared with the forearm segment, or an absolute velocity below roughly fifty meters per second across the elbow, along with conduction block or a significant amplitude drop. Perform the study with the elbow flexed at roughly seventy to ninety degrees for accurate surface measurement of nerve length. Electromyography of the first dorsal interosseous, abductor digiti minimi, and flexor carpi ulnaris helps establish the level; involvement of flexor carpi ulnaris supports a lesion at or above the elbow, although the fascicles to flexor carpi ulnaris are often relatively spared, so a normal flexor carpi ulnaris does not exclude an elbow lesion. Ultrasound demonstrating nerve enlargement at the elbow and dynamic assessment of subluxation is increasingly used and is worth mentioning.

The differential includes eighth cervical or first thoracic radiculopathy, which would involve the medial forearm sensation supplied by the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve, a branch of the medial cord that is not part of the ulnar nerve. So numbness of the medial forearm points proximal to the ulnar nerve, to the lower trunk or medial cord or the root. That is an excellent discriminator to volunteer. Also consider thoracic outlet syndrome, Pancoast tumor involving the lower trunk, brachial plexopathy, motor neuron disease when there is painless progressive wasting without sensory findings, and polyneuropathy.

Management begins conservatively for mild to moderate disease without significant weakness or wasting. Activity modification to avoid sustained flexion and direct pressure, nocturnal extension splinting or simply wrapping a towel around the elbow to prevent flexion during sleep, and elbow pads. Steroid injection is not recommended here, unlike carpal tunnel, because of limited benefit and the risk of injury to a superficial nerve. Give conservative management a genuine trial of roughly three months.

Surgical indications are failure of conservative management, and more urgently the presence of intrinsic muscle wasting, significant weakness, or electrodiagnostic evidence of axonal loss, because those findings predict incomplete recovery and argue against continued observation.

Now the operative debate, which is the heart of the examination question. In situ decompression divides Osborne ligament and the fascial constrictions without moving the nerve. It is faster, preserves the nerve's blood supply and its bed, can often be done under local anesthesia, and avoids the devascularization inherent in mobilization. Anterior transposition moves the nerve in front of the medial epicondyle, and is subcategorized as subcutaneous, intramuscular, or submuscular. Transposition eliminates the traction and compression that occur with flexion and is indicated when the nerve subluxates, when there is significant elbow deformity such as a cubitus valgus from an old fracture with tardy ulnar palsy, when there is a bony abnormality or heterotopic ossification, and in revision surgery. Medial epicondylectomy is a third option that decompresses and effectively transposes without mobilizing the nerve as extensively, at the cost of potential elbow instability and medial elbow pain.

The evidence, including randomized trials and meta-analyses, shows broadly equivalent clinical outcomes between simple decompression and transposition, with transposition carrying a higher complication and wound morbidity rate. Therefore the defensible position, and the one to articulate, is that simple in situ decompression is the preferred initial operation for the typical idiopathic case, and that transposition is reserved for subluxation, deformity, prior trauma, or revision. Stating the evidence and then giving a reasoned, conditional preference is exactly what scores.

Technical points to mention. Protect the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve branches crossing the operative field, because injury produces a painful neuroma and is the most common cause of postoperative dissatisfaction. Decompress completely both proximally, including the intermuscular septum and any arcade of Struthers, and distally between the heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. If you transpose, you must excise the medial intermuscular septum, or you simply create a new compression point where the nerve angles over it, which is a classic cause of failed transposition. Ensure the transposed nerve lies in a tension free, kink free path through the full arc of motion, and check it intraoperatively.

Complications include incomplete decompression, medial antebrachial cutaneous neuroma, new compression at the intermuscular septum after transposition, nerve devascularization, hematoma, infection, elbow stiffness, and persistent symptoms from irreversible axonal loss. Counsel the patient that pain and paresthesia usually improve substantially, but that established intrinsic wasting may not recover, which is the honest and correct thing to say.

Grade the severity formally, because it guides the operative decision and the prognosis you give. The McGowan classification is the one to quote: grade one is sensory symptoms and paraesthesia without measurable motor weakness; grade two is intrinsic weakness without visible wasting, sometimes subdivided by severity; and grade three is intrinsic wasting with severe weakness. The practical consequence is that grade one disease deserves a genuine conservative trial, grade two warrants surgery without prolonged delay, and grade three predicts incomplete recovery no matter what you do, which is the counselling point patients most need to hear before they consent.

Understand the mechanical numbers behind the syndrome. Intraneural pressure in the cubital tunnel rises several fold with elbow flexion, and rises further with simultaneous shoulder abduction and wrist extension. The nerve also elongates by roughly five millimetres across the joint in full flexion. That is why symptoms are nocturnal and positional, why the elbow flexion test works, and why a simple night time extension splint, or a towel wrapped around the elbow to prevent flexion during sleep, is genuinely effective rather than a token measure. It is equally the rationale for transposition, which shortens the excursion by moving the nerve anterior to the axis of rotation.

Add imaging to the workup, because it has become standard in good practice. High resolution ultrasound shows an increased cross sectional area of the ulnar nerve at the medial epicondyle, typically above roughly ten square millimetres, and uniquely allows dynamic assessment: you can watch the nerve subluxate over the epicondyle as the elbow flexes, which directly changes the operation you choose. Ultrasound also identifies structural causes that mandate a different approach, including a ganglion, an osteophyte, heterotopic ossification, an anconeus epitrochlearis accessory muscle, or a nerve sheath tumour. Magnetic resonance shows T2 hyperintensity and enlargement of the nerve and denervation oedema in the intrinsic muscles. Saying that you would obtain ultrasound to assess for subluxation and structural pathology before deciding between decompression and transposition is a strong, current answer.

Be specific about the operation. For in situ decompression, use a posteromedial incision centred between the olecranon and the medial epicondyle, identify and protect the branches of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve crossing the field, and release the nerve continuously: the deep fascia proximally, the intermuscular septum and any arcade of Struthers, Osborne ligament over the retrocondylar groove, and the aponeurosis between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris distally. Then flex and extend the elbow and watch the nerve; if it subluxates over the epicondyle after release, you have created instability and should transpose. For subcutaneous transposition, mobilise the nerve with its accompanying vessels, excise the medial intermuscular septum completely, and secure the nerve anteriorly with a fascial sling that does not constrict it, confirming a smooth, kink free path through a full arc of motion. Submuscular transposition places the nerve beneath the flexor pronator mass and is generally reserved for revision, for very thin patients in whom a subcutaneous nerve would be exposed, and for throwing athletes.

Emphasise the failure modes, because the examiner will construct the unhappy patient. The most common cause of persistent symptoms is incomplete decompression, most often of the flexor carpi ulnaris aponeurosis distally or the intermuscular septum proximally. After a transposition, failure to excise the intermuscular septum creates a new, sharper compression point exactly where the nerve angles over it, which is the classic iatrogenic cause of a failed operation. Injury to the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve produces a painful neuroma over the medial forearm and is the commonest cause of postoperative dissatisfaction even when the ulnar symptoms resolve. Perineural scarring, devascularisation from over aggressive circumferential mobilisation, haematoma, and elbow stiffness complete the list. For revision surgery, submuscular transposition with thorough neurolysis, and occasionally a vascularised adipofascial flap to provide a fresh gliding bed, are the salvage options.

Quote the evidence on the central controversy accurately. Randomised trials and meta analyses comparing simple in situ decompression with anterior transposition show broadly equivalent clinical outcomes, with transposition carrying higher rates of wound complications and a more extensive dissection. That supports a default of in situ decompression, with transposition reserved for demonstrated nerve subluxation, significant post traumatic deformity such as cubitus valgus with tardy ulnar palsy, intra articular or bony pathology, and revision. Presenting the evidence and then giving a conditional, reasoned preference is precisely what earns marks; asserting a single approach as always correct does not.

Finally, counsel realistically. Pain and paraesthesia improve in the large majority. Strength improves more slowly and less completely. Established intrinsic wasting, clawing and first dorsal interosseous atrophy frequently persist, because the axons have already been lost and the denervated intrinsics sit at the far end of a long regeneration distance. For the patient with a fixed severe deficit, discuss tendon transfers for clawing and for key pinch rather than promising that decompression will restore the hand.

To summarize. Five compression sites at the elbow, with the retrocondylar groove and Osborne ligament being the common ones. Dorsal ulnar cutaneous involvement localizes proximal to the wrist; sparing localizes to Guyon canal. Medial forearm numbness localizes proximal to the ulnar nerve entirely. Remember the ulnar paradox. Confirm with inching studies across the elbow. Treat conservatively for three months unless there is wasting or axonal loss. Choose simple decompression by default and transpose for subluxation, deformity, or revision, and always excise the intermuscular septum when you transpose.
