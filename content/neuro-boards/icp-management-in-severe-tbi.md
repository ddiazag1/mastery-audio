Let's talk about ICP management in severe traumatic brain injury. This is one of the most commonly tested topics on the ABNS oral boards, and it is a scenario you will face repeatedly in clinical practice. The examiners expect you to move through this systematically, citing guidelines, knowing the evidence, and avoiding classic pitfalls. Let's build from the ground up.

Severe traumatic brain injury is defined as a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3 through 8. TBI remains the leading cause of death in people under the age of 45, and the management decisions you make in the first hours and days directly determine who lives, who dies, and who walks out of the hospital versus who goes to a skilled nursing facility. The physiology begins with the Monro-Kellie doctrine. The skull is a fixed, rigid container. Its contents are divided roughly into brain parenchyma at 80 percent of total volume, blood at 10 percent, and cerebrospinal fluid at 10 percent. Any increase in the volume of one compartment must be compensated by a decrease in another, or intracranial pressure rises. Normal ICP in adults ranges from 5 to 15 millimeters of mercury. When compensatory mechanisms are exhausted, even small additional volume increases cause exponential rises in pressure. This is the steep portion of the pressure-volume curve, and it is where patients decompensate rapidly.

Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. The Brain Trauma Foundation fourth edition guidelines from 2016 recommend targeting a CPP of 60 to 70 millimeters of mercury. Below 60 risks ischemia. Above 70 risks acute respiratory distress syndrome from the aggressive vasopressor use required to maintain it, as demonstrated by Robertson's CPP trial.

Autoregulation is the brain's ability to maintain constant cerebral blood flow across a wide range of mean arterial pressures, typically between 50 and 150 millimeters of mercury. This is the Lassen curve. In traumatic brain injury, autoregulation is impaired, sometimes focally, sometimes globally. Blood flow becomes pressure-passive, meaning it rises and falls directly with changes in blood pressure. This makes both hypotension and hypertension dangerous. A single episode of hypotension, defined as systolic blood pressure below 90, doubles mortality in severe TBI. This is one of the most important numbers to know.

Secondary injury is the preventable portion of damage that occurs after the initial impact. The primary injury is done at the moment of impact and cannot be reversed. Everything we do in neurosurgical critical care is about preventing secondary injury. The major drivers of secondary injury are hypotension, hypoxia, hyperthermia, seizures, and elevated intracranial pressure. Every intervention we discuss today targets one or more of these.

Before we move into the clinical scenario, you need to understand ICP waveform morphology. The normal ICP waveform has three components. P1 is the percussion wave, which reflects arterial pulsation. P2 is the tidal wave, which reflects brain compliance. P3 is the dicrotic wave, which reflects venous pulsation. In a normal waveform, P1 is the tallest peak. When P2 exceeds P1 in amplitude, this indicates reduced intracranial compliance. The brain is running out of compensatory reserve, and herniation may be imminent. Recognizing this waveform change is a critical early warning sign.

Now, herniation syndromes. Uncal herniation is the most common and most testable. The medial temporal lobe herniates over the tentorial edge, compressing the ipsilateral third cranial nerve. The classic progression is ipsilateral pupil dilation from parasympathetic fiber compression on the outside of the third nerve, followed by contralateral hemiparesis from cerebral peduncle compression, followed by death from brainstem compression. Central herniation occurs with bilateral supratentorial mass effect, producing bilateral pupil dilation and the Cushing triad of hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. Tonsillar herniation occurs with posterior fossa pathology as the cerebellar tonsils descend through the foramen magnum, compressing the medulla and causing respiratory arrest.

Now let's move into the clinical case. A 28-year-old man is involved in a high-speed motorcycle crash. He is found at the scene with a GCS of 6, broken down as E1, V2, M3. He is intubated in the field. He arrives at your trauma center hemodynamically stable after receiving 2 liters of crystalloid. A CT head is obtained and shows bilateral frontal contusions with surrounding edema, a right temporal contusion, diffuse sulcal effacement, compressed basal cisterns, and no midline shift. There is no surgical mass lesion that requires emergent evacuation. This is a classic severe TBI scenario with diffuse injury and elevated ICP risk, and the examiners are going to walk you through your management step by step.

The first question will be whether this patient needs an ICP monitor. The answer is yes. The Brain Trauma Foundation provides a Level IIB recommendation that ICP monitoring is indicated in patients with a GCS of 8 or less who have an abnormal CT scan. This patient has a GCS of 6 and multiple contusions with compressed cisterns. He absolutely meets criteria. For completeness, you should also know the indication for monitoring with a normal CT: if the GCS is 8 or less and the CT is normal, monitoring is still indicated if two or more of the following risk factors are present: age greater than 40, systolic blood pressure less than 90, or motor posturing on exam.

The next question is what type of monitor to place. The external ventricular drain is the gold standard. It is both diagnostic and therapeutic because it allows you to measure ICP and drain cerebrospinal fluid to treat elevated pressure. Placement is at Kocher's point: 1 centimeter anterior to the coronal suture, 2 to 3 centimeters lateral to the midline at the mid-pupillary line, directed toward the medial canthus of the ipsilateral eye in the coronal plane and toward the tragus of the ear in the sagittal plane. Target the ipsilateral frontal horn of the lateral ventricle. The catheter typically reaches the ventricle at a depth of 5 to 7 centimeters from the skin surface. The alternative is an intraparenchymal monitor such as a Codman or Camino device. These are easier to place, especially when ventricles are small or compressed, but they cannot drain CSF and cannot be recalibrated once placed. For a patient like this, the EVD is the preferred choice.

Now the examiners will test your knowledge of tiered ICP management. The threshold for treatment is an ICP greater than 22 millimeters of mercury. Note that this was changed from 20 in the fourth edition BTF guidelines in 2016, and this is a commonly tested update.

Tier 1 interventions are your first-line treatments. Start with positioning: elevate the head of bed to 30 degrees and keep the neck in neutral alignment. Head rotation compresses the jugular veins and impairs venous outflow, which raises ICP. This is simple, free, and effective.

Next is sedation and analgesia. Propofol is the preferred agent because of its rapid onset and offset, which allows intermittent neurological examination. The alternative is a combination of midazolam and fentanyl. Pain and agitation increase ICP through sympathetic activation, coughing, and straining against the ventilator. Adequate sedation is essential.

CSF drainage via the EVD is a powerful Tier 1 intervention. Most centers use intermittent drainage, opening the drain at a level of 15 to 20 centimeters of water above the tragus and closing it to measure ICP. Continuous drainage is an option but carries a risk of overdrainage and ventricular collapse. Intermittent drainage with periodic ICP measurement is the standard approach.

Osmotherapy is the pharmacologic cornerstone of Tier 1. You have two options. Mannitol 20 percent is given as an intravenous bolus of 0.25 to 1 gram per kilogram every 4 to 6 hours. Monitor serum osmolality and hold mannitol if osmolality exceeds 320 milliosmoles per kilogram or if the osmolal gap exceeds 10. The mechanism is an osmotic gradient that draws water from brain parenchyma into the intravascular space. Mannitol also has a rheological effect, reducing blood viscosity and improving microcirculatory flow. The alternative is hypertonic saline. For acute herniation, 23.4 percent hypertonic saline at a dose of 30 milliliters is given through a central venous catheter. For ongoing ICP management, a continuous infusion of 3 percent sodium chloride targeting a serum sodium of 145 to 155 milliequivalents per liter is commonly used. In head-to-head comparisons, there is no clear superiority of one agent over the other. However, hypertonic saline has a theoretical advantage in hypotensive patients because it provides volume expansion, whereas mannitol causes diuresis and can worsen hypotension. This is a critical distinction: never give mannitol to a hypotensive patient.

Fever control is a Tier 1 priority. Target normothermia at 36 to 37 degrees Celsius. Every 1 degree Celsius above 37 increases cerebral metabolic rate, increases ICP, and worsens outcomes. Use acetaminophen and cooling blankets aggressively.

Seizure prophylaxis rounds out Tier 1. Levetiracetam or phenytoin for 7 days reduces early post-traumatic seizures. The BTF guidelines are clear that prophylaxis prevents early seizures but does not prevent late post-traumatic epilepsy. Do not continue prophylaxis beyond 7 days unless the patient has a documented seizure.

If ICP remains above 22 despite these Tier 1 measures, you escalate to Tier 2. Mild hyperventilation targets a PaCO2 of 30 to 35 millimeters of mercury. The mechanism is cerebral vasoconstriction, which reduces cerebral blood volume and thereby reduces ICP. This is important: do not hyperventilate below a PaCO2 of 30. Aggressive hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction severe enough to produce ischemia, and it worsens outcomes. The BTF explicitly recommends avoiding PaCO2 below 30. Additionally, the effect of hyperventilation wanes within 12 to 24 hours as CSF bicarbonate equilibrates and the pH normalizes. Use hyperventilation as a bridge to definitive treatment, not as a sustained therapy.

Neuromuscular blockade is the next Tier 2 intervention. Cisatracurium or vecuronium as a continuous infusion eliminates coughing, straining, and ventilator dyssynchrony, all of which cause transient but dangerous ICP spikes. The tradeoff is that you lose the ability to perform a neurological examination while the patient is paralyzed. You must rely on ICP monitoring and continuous EEG to follow the patient's status.

CPP optimization is also a Tier 2 consideration. If the MAP is insufficient to maintain a CPP of 60 to 70, vasopressors are indicated. Norepinephrine is the preferred agent. However, do not target a CPP above 70. The Robertson trial demonstrated that aggressive CPP augmentation above 70 increases the risk of ARDS with no improvement in neurological outcomes.

If intracranial hypertension remains refractory despite Tier 1 and Tier 2 interventions, you have reached Tier 3, and the examiners will want a detailed discussion of your options.

Decompressive craniectomy is the primary Tier 3 intervention. The bone flap must be large, at least 12 by 15 centimeters as a frontotemporoparietal flap. This is critical. Small bone flaps are inadequate and actually worsen outcomes by causing brain fungation through an insufficient opening. An expansion duraplasty is mandatory. You must open the dura widely and place a dural graft to allow the brain to expand.

Two landmark randomized controlled trials define the evidence. DECRA was published by Cooper in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011. It studied bilateral decompressive craniectomy for diffuse TBI with ICP greater than 20 refractory to first-tier therapy for only 15 minutes. The result was that surgery reduced ICP effectively but worsened functional outcomes: 70 percent unfavorable outcome in the surgical group versus 51 percent in the medical group. This trial was widely criticized. The randomization threshold was too early, requiring only 15 minutes of refractory ICP. The bone flaps used were smaller than what many surgeons consider adequate. And patients were operated on before exhausting medical therapy. DECRA is best understood as evidence that premature decompression without adequate medical optimization leads to worse outcomes.

RESCUEicp was published by Hutchinson in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016. It studied decompressive craniectomy for ICP greater than 25 refractory to Stage 1 and Stage 2 treatments for 1 to 12 hours. The results showed that surgery dramatically reduced mortality: 26.9 percent in the surgical group versus 48.9 percent in the medical group. However, the surgery group had a higher rate of vegetative state: 8.5 percent versus 2.1 percent. At 12 months, more patients in the surgical group achieved favorable outcomes. The interpretation is that decompressive craniectomy saves lives, but some of those survivors have severe disability. This is a nuanced discussion the examiners expect you to navigate. The bottom line for boards: DECRA showed bad outcomes with early surgery; RESCUEicp showed that appropriately timed surgery after exhausting medical therapy saves lives.

Barbiturate coma is the other Tier 3 option. Pentobarbital is loaded at 5 to 10 milligrams per kilogram intravenously over 30 minutes, followed by a continuous infusion of 1 to 3 milligrams per kilogram per hour. The target is burst suppression on continuous EEG. Barbiturates profoundly reduce ICP by suppressing cerebral metabolic rate and reducing cerebral blood flow. However, the side effect profile is severe: systemic hypotension requiring vasopressor support, immunosuppression increasing infection risk, paralytic ileus, and cardiac depression. Barbiturate coma renders the neurological examination meaningless for the duration of therapy. This is a last resort, used before or sometimes instead of decompressive craniectomy depending on clinical circumstances.

Therapeutic hypothermia targeting 32 to 34 degrees Celsius was once considered a potential Tier 3 option. However, the BTF does not recommend it for ICP control. Two major randomized trials settled this question. Eurotherm, published by Andrews in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2015, showed no benefit from hypothermia and suggested possible harm. The POLAR trial, published by Cooper in 2018, similarly showed no benefit. Hypothermia for ICP control in TBI is not supported by evidence.

Now let's address the trap questions, because the examiners will test you with these.

First scenario: ICP is 25, you are draining CSF, but the ventricles are slit on CT. What do you do? Slit ventricles mean CSF drainage has been maximized and is no longer an effective option. You escalate to Tier 2 with hyperventilation and neuromuscular blockade. You should also consider whether the contusions have expanded and obtain a repeat CT. If ICP remains refractory, you move to Tier 3.

Second scenario: Would you hyperventilate to a PaCO2 of 25 for persistent ICP elevation? The answer is no. Aggressive hyperventilation below a PaCO2 of 30 causes cerebral ischemia and worsens outcomes. The BTF explicitly recommends against it. The only exception is a brief period of hyperventilation to a PaCO2 of 28 to 30 as a temporizing bridge during acute herniation while you are preparing for definitive treatment such as osmotherapy bolus or emergent decompressive craniectomy. This is minutes, not hours.

Third scenario: The patient has bilateral fixed dilated pupils and an ICP above 40. Is this patient salvageable? This is not brain death until you have excluded all confounders. Sedation, hypotension, and hypothermia can all produce fixed dilated pupils. You must ensure these confounders are addressed before declaring the examination reliable. Young patients can survive even bilateral fixed dilated pupils from herniation if treated rapidly and aggressively with osmotherapy, brief hyperventilation, and emergent decompressive craniectomy. Do not give up on a 28-year-old based on a single examination in the setting of active resuscitation.

Let's close with the key evidence summary. The Brain Trauma Foundation fourth edition guidelines from 2016 are the foundational reference. DECRA by Cooper in the NEJM 2011 showed decompressive craniectomy performed too early worsened outcomes. RESCUEicp by Hutchinson in the NEJM 2016 showed appropriately timed decompressive craniectomy reduced mortality. Eurotherm by Andrews in the NEJM 2015 showed therapeutic hypothermia provided no benefit. The POLAR trial by Cooper in 2018 confirmed that hypothermia does not help. Robertson's CPP trial showed that targeting CPP above 70 increases ARDS risk. And BEST TRIP by Chesnut in the NEJM 2012 compared ICP monitoring to clinical and imaging-based management and found no difference in outcomes. That trial is controversial because it was conducted in Bolivia where monitoring resources differed from North American centers, and the standard interpretation is that ICP monitoring remains the standard of care in the developed world despite that trial.

Here are your board pearls. ICP greater than 22 is the treatment threshold, not 20. CPP target is 60 to 70. Mannitol serum osmolality ceiling is 320. Never hyperventilate below a PaCO2 of 30. DECRA equals bad outcomes from premature surgery. RESCUEicp equals lives saved with appropriately timed surgery. Hypertonic saline over mannitol in the hypotensive patient. EVD is the gold standard monitor because it is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Large bone flap for decompressive craniectomy, at least 12 by 15 centimeters, with expansion duraplasty. Barbiturate coma is a last resort with severe hemodynamic consequences. Hypothermia does not work for ICP control. BEST TRIP does not mean you should abandon ICP monitoring. And never give up on a young patient with bilateral fixed pupils until you have excluded confounders and attempted maximal therapy.

That covers ICP management in severe TBI. You're ready for this one.
