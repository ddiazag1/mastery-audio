Let's master pediatric cerebrovascular disease for the oral boards.

Children are not small adults in cerebrovascular terms, and the examiner uses this topic to find out whether you know that. The lesions are different, the presentations are different, and the treatment thresholds are different. Three entities dominate: vein of Galen malformation, moyamoya disease and syndrome, and pediatric arteriovenous malformation, with pediatric aneurysm and arterial ischemic stroke rounding out the picture. Let's take them in turn.

Start with vein of Galen malformation, which is the most distinctive lesion in pediatric neurovascular disease and the one most likely to be presented as a neonatal emergency.

Understand the embryology first, because the name is misleading and the examiner may test exactly that. This is not a malformation of the vein of Galen. It is an arteriovenous fistula draining into the persistent embryonic median prosencephalic vein of Markowski, which is the precursor of the vein of Galen and which fails to regress because of the high flow through it. So the correct statement is that the true vein of Galen never forms; the dilated structure is a persistent embryonic vessel. Saying this precisely marks you as someone who knows the lesion.

The arterial supply comes from choroidal arteries, the anterior and posterior choroidal arteries, and from the anterior cerebral and posterior cerebral circulations, often with contributions from the subfornical and thalamoperforating vessels. Two angioarchitectural subtypes are described: the choroidal type, with a diffuse network of multiple small feeders, which presents earlier and more severely, and the mural type, with one or a few direct fistulous connections into the wall of the vein, which presents later and is more amenable to treatment.

The presentation is strongly age dependent, and this is the key organizing principle. In the neonate, the dominant problem is not neurologic at all; it is cardiac. The enormous arteriovenous shunt diverts a large fraction of the cardiac output, producing high output congestive heart failure, which can be severe and refractory, with cardiomegaly, pulmonary hypertension, and multiorgan failure. A neonate may also show a cranial bruit, which is a wonderful physical sign to mention. In the infant, the presentation shifts to macrocephaly and hydrocephalus, which occurs through venous hypertension impairing cerebrospinal fluid absorption rather than through simple aqueductal compression, and this matters enormously for management. In the older child, the presentation is developmental delay, seizures, hemorrhage, and the consequences of chronic venous hypertension, including the devastating melting brain syndrome of progressive parenchymal loss.

Now the management principles, which contain two counterintuitive points the examiner is hunting for.

First, treatment is endovascular. Transarterial embolization, usually staged, is the standard of care, and open surgery has essentially no role and is associated with historically dismal outcomes. Say that plainly.

Second, and this is the crucial one: do not shunt the hydrocephalus. Because the hydrocephalus is driven by venous hypertension rather than by obstruction, placing a ventriculoperitoneal shunt does not address the underlying problem and can precipitate catastrophic deterioration, including intracranial hemorrhage and worsening of the venous congestion. The correct approach is to treat the fistula endovascularly, which reduces venous pressure and typically resolves the hydrocephalus. Reserve shunting for the minority in whom hydrocephalus persists after the shunt has been treated. Candidates who reflexively shunt a macrocephalic infant fail this case.

Third, timing. In a neonate with manageable heart failure, the preference is to stabilize medically and delay embolization to roughly four to six months of age, when the child is larger, the vessels can accommodate catheters more safely, and the procedure is better tolerated. Emergent neonatal embolization is reserved for medically refractory heart failure, and it carries substantially higher risk. Use the Bicêtre neonatal evaluation score, which grades cardiac, cerebral, respiratory, hepatic, and renal function, to decide whether to treat emergently, to treat electively, or, in the child with catastrophic scores and evidence of established severe brain injury, to consider that treatment may be futile and to have an honest conversation with the family about comfort care. Being willing to name futility appropriately is a mark of maturity in the examination.

Now moyamoya, which is the second major pediatric entity.

Moyamoya is a progressive steno-occlusive arteriopathy affecting the terminal internal carotid artery and the proximal anterior and middle cerebral arteries, with the development of a fragile collateral network of dilated lenticulostriate and thalamoperforating vessels. The Japanese term moyamoya means puff of smoke, describing the appearance of these collaterals on angiography. Distinguish moyamoya disease, the idiopathic and often bilateral form associated with RNF213 variants and most prevalent in East Asian populations, from moyamoya syndrome, the same angiographic picture occurring in association with another condition. Name the associations: sickle cell disease, neurofibromatosis type one, Down syndrome, prior cranial irradiation particularly for optic pathway glioma or craniopharyngioma, Graves disease, and various congenital and connective tissue disorders.

Know the age dependent presentation, because it is a classic examination discriminator. Children present with ischemia: transient ischemic attacks, arterial ischemic strokes, and characteristically symptoms provoked by hyperventilation, such as crying, blowing, playing a wind instrument, or eating hot food, because hypocapnia causes vasoconstriction in a bed with no remaining reserve. Children may also present with choreiform movements, headache, and cognitive decline. Adults, by contrast, present more often with hemorrhage from rupture of the fragile collateral vessels. Pediatric ischemia, adult hemorrhage. That sentence is worth saying.

For evaluation, obtain magnetic resonance with angiography, looking for the ivy sign, which is leptomeningeal hyperintensity on fluid attenuated inversion recovery reflecting slow flow in pial collaterals, and for infarcts in watershed territories. Catheter angiography remains the gold standard and permits Suzuki grading from one through six, tracking the progression from carotid narrowing through development and then regression of the moyamoya collaterals with eventual recruitment of external carotid supply. Critically, obtain a hemodynamic study: perfusion imaging with an acetazolamide or carbon dioxide challenge, or a similar cerebrovascular reserve assessment, to determine whether the vasculature retains any capacity to dilate. Exhausted or paradoxical reserve identifies the child at highest risk and supports revascularization.

The essential management principle is that moyamoya is progressive and that medical therapy does not stop it. Antiplatelet therapy with aspirin is used to reduce thrombotic events but it does not alter the arteriopathy. Therefore surgical revascularization is the definitive treatment, and it should be offered to symptomatic children and to those with demonstrated hemodynamic compromise, and it should not be delayed, because each additional stroke causes cumulative cognitive injury.

Know the operations. Direct revascularization, typically superficial temporal artery to middle cerebral artery bypass, provides immediate augmentation of flow but requires recipient vessels of adequate caliber, which are often too small in young children, and it is technically demanding. Indirect revascularization relies on placing vascularized tissue against the brain surface and allowing neoangiogenesis to develop over weeks to months. The named techniques include encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis, laying the superficial temporal artery onto the cortical surface; pial synangiosis, a refinement in which the artery is sutured to the pia; encephalomyosynangiosis using temporalis muscle; and multiple burr holes. Indirect techniques work particularly well in children because their angiogenic capacity is robust, and pial synangiosis is the most widely used pediatric approach. Combined direct and indirect procedures are also used.

Perioperative management is where children with moyamoya are lost, and the examiner will probe it. State the principles clearly: avoid hypotension, avoid hypovolemia, maintain generous hydration before, during, and after surgery, avoid hyperventilation and maintain normocapnia because hypocapnia causes vasoconstriction, control pain and agitation aggressively since crying causes hyperventilation, maintain normothermia, and continue aspirin perioperatively in most protocols. The highest risk period for stroke is the perioperative window, and meticulous attention to these physiologic parameters is what prevents it.

Now pediatric arteriovenous malformation. Arteriovenous malformations are the most common cause of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage in children, and children are far more likely than adults to present with hemorrhage as the first symptom, with a majority presenting that way. The annual hemorrhage risk is broadly similar to adults, around two to four percent per year, but because a child has many more years of life remaining, the cumulative lifetime risk is very high, and this changes the treatment calculus decisively toward intervention. State that reasoning explicitly: a lesion with a three percent annual risk in a seven year old represents an enormous cumulative burden.

Management principles follow the adult framework, using the Spetzler Martin grading with points for size, eloquence, and deep venous drainage, and choosing among microsurgical resection, embolization as an adjunct, and stereotactic radiosurgery. The pediatric specific points to raise are these: radiosurgery in children must be weighed against the long term risks of radiation to the developing brain, including cognitive effects and secondary malignancy, and against the latency period of two to three years during which the child remains at risk; complete obliteration is the goal because partial treatment does not reliably reduce hemorrhage risk; and children have a meaningful rate of angiographic recurrence after apparently complete resection, which is essentially not seen in adults, so follow up angiography is required, typically at intervals including a study several years after treatment. That recurrence point is a genuinely pediatric fact and worth having.

Mention also vein of Galen's cousins and the other pediatric shunting lesions: dural sinus malformations and pial arteriovenous fistulas, which are high flow single hole fistulas treated endovascularly.

Pediatric aneurysms are uncommon and differ from adult aneurysms in important ways. They are more often large or giant, more often located at the internal carotid bifurcation and in the posterior circulation, more often fusiform or dissecting rather than saccular, more often associated with trauma or infection, and more common in boys. Associations include coarctation of the aorta, polycystic kidney disease, and connective tissue disorders. Treatment is individualized, and the durability of endovascular treatment over a child's long life expectancy is a genuine concern that favors considering surgical options, including bypass for complex fusiform lesions.

Finally, pediatric arterial ischemic stroke. Recognize that the etiologies are entirely different from adults: congenital and acquired heart disease, sickle cell disease, focal cerebral arteriopathy often post-varicella, arterial dissection, prothrombotic states, infection and meningitis, and metabolic disorders. Diagnosis is frequently delayed because stroke is not considered in children. For sickle cell disease, know that transcranial Doppler screening identifies children at high stroke risk based on elevated middle cerebral artery velocities and that chronic transfusion therapy substantially reduces that risk, which is a rare example of effective primary stroke prevention in children. The neurosurgical role in pediatric stroke is principally decompressive craniectomy for malignant middle cerebral artery infarction, where children tolerate and recover from decompression remarkably well, and you should have a low threshold to offer it.

Give the Bicêtre neonatal evaluation score some substance, since you have named it. It scores cardiac, cerebral, respiratory, hepatic and renal function out of a total of twenty one. A score above roughly twelve supports medical management with elective embolization later; a score between roughly eight and twelve supports emergency embolization; and a score below about eight, particularly with established severe brain injury, indicates that treatment is unlikely to yield a meaningful outcome and that comfort care should be discussed honestly with the family. Being willing to articulate that last tier, calmly and without evasion, is treated as a marker of maturity rather than defeatism.

Add the technical and follow up detail for vein of Galen malformation. Access in a neonate is challenging and may be transfemoral or, in the smallest infants, transumbilical. Embolization is transarterial with liquid embolic agents, most often n-butyl cyanoacrylate, and is deliberately staged, because abrupt complete occlusion of a high flow shunt risks perfusion breakthrough haemorrhage, venous thrombosis and acute cardiac decompensation. The goal of the first stage is to reduce the shunt enough to control heart failure, not to cure. Transvenous approaches are used selectively. Complications include haemorrhage, vessel perforation, non-target embolization with infarction, and the perfusion breakthrough phenomenon. Follow up is with serial imaging and developmental assessment; with modern staged treatment in experienced centres, a substantial majority of appropriately selected children achieve normal or near normal neurological development, which is a dramatic change from historical series and is worth stating.

Expand moyamoya with the operative specifics. Direct revascularization, usually superficial temporal artery to middle cerebral artery bypass, gives immediate augmentation but demands a recipient cortical vessel of adequate calibre, often under one millimetre in a small child, and is technically exacting. Indirect revascularization relies on placing vascularised tissue against the pial surface and allowing neoangiogenesis over weeks to months, and it exploits the robust angiogenic capacity of the paediatric brain. The named techniques to have ready are encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis, laying the superficial temporal artery with its adventitia onto the cortex; pial synangiosis, in which the artery is sutured directly to the opened pia, which is the workhorse paediatric operation; encephalomyosynangiosis using temporalis muscle; encephaloduroarteriomyosynangiosis combining these; omental transposition; and multiple burr holes, which is simple and useful for territories outside the middle cerebral distribution. Combined direct and indirect procedures are common in older children and adults.

Make the perioperative protocol explicit, because that is where these children are actually lost. Admit for intravenous hydration before surgery rather than allowing a long fast. Continue aspirin. Maintain normotension or mild hypertension and avoid any period of hypotension. Maintain strict normocapnia, because both hypocapnia from crying or hyperventilation and hypercapnia are harmful, and hypocapnia in particular causes vasoconstriction in a bed with no reserve. Maintain normothermia and adequate haematocrit. Control pain and agitation aggressively postoperatively, because a crying child hyperventilates and infarcts. Continue generous hydration for several days. The highest risk window for stroke is perioperative, and reciting this protocol demonstrates that you understand why.

Add the sickle cell pathway in more detail, since it is the one paediatric stroke syndrome with proven primary prevention. Transcranial Doppler screening measures time averaged mean maximum velocity in the middle cerebral and distal internal carotid arteries; velocities of two hundred centimetres per second or greater define high risk, and chronic transfusion to keep haemoglobin S below about thirty percent reduces first stroke risk dramatically. Hydroxyurea can substitute for transfusion in selected children after a period of stability. Children with sickle cell disease who develop a moyamoya pattern may still require revascularization, and they need careful transfusion and hydration around surgery to avoid sickling.

Broaden the paediatric stroke differential and workup. Consider congenital and acquired heart disease with paradoxical or cardioembolic stroke, focal cerebral arteriopathy of childhood often following varicella, arterial dissection after minor trauma, prothrombotic states including protein C and S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency, factor five Leiden and antiphospholipid syndrome, infection and bacterial meningitis, and metabolic disorders such as mitochondrial encephalopathy with lactic acidosis and stroke like episodes, which produces lesions that cross vascular territories. Workup includes echocardiography with bubble study, vascular imaging of the head and neck, a thrombophilia screen, and metabolic testing where the pattern fits. The neurosurgical contributions are decompressive craniectomy for malignant middle cerebral artery infarction, which children tolerate and recover from remarkably well and for which you should hold a low threshold, and management of haemorrhagic transformation.

Finally, handle paediatric aneurysms and other lesions briefly but accurately. Paediatric aneurysms are rare, more often large or giant, more often at the internal carotid bifurcation and in the posterior circulation, more often fusiform, dissecting, traumatic or infectious rather than saccular, and commoner in boys. Associations include coarctation of the aorta, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and connective tissue disorders. Because a child has decades of life ahead, the durability of treatment matters more than in an adult, which argues for considering surgical options including trapping and bypass for complex fusiform lesions, and for committing to long term angiographic follow up whatever is done. Cavernous malformations in children more often present with haemorrhage and are more often multiple and familial, and radiation induced cavernomas after treatment for a childhood brain tumour are common and should be anticipated.

To summarize. Vein of Galen malformation is a fistula into the persistent median prosencephalic vein, presents with high output cardiac failure in neonates and hydrocephalus in infants, is treated by staged transarterial embolization ideally around four to six months, and must not be shunted, because the hydrocephalus is venous in origin. Moyamoya causes ischemia in children and hemorrhage in adults, is progressive and unaffected by medical therapy, requires revascularization with indirect techniques such as pial synangiosis favored in children, and demands meticulous perioperative normocapnia, hydration, and blood pressure. Pediatric arteriovenous malformations present with hemorrhage, carry an enormous cumulative lifetime risk that favors treatment, and uniquely can recur after apparently complete resection, mandating follow up angiography.
