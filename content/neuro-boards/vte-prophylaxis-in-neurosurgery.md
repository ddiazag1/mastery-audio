Welcome back. This session covers venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in neurosurgery. This is a guaranteed oral board topic. The examiners love it because it forces you to demonstrate that you can balance two competing catastrophic risks: intracranial hemorrhage on one side, fatal pulmonary embolism on the other. You need to know the epidemiology cold, your mechanical prophylaxis protocols, the exact timing of chemical prophylaxis for each procedure type, and what to do when a patient develops a clot in the acute postoperative period. Let's build this from the ground up.

Start with epidemiology, because the numbers justify the aggressive approach. Craniotomy patients who receive no prophylaxis develop deep vein thrombosis at a rate of twenty to thirty percent. That is not a small risk. That is roughly one in four patients forming a clot in their legs after a craniotomy. Among those, pulmonary embolism occurs in one and a half to five percent, and PE remains a leading cause of preventable death in hospitalized neurosurgical patients. Now move to spinal cord injury, and the numbers get dramatically worse. SCI patients without prophylaxis develop DVT at rates up to eighty percent. That is the highest DVT rate of any hospitalized patient population. The combination of complete paralysis, venous stasis, and systemic inflammatory response creates a perfect thrombogenic storm. Traumatic brain injury falls in an intermediate risk category, with DVT rates of twenty to thirty percent without prophylaxis, similar to elective craniotomy but complicated by the fact that you often have expanding hemorrhagic contusions that make chemical prophylaxis timing more difficult. Spine surgery without SCI carries a lower but still significant risk of five to fifteen percent for DVT.

Now let's talk risk factors, because the examiners will expect you to stratify patients. The major risk factors in neurosurgical patients are immobility, malignancy, lower extremity paresis or plegia, prolonged operative time greater than four hours, dehydration, corticosteroid use, advanced age, obesity, prior history of VTE, and use of oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy. Among these, malignancy deserves special attention. Glioblastoma carries the highest thrombotic risk of any cancer. Read that sentence again. GBM has the highest thrombotic risk of any cancer, including pancreatic cancer, which most people assume holds that distinction. The incidence of VTE in GBM patients ranges from twenty to thirty percent even with prophylaxis. The mechanism is multifactorial: GBM cells express tissue factor, they release procoagulant microparticles, patients are on dexamethasone which is prothrombotic, they are frequently immobile, and they often have lower extremity weakness from corticospinal tract involvement. When you have a GBM patient, you must be thinking about VTE from the moment they hit the door.

Let's move to mechanical prophylaxis first because this is the foundation and requires no difficult decision-making. Sequential compression devices go on every single neurosurgical patient from the time of admission. There is no contraindication to SCDs in the absence of acute lower extremity DVT or severe peripheral arterial disease. SCDs work by intermittent pneumatic compression of the calf and thigh, which augments venous return, reduces venous stasis, and stimulates endogenous fibrinolysis. The evidence supporting SCDs in neurosurgery is strong. The landmark study by Turpie and colleagues showed a significant reduction in DVT with SCDs alone in neurosurgical patients. The key point for boards: SCDs go on in the preoperative holding area before induction, they stay on throughout surgery, and they remain on until the patient is fully ambulatory. Not until they take a few steps with physical therapy. Until they are independently and consistently ambulatory. Graduated compression stockings, also called TED hose, provide additive benefit when used in combination with SCDs. The evidence for stockings alone is weaker than for SCDs alone, but the combination is better than either modality individually. In practice, you use both. SCDs plus TED hose on every patient. This is the noncontroversial part of VTE prophylaxis. If an examiner asks what you do for every neurosurgical patient, the answer starts here.

Now we arrive at chemical prophylaxis, and this is where the neurosurgical dilemma lives. Every other surgical specialty starts chemical prophylaxis within twelve hours of surgery without much hand-wringing. In neurosurgery, we hesitate because the consequence of a postoperative intracranial hemorrhage is devastating, potentially fatal or permanently disabling. The fear of causing a bleed in the tumor bed, the epidural space, or around a fresh aneurysm clip has historically led many neurosurgeons to avoid chemical prophylaxis entirely. That fear is understandable but not supported by the evidence. Multiple meta-analyses, including the frequently cited work by Constantini and colleagues and by Hamilton and colleagues, have demonstrated that low-molecular-weight heparin or unfractionated heparin started twenty-four to forty-eight hours postoperatively does not significantly increase the rate of intracranial hemorrhage. The absolute increase in clinically significant hemorrhage is less than one percent across the major studies, while the reduction in DVT is approximately forty to fifty percent. The risk-benefit calculation clearly favors chemical prophylaxis.

Here is where you need to know the specific timing by procedure type, because this is exactly what the examiner will drill into. After craniotomy for tumor resection, chemical prophylaxis starts at twenty-four hours postoperatively. This timing comes from the Constantini data and the Goldhaber recommendations. Twenty-four hours. Not forty-eight. Not when you feel like it. Twenty-four hours, assuming the postoperative CT shows expected surgical changes without unexpected hemorrhage. After craniotomy for vascular pathology, meaning after aneurysm clipping or AVM resection, you are more conservative. Start chemical prophylaxis at twenty-four to forty-eight hours. The rationale for the more conservative window is that you have a freshly clipped or resected vascular lesion, and early rebleeding in this setting can be catastrophic. Most vascular neurosurgeons trend toward the forty-eight-hour mark for these cases. For traumatic brain injury, start chemical prophylaxis at twenty-four to forty-eight hours, but only after confirming stability of hemorrhagic lesions on repeat CT. This is critical. You do not start chemical prophylaxis in a TBI patient based on the clock alone. You get a repeat CT at twenty-four hours. If there is no expansion of contusions, no new hemorrhage, no expanding epidural or subdural, then you start prophylaxis. If there is an expanding lesion, you hold and reimage. For routine spine surgery, chemical prophylaxis starts at twenty-four to forty-eight hours postoperatively. The risk of epidural hematoma exists but is very low with prophylactic dosing. For spinal cord injury, these are your highest risk patients, and chemical prophylaxis starts at twenty-four to seventy-two hours, as soon as hemostasis is confirmed. Given the extraordinarily high DVT rate in SCI, you push to start as early as safely possible. Many SCI centers start at twenty-four hours if there is no ongoing surgical bleeding.

For the specific agents and dosing, you need two regimens at your fingertips. Enoxaparin, a low-molecular-weight heparin, is the most studied agent in neurosurgical VTE prophylaxis. The dose is forty milligrams subcutaneously once daily. Alternatively, unfractionated heparin at five thousand units subcutaneously every eight to twelve hours. LMWH is slightly more effective than UFH for DVT prevention based on available comparative data, but UFH has one important advantage: it is easier to reverse. Protamine fully reverses UFH but only partially reverses LMWH, approximately sixty percent neutralization. This distinction matters when you are operating on a patient who may need to return to the OR urgently. In a patient with a high risk of reoperation, some surgeons prefer UFH for this reason. For the boards, know both regimens, state that enoxaparin forty milligrams daily is the standard first-line choice, and mention the reversibility difference as a consideration for UFH selection.

Let's shift to diagnosis. When you suspect DVT, the test of choice is duplex ultrasonography of the lower extremities. Duplex ultrasound has a sensitivity of approximately ninety-five percent for proximal DVT, meaning iliac, femoral, and popliteal veins. However, the sensitivity drops significantly for isolated calf vein DVT, where it may be only fifty to seventy percent sensitive. For a suspected pulmonary embolism, CT pulmonary angiography is the gold standard. It is fast, widely available, and highly sensitive and specific. One important board pearl here: D-dimer is not useful in the postoperative neurosurgical patient. D-dimer is always elevated after surgery, after trauma, and in the setting of malignancy. A positive D-dimer in a postoperative craniotomy patient tells you nothing. Do not order it. If you have clinical suspicion for PE, go straight to CT pulmonary angiography.

Now let's address the hardest clinical scenario: treatment of a diagnosed VTE in a neurosurgical patient. This is not prophylaxis anymore. This is therapeutic anticoagulation, and it requires careful judgment. For a DVT or PE diagnosed in a patient who is more than one to two weeks out from craniotomy, the management is relatively straightforward. You confirm stability on repeat brain imaging, ensure there are no hemorrhagic complications, and start therapeutic anticoagulation. The standard approach is intravenous heparin drip titrated to a PTT of sixty to eighty seconds, then transition to either therapeutic-dose LMWH such as enoxaparin one milligram per kilogram subcutaneously every twelve hours, or a direct oral anticoagulant. Duration of anticoagulation is typically three to six months for a provoked VTE.

The genuinely difficult scenario, the one the examiners will test you on, is VTE in the acute postoperative period. When a patient develops a proximal DVT or PE within the first forty-eight to seventy-two hours after craniotomy, you cannot safely give therapeutic anticoagulation. The risk of hemorrhage into a fresh surgical bed is unacceptably high. This is where the IVC filter comes in.

IVC filter indications in neurosurgery are well-defined. First, contraindication to anticoagulation in the setting of acute proximal DVT or PE. This is the classic indication. Your patient is two days post-craniotomy for GBM, develops an acute iliofemoral DVT, and you cannot anticoagulate. Place an IVC filter. Second, failure of anticoagulation, meaning recurrent VTE despite documented therapeutic anticoagulation levels. This is less common but is an absolute indication for filter placement. Use retrievable filters whenever possible. The filter should be removed once anticoagulation can be safely initiated, ideally within several weeks. Leaving a filter in permanently creates its own set of complications: IVC thrombosis in up to ten percent, filter migration, filter fracture with embolization of strut fragments, and perforation of the IVC wall. The FDA has issued guidance recommending retrieval of temporary filters as soon as the indication for the filter has resolved.

Let's work through a clinical case to tie this together. A sixty-two-year-old woman undergoes a right frontotemporal craniotomy for a newly diagnosed GBM. The operation takes five hours. She has left lower extremity weakness, four out of five strength, from tumor involvement of the motor cortex. She is on dexamethasone ten milligrams every six hours. Postoperative CT at six hours shows expected surgical changes without hemorrhage. Walk through your VTE prophylaxis plan.

From admission, this patient has SCDs on both lower extremities and graduated compression stockings. She goes to the OR with SCDs on. Postoperatively, SCDs remain on continuously. At twenty-four hours, after confirming the stable postoperative CT, you start enoxaparin forty milligrams subcutaneously daily. This patient has essentially every risk factor: malignancy, GBM specifically, lower extremity weakness, prolonged surgery, corticosteroid use, and postoperative immobility. She is as high-risk for VTE as a neurosurgical patient can be. Chemical prophylaxis at twenty-four hours is not optional for this patient. It is mandatory.

Now on postoperative day three, the nurse calls you because the patient's left leg is swollen. You order a duplex ultrasound which confirms an acute left femoral vein DVT. What do you do? First, you are now seventy-two hours from surgery. You check the most recent head CT. If imaging shows stable postoperative changes without hemorrhage, you have a reasonable window to start therapeutic anticoagulation. You start an intravenous heparin drip, titrate to a PTT of sixty to eighty seconds, and monitor closely. You get a repeat head CT at twenty-four hours after starting the drip to confirm no intracranial hemorrhage. If the repeat CT is clean, you continue heparin and plan transition to therapeutic LMWH or a DOAC for a total treatment duration of three to six months. If at any point you see hemorrhagic changes on imaging, you stop the drip, reverse with protamine, and place an IVC filter.

Time for examiner questions. The examiner says: the patient is twelve hours post-craniotomy for GBM and develops acute massive PE with hemodynamic instability. What do you do? This is a trap question designed to see if you panic and either give systemic thrombolytics, which would be catastrophic, or freeze and do nothing. The answer is structured. First, hemodynamic resuscitation with IV fluids and vasopressors. Second, you cannot give therapeutic anticoagulation at twelve hours post-craniotomy. Third, you cannot give systemic TPA. The intracranial hemorrhage risk is near one hundred percent. Fourth, emergent IVC filter placement to prevent further embolic burden. Fifth, consider catheter-directed therapy in consultation with interventional radiology if the patient remains hemodynamically unstable despite supportive care, as catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy or low-dose catheter-directed thrombolysis targeted to the pulmonary arteries may be considered in a life-threatening scenario, understanding that even catheter-directed thrombolysis carries significant hemorrhagic risk at twelve hours post-craniotomy. Sixth, if the patient survives the acute event, reassess for therapeutic anticoagulation at forty-eight to seventy-two hours with repeat head CT. The key point is: do not give systemic thrombolytics to a patient who is twelve hours post-craniotomy. That is the trap, and the examiners want to hear you say it clearly.

Second trap question: the examiner asks, would you give prophylactic LMWH to a patient with a known, untreated, unruptured intracranial aneurysm? The answer is yes. Prophylactic-dose LMWH does not significantly increase the risk of aneurysm rupture. An untreated unruptured aneurysm is not a contraindication to prophylactic anticoagulation. The annual rupture rate of most unruptured aneurysms is well under one percent, and prophylactic-dose LMWH does not meaningfully change that risk. If this patient is undergoing a non-neurosurgical procedure, say a hip replacement, they get standard VTE prophylaxis. If they are being admitted for observation or for workup prior to planned aneurysm treatment, they get mechanical prophylaxis and chemical prophylaxis per standard protocols. The trap is that some candidates reflexively say no because they hear "aneurysm" and think anticoagulation is contraindicated. It is not. Therapeutic anticoagulation in the setting of a freshly ruptured aneurysm that has not been secured is a different question entirely. Prophylactic dosing with an unruptured aneurysm is safe.

Another question the examiner might ask: what about a patient with a fresh, unsecured, ruptured aneurysm who is awaiting treatment? For example, a subarachnoid hemorrhage patient on day of admission who has not yet been clipped or coiled. Do you give chemical prophylaxis? The answer is no. You use mechanical prophylaxis only, SCDs and TED hose, until the aneurysm is secured. Once the aneurysm has been clipped or coiled, you start chemical prophylaxis at twenty-four to forty-eight hours as discussed. The unsecured ruptured aneurysm is one of the few true contraindications to chemical prophylaxis in neurosurgery.

Let me give you the board pearls to close this out. First, SCDs go on every neurosurgical patient from admission, no exceptions, and stay on until independently ambulatory. Second, chemical prophylaxis with enoxaparin forty milligrams daily starts at twenty-four hours post-craniotomy for tumor, twenty-four to forty-eight hours for vascular cases, twenty-four to forty-eight hours for TBI after a stable repeat CT, and twenty-four to seventy-two hours for SCI once hemostasis is confirmed. Third, GBM has the highest thrombotic risk of any cancer. Treat these patients aggressively. Fourth, D-dimer is useless in postoperative patients. Go straight to duplex ultrasound for DVT and CT pulmonary angiography for PE. Fifth, IVC filter indications are contraindication to anticoagulation with acute proximal DVT or PE, and failure of anticoagulation with recurrent VTE. Always use retrievable filters and remove them when the indication resolves. Sixth, never give systemic thrombolytics to a patient in the acute post-craniotomy period. Seventh, prophylactic-dose LMWH is safe in the presence of an untreated unruptured aneurysm. Eighth, the only absolute contraindication to chemical prophylaxis in neurosurgery is an unsecured ruptured aneurysm or active intracranial hemorrhage that has not stabilized. Ninth, when starting therapeutic anticoagulation in a neurosurgical patient for diagnosed VTE, always get repeat brain imaging before and twenty-four hours after initiating treatment. Tenth, the overall message for the board examiner is that you take VTE seriously, you are not paralyzed by fear of hemorrhage, and you have a systematic protocol that balances both risks with evidence-based timing.

That covers VTE prophylaxis in neurosurgery. You're ready for this one.
