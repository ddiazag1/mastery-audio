Welcome to this session on movement disorder surgery principles. This is a high-yield topic for the oral boards and one that rewards precise, systematic thinking. We will cover the historical evolution of surgical treatment, basal ganglia circuitry, patient selection with case-based reasoning, ablative and neuromodulatory techniques, and the key evidence you need to cite. Let us begin.

To understand where we are today, you need to know where this field came from. Modern stereotactic surgery began in 1947 when Spiegel and Wycis introduced the first human stereotactic frame, enabling precise targeting of deep brain structures through a fixed coordinate system referenced to intracerebral landmarks. Through the 1950s and 1960s, Lars Leksell perfected stereotactic pallidotomy for Parkinson disease, and thalamotomy became a standard treatment for tremor. These ablative procedures were effective but carried permanent risk. A lesion that was too large or misplaced caused irreversible deficits. With the introduction of levodopa in the late 1960s, surgical treatment fell out of favor because medical therapy was so effective initially. But as clinicians recognized the long-term complications of levodopa, specifically motor fluctuations, dyskinesias, and wearing-off phenomena, interest in surgery returned. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, Alim-Louis Benabid and colleagues in Grenoble discovered that high-frequency electrical stimulation of the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus could suppress tremor without creating a permanent lesion. This was the birth of deep brain stimulation. DBS rapidly expanded to targets in the subthalamic nucleus and the globus pallidus internus, and by the 2000s it had largely replaced ablative surgery for Parkinson disease because of two fundamental advantages: adjustability and reversibility. Stimulation parameters can be modified over time to optimize benefit and minimize side effects, and the device can be turned off or removed if needed. Now, in the current era, we are witnessing a return of lesioning through a completely different approach. MRI-guided focused ultrasound, or MRgFUS, uses transcranial convergent ultrasound beams to create a precise thermal lesion deep in the brain without any incision, any implant, or any cranial opening. This represents a full circle from Leksell's pallidotomy to Benabid's DBS and now back to lesioning, but with non-invasive technology.

To make sense of surgical targets, you must have basal ganglia circuitry locked in. There are two principal pathways through the basal ganglia. The direct pathway facilitates movement. Cortical input activates striatal medium spiny neurons that express D1 dopamine receptors. These neurons project directly to the globus pallidus internus, inhibiting it. Because the GPi tonically inhibits the thalamus, inhibiting the GPi disinhibits the thalamus, which then excites the cortex and movement is facilitated. The indirect pathway suppresses movement. Striatal medium spiny neurons expressing D2 dopamine receptors project to the globus pallidus externus and inhibit it. Because the GPe normally inhibits the subthalamic nucleus, inhibiting the GPe disinhibits the STN. The STN then sends excitatory glutamatergic projections to the GPi, increasing GPi activity, which in turn increases thalamic inhibition and suppresses movement. In Parkinson disease, the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta disrupts this balance. Dopamine depletion reduces D1 activation of the direct pathway and reduces D2 inhibition of the indirect pathway simultaneously. The net effect is overactivity of the indirect pathway, which leads to excessive excitatory drive from the STN to the GPi, excessive GPi inhibition of the ventrolateral thalamus, and the clinical manifestations of bradykinesia and rigidity. DBS of the STN or GPi modulates this pathological circuit, though the precise mechanism, whether it is inhibition, excitation, disruption of pathological oscillatory activity, or information lesioning, remains debated.

Now let us anchor the anatomy precisely because the examiner will test you on this. The subthalamic nucleus sits in a critical corridor. Dorsally lies the zona incerta. Ventrally lies the substantia nigra pars reticulata. Laterally runs the internal capsule, and stimulation spreading laterally will cause contralateral motor contractions. Medially sits the red nucleus, and medial spread can cause diplopia or contralateral ataxia. The sensorimotor territory of the STN is dorsolateral, which is the target. The GPi target for Parkinson disease is the posteroventral motor territory. The anterodorsal GPi is associative and limbic territory. Stimulation in the wrong part of the GPi will not improve motor symptoms and may cause cognitive or behavioral side effects. For tremor, the ventral intermediate nucleus, or VIM, is the cerebellar-receiving nucleus of the thalamus, positioned between the ventralis caudalis nucleus posteriorly, receiving sensory input, and the ventralis oralis posterior anteriorly, receiving pallidal input. VIM receives projections from the contralateral cerebellum via the dentatorubrothalamic tract, which is the target for both DBS and focused ultrasound in essential tremor.

Let us now move to patient selection by working through three clinical scenarios. This is how the examiner will present these concepts, and you need to handle each one decisively.

Patient A is a 55-year-old with Parkinson disease diagnosed 8 years ago. He has developed significant motor fluctuations with 4 to 5 hours of off time daily and peak-dose dyskinesias. On formal levodopa challenge, his UPDRS Part III motor score improves by 50 percent from the off state to the on state. His Mini-Mental State Examination score is 29 out of 30. He has no psychiatric history.

Patient B is a 72-year-old with a 3-year history of progressive parkinsonism. He has had frequent falls beginning within the first year of symptom onset. On examination he has vertical gaze palsy, particularly impaired downgaze, prominent axial rigidity greater than appendicular rigidity, and minimal response to levodopa.

Patient C is a 40-year-old with severe generalized dystonia that began in childhood. Genetic testing confirms a DYT1 mutation, also designated TOR1A. She is wheelchair-bound despite maximal medical therapy including trihexyphenidyl, baclofen, and benzodiazepines.

Let us evaluate each patient as the examiner would probe you.

Patient A is an excellent candidate for deep brain stimulation. Walk through why. First, he has idiopathic Parkinson disease, not atypical parkinsonism. Second, he demonstrates robust levodopa responsiveness. His 50 percent improvement on the levodopa challenge exceeds the generally accepted threshold of 30 percent improvement on UPDRS-III. This is the single most important predictor of DBS outcome: what levodopa improves, DBS can improve. Third, he has motor fluctuations that cannot be adequately managed with medication adjustments, which is the typical indication for considering DBS. Fourth, his cognition is intact with an MMSE of 29. Fifth, there is no psychiatric contraindication such as active depression, psychosis, or impulse control disorder. Sixth, at 55 years old, he is at a reasonable age to benefit from decades of DBS therapy. The appropriate workup before proceeding includes a formal levodopa challenge with standardized UPDRS-III scoring in the practically defined off state, which is after overnight withdrawal of dopaminergic medications, and then in the on state after a supratherapeutic dose of levodopa. Neuropsychological testing is mandatory to identify subtle cognitive impairment that the MMSE might miss. Brain MRI is required both to rule out structural pathology and for surgical planning. A multidisciplinary team evaluation should include the movement disorders neurologist, the neurosurgeon, a neuropsychologist, and ideally a psychiatrist. If all evaluations are favorable, you proceed with bilateral DBS. The two principal targets are the subthalamic nucleus and the globus pallidus internus. STN DBS allows reduction of dopaminergic medications by approximately 50 percent, which is advantageous for patients with medication-related side effects or dyskinesias. GPi DBS provides more direct suppression of dyskinesias and may have fewer cognitive and psychiatric side effects. Both targets have comparable motor outcomes at 2 to 3 years based on the VA CSP-468 randomized trial.

Patient B is absolutely not a surgical candidate, and this is a scenario designed to test whether you will operate on someone you should not. The clinical picture is classic for progressive supranuclear palsy. The key red flags are vertical gaze palsy, especially impaired downgaze, which is nearly pathognomonic, frequent falls beginning within the first year of disease onset suggesting early postural instability, axial rigidity predominating over appendicular rigidity, and minimal levodopa response. This is atypical parkinsonism, and atypical parkinsonism does not respond to DBS. Operating on this patient is a board-failing answer. While you are discussing Patient B, the examiner may ask you to identify other forms of atypical parkinsonism. You should be prepared to describe multiple system atrophy, which presents with cerebellar ataxia in MSA-C or severe early autonomic failure in MSA-P, including orthostatic hypotension, urinary retention, and erectile dysfunction. You should describe corticobasal degeneration, which presents with markedly asymmetric cortical signs including limb apraxia, alien limb phenomenon, and cortical sensory loss. And you should describe dementia with Lewy bodies, characterized by visual hallucinations, fluctuating cognition, and REM sleep behavior disorder. None of these conditions respond to DBS. This distinction between idiopathic Parkinson disease and atypical parkinsonism is one of the most critical decision points on the boards.

Patient C is a candidate for DBS, but the target and expectations differ from Parkinson disease. For generalized dystonia, the target is the globus pallidus internus bilaterally. DYT1-positive generalized dystonia has the best surgical outcomes of any dystonia subtype, with 70 to 90 percent improvement in the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale at 1 to 3 years postoperatively. This was demonstrated in the landmark Vidailhet study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005. However, there is a critical counseling point that the examiner will expect you to articulate. Unlike Parkinson disease, where tremor can improve on the operating table during intraoperative stimulation testing, dystonia improvement is gradual, evolving over weeks to months after DBS activation. Patients and families must be warned about this timeline or they will be disillusioned. Programming for dystonia also differs from Parkinson disease. Dystonia programming often requires higher amplitude, and some protocols use lower frequency settings in the range of 60 to 80 Hz, though 130 Hz is also commonly used. Pulse width is wider than in PD settings, typically 120 to 450 microseconds compared to the 60 to 90 microsecond pulse widths common in Parkinson stimulation.

Now let us discuss ablative procedures, because the examiner will want to know when and why you would choose a lesion over a stimulator.

Unilateral pallidotomy was the historical gold standard for Parkinson disease before DBS. It remains indicated in situations where DBS is contraindicated or impractical. Consider it for patients at high risk of hardware infection, patients who cannot manage the implanted device, or patients in remote locations without access to DBS programming. The Leksell target for pallidotomy is 2 to 3 millimeters anterior to the midcommissural point, 20 to 21 millimeters lateral to the midline, and 4 to 6 millimeters below the anterior commissure to posterior commissure line. This places the lesion in the posteroventral sensorimotor GPi. A critical rule: bilateral pallidotomy is contraindicated. Bilateral lesions in the GPi carry an unacceptable risk of pseudobulbar symptoms, including severe dysarthria and dysphagia, as well as cognitive decline. If a patient needs bilateral pallidal intervention, DBS is the answer, not bilateral pallidotomy.

MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy is a transformative technology. It received FDA approval for medication-refractory essential tremor in 2016 and for tremor-dominant Parkinson disease in 2018. The procedure uses a hemispheric array of over 1,000 ultrasound transducers mounted in a stereotactic frame. The beams converge through the intact skull on the VIM target. Real-time MRI thermometry monitors tissue temperature during sonication, and clinical testing is performed during sublesional sonications to confirm tremor suppression before the final ablative temperature, typically 55 to 60 degrees Celsius, is reached. The advantages are compelling: no incision, no burr hole, no implanted hardware, no infection risk, immediate clinical effect, and the procedure is performed with the patient awake for real-time assessment. The disadvantages are equally important to know. It is unilateral only. Bilateral MRgFUS thalamotomy carries the same risk as any bilateral thalamotomy, namely permanent dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive decline. It requires an adequate skull density ratio, defined as greater than 0.4, and approximately 10 to 15 percent of patients are excluded because their skull density ratio is too low to achieve adequate thermal focusing. Unlike DBS, once the lesion is made, it cannot be adjusted. If the lesion is too small, tremor will recur. If it is too large, the patient may have permanent sensory or motor side effects. The pivotal evidence is the Elias trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016, which demonstrated a 47 percent reduction in hand tremor score compared to sham at 3 months in patients with essential tremor. At 2 years, benefit was maintained though with some attenuation.

Gamma Knife thalamotomy is an alternative for patients who are not candidates for either DBS or MRgFUS. This may include patients with a skull density ratio below 0.4 who therefore cannot undergo focused ultrasound. The typical prescription is a marginal dose of 130 to 140 Gray delivered to a single 4-millimeter isocenter placed at the VIM target. The disadvantages compared to MRgFUS are significant: the effect is delayed by weeks to months because the lesion develops gradually from radiation necrosis, the lesion size is less predictable, and there is no real-time clinical testing during the procedure. It is less precise and less preferred when other options exist, but it fills a niche for patients who cannot tolerate or access alternatives.

The examiner will almost certainly ask about microelectrode recording versus imaging-based targeting, which maps to the awake versus asleep DBS debate. In the traditional awake DBS approach, the patient is placed in a stereotactic frame, a burr hole is made, and a microelectrode is advanced toward the target. Microelectrode recording captures single-unit neuronal activity. The STN has a characteristic irregular firing pattern at 15 to 30 Hz. As the electrode enters the dorsal border of the STN, you encounter border cells with a distinctive regular firing pattern. Within the STN, sensorimotor testing confirms somatotopic organization: neurons in the dorsolateral sensorimotor territory respond to passive limb movement. Below the STN, you enter the substantia nigra, where firing rates decrease and the pattern changes. After electrophysiological mapping, macrostimulation is performed to test clinical benefit, specifically tremor reduction and rigidity improvement, and to screen for side effects such as muscle contractions from capsular spread, paresthesias from sensory thalamic spread, or diplopia from medial spread toward the oculomotor complex. This provides real-time physiological confirmation of target accuracy.

In asleep or iMRI-guided DBS, the electrode is placed under general anesthesia using direct visualization of the target on high-field MRI. The electrode position is confirmed with intraoperative imaging, and there is no physiological testing. Multiple retrospective studies, including work by Starr and colleagues, have demonstrated equivalent motor outcomes between awake MER-guided and asleep imaging-guided DBS. The advantages of asleep DBS include a better patient experience with no anxiety, no pain, and no need for the patient to remain cooperative for hours, as well as a faster procedure. The disadvantages are the absence of real-time physiological confirmation and complete reliance on anatomical targeting, which means that if the MRI introduces distortion or the anatomy is atypical, there is no electrophysiological safety net.

Let me now address several trap questions that are common on the oral boards.

First scenario. The patient has Parkinson disease, and levodopa-responsive freezing of gait is the primary complaint. Would STN DBS help? The answer is nuanced. If freezing of gait is clearly levodopa-responsive, meaning it occurs primarily in the off-medication state and resolves with levodopa, then STN DBS may improve it because STN stimulation effectively reduces off time. However, if the freezing persists in the on-medication state, the prognosis with DBS is poor. On-medication freezing of gait is one of the most treatment-resistant symptoms in Parkinson disease. For isolated gait freezing that does not respond to levodopa, the investigational target is the pedunculopontine nucleus, though PPN stimulation remains experimental and is not a standard-of-care answer on the boards.

Second scenario. Would you offer bilateral MRgFUS thalamotomy for a patient with bilateral essential tremor? No. This is a hard stop. Bilateral thalamotomy, regardless of the technique used to create the lesion, carries an unacceptable risk of permanent dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive decline. If a patient requires bilateral tremor control, DBS is the appropriate intervention because stimulation can be titrated to minimize bilateral side effects and can be turned off if complications arise.

Third scenario. A patient with Parkinson disease and concurrent dementia, with an MMSE of 18, wants DBS. How do you respond? DBS is contraindicated in patients with significant cognitive impairment. This is a firm contraindication, not a relative one. There are two reasons. First, STN DBS in particular is associated with worsening of executive function, verbal fluency, and processing speed even in cognitively normal patients. In a patient with existing dementia, these cognitive effects can be devastating. Second, the patient with dementia is unlikely to derive sufficient motor benefit to justify the surgical risk because cognitive impairment itself limits functional recovery. You must counsel the patient and family clearly that DBS is not appropriate and explore medical optimization and supportive care instead.

Let us anchor the key evidence before closing. The foundational DBS publications come from Benabid and colleagues in the late 1980s and early 1990s, initially describing VIM stimulation for tremor and subsequently extending to STN stimulation for Parkinson disease. The VA Cooperative Studies Program trial 468, or VA CSP-468, was the definitive randomized comparison of STN versus GPi DBS, demonstrating comparable motor outcomes at 24 months with differences in secondary outcomes: STN allowed greater medication reduction while GPi had fewer cognitive and behavioral adverse effects. The EARLYSTIM trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2013, demonstrated that STN DBS was superior to best medical therapy in early-stage Parkinson disease with recent onset of motor complications, expanding the window for surgical intervention. The Vidailhet study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005, established GPi DBS as effective for generalized dystonia in a randomized sham-controlled design. The Elias trial in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016 provided level-one evidence for MRgFUS VIM thalamotomy in essential tremor. And the work of Starr and colleagues provided outcomes data supporting asleep, imaging-guided DBS as equivalent to traditional awake MER-guided approaches.

Let me close with the board pearls that you must carry into the examination room. Levodopa response is the single best predictor of DBS success in Parkinson disease. If levodopa does not improve the symptom, DBS will not improve it either, with the notable exception of tremor, which sometimes responds to DBS even when partially refractory to levodopa. Atypical parkinsonism is an absolute contraindication to DBS. If the clinical picture suggests PSP, MSA, CBD, or DLB, do not operate. This is a board-failing mistake if you get it wrong. Dystonia improvement after DBS is delayed by weeks to months, unlike the immediate tremor suppression seen in Parkinson disease. Program patiently and counsel patients appropriately before surgery. Never perform bilateral lesioning of any target. This applies to thalamotomy, pallidotomy, and MRgFUS. If bilateral treatment is needed, use DBS. Cognitive impairment is a firm contraindication to DBS, not a relative one. And always remember that the multidisciplinary evaluation, including neurology, neurosurgery, neuropsychology, and psychiatry, is the standard of care before any movement disorder surgery.

That covers movement disorder surgery principles. You are ready for this one.
