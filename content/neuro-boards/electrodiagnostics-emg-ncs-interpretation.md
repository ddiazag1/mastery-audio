questions:
  - q: State the master rule that answers most electrodiagnostic questions.
    a: Demyelination slows conduction: prolonged latency and reduced velocity with relatively preserved amplitude. Axonal loss removes conducting fibres: reduced amplitude with relatively preserved velocity, because surviving fibres still conduct normally.
    probe: Why can axonal loss still slow the velocity a little?
    probeA: Because conduction velocity is measured from the fastest conducting fibres in the nerve. If axonal loss happens to remove the largest, fastest myelinated fibres, the recorded velocity falls to that of the next fastest surviving population. The slowing is modest, conventionally not below about seventy-five percent of the lower limit of normal, which is the rule of thumb that separates it from genuine demyelination. So mild slowing with a markedly reduced amplitude is still an axonal picture; marked slowing with preserved amplitude is demyelinating.
  - q: What is conduction block and what does it predict?
    a: Focal failure of transmission across a segment: a drop in amplitude of more than about fifty percent stimulating proximal versus distal to the lesion, with preserved distal responses. It is the electrophysiological signature of neurapraxia and predicts good recovery.
    probe: What is temporal dispersion and what does it suggest?
    probeA: Temporal dispersion is a prolonged, lower-amplitude but broadened proximal response, where the area under the curve is relatively preserved even though the peak amplitude falls. It happens because individual fibres conduct at differing velocities, so their contributions arrive spread out in time and the waveform disperses rather than summating sharply. It indicates segmental demyelination, and it is the reason you compare area as well as amplitude: a drop in peak amplitude with preserved area is dispersion, whereas a drop in both is genuine conduction block. Both point toward an acquired demyelinating neuropathy rather than a hereditary one, which tends to produce uniform slowing without dispersion.
  - q: Radiculopathy or plexopathy? Give me the two discriminators.
    a: Radiculopathy: denervation across muscles sharing a root but different peripheral nerves, denervation in the paraspinals because the dorsal ramus branches at the root, and a normal sensory nerve action potential because the lesion is proximal to the dorsal root ganglion. Plexopathy: abnormal sensory potentials and normal paraspinals.
    probe: Explain why the sensory potential survives in a root lesion.
    probeA: Because the sensory cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglion, which lies distal to the root lesion. A compressive radiculopathy damages the root proximal to the ganglion, so the sensory axon remains in continuity with its cell body and does not undergo Wallerian degeneration. The peripheral sensory nerve therefore continues to conduct normally even though the patient reports numbness, because the central pathway to the cord is interrupted. It is the same principle as the preserved sensory potential in a preganglionic plexus avulsion, and it is what allows electrodiagnosis to separate a root lesion from a plexus lesion.
  - q: Describe the motor unit potential changes over time after denervation.
    a: Acutely morphology is normal because surviving units have not changed. Over weeks to months, surviving axons sprout to reinnervate orphaned fibres, enlarging the unit and producing large amplitude, long duration, polyphasic potentials, the signature of chronic denervation with reinnervation. Early reinnervation gives small short polyphasic nascent units.
    probe: What do myopathic units look like instead?
    probeA: The opposite: small amplitude, short duration, polyphasic units. In a myopathy individual muscle fibres drop out of motor units, so each unit contains fewer fibres and generates a smaller, briefer potential. The polyphasia reflects desynchronised firing of the remaining fibres. The trap is that early reinnervation produces small, short, polyphasic nascent units that look similar, so the discriminator is recruitment: myopathic units recruit early and fully, whereas nascent reinnervating units recruit poorly in a muscle that is still neurogenically denervated.
  - q: Neurogenic or myopathic recruitment?
    a: Neurogenic: reduced recruitment, fewer units firing rapidly to generate force, giving an incomplete interference pattern. Myopathic: early or full recruitment, many small units recruited at low force because each contributes little.
    probe: Which conduction studies accompany each?
    probeA: In a neurogenic process, conduction studies typically show reduced compound muscle action potential amplitude with relatively preserved velocity in axonal loss, or slowing and conduction block in demyelination, and sensory potentials are abnormal in a peripheral nerve or plexus lesion and normal in a root lesion. In a myopathy, motor conduction studies are usually entirely normal, or show mildly reduced amplitude only in severe disease, and sensory studies are normal, because the nerve itself is healthy. So a patient with profound weakness, normal sensation, normal conduction studies and early full recruitment of small units is myopathic, and that combination is the pattern to recognise.
  - q: Painless progressive wasting with normal sensation. What must you exclude before operating?
    a: Motor neuron disease. Widespread denervation with fasciculations across multiple root and nerve territories and body regions, with entirely normal sensory studies. Examiners construct the patient sent for cervical decompression who actually has amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and electrodiagnosis is how you catch it.
    probe: What treatable condition mimics it, and how would you spot that?
    probeA: Multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block. It mimics motor neuron disease closely: asymmetric painless weakness and wasting, often starting in the hand, with entirely normal sensation. The way to spot it is to look specifically for motor conduction block at sites that are not usual entrapment points, which means an extended study including proximal stimulation rather than a routine one. Supporting features are the absence of upper motor neuron signs, a relatively slow course, and raised anti-GM1 antibodies in perhaps half of patients. It matters enormously because it is immune-mediated and responds to intravenous immunoglobulin, so missing it means withholding an effective treatment from a patient given a terminal diagnosis.
  - q: Name the technical pitfalls before I use one against you.
    a: Limb temperature is the big one: cooling slows conduction and prolongs latency, so warm to thirty two to thirty four degrees. Excessive stimulation spreads to neighbouring nerves. Distance measurement errors corrupt velocity, worst across short segments. And anomalous innervation such as Martin-Gruber mimics conduction block.
    probe: How does the inching study reduce one of those?
    probeA: The inching or short-segment study addresses the distance measurement error across short segments. Instead of stimulating across a long segment and dividing by a distance that is hard to measure accurately over a curved limb, you stimulate at one-centimetre intervals across the suspected lesion and look for an abrupt step change in latency or amplitude between adjacent points. That localises the lesion precisely, for example across the elbow in ulnar neuropathy, and it is far less vulnerable to a small absolute error in measured distance than a long-segment velocity calculation is.
  - q: Bound your own evidence: what can this test not do?
    a: It assesses large myelinated fibres only, so pure small fibre neuropathy gives a normal study. It is operator dependent and uncomfortable, limiting sampling. Roughly ten to fifteen percent of genuine carpal tunnel syndrome has a normal study. And an incidental abnormality in an asymptomatic nerve is not an indication to operate.
    probe: So how should the report be used?
    probeA: As an extension of the clinical examination, answering a specific question I have already formulated, and never as a screening test read in isolation. I would tell the electromyographer my hypothesis and what I want sampled, because the study is only as good as the muscles chosen. I would then interpret the result against the patient: a normal study does not exclude the diagnosis in a classic history, and an abnormality in an asymptomatic nerve is not an indication to operate. The report informs the decision; it does not make it.
cases:
  - label: The wrong operation nearly happened
    stem: A 61-year-old man is referred for cervical decompression. He has eight months of progressive painless weakness and wasting of both hands with difficulty turning keys, and MRI shows multilevel cervical spondylosis with mild cord compression at C5-6. There is no neck pain and no sensory symptoms.
    q1: What about this history is not adding up?
    a1: Painless, progressive, symmetrical wasting with no sensory symptoms does not fit a compressive radiculopathy or myelopathy, and the imaging findings are common and often incidental at this age. Sensory sparing is the discordant feature, and I would not operate on the basis of imaging that matches the population rather than the patient.
    p1: What would you look for on examination that supports your suspicion?
    pa1: Upper and lower motor neuron signs in the same region, which is the hallmark. So: wasting and fasciculations in the hands and forearms, which are lower motor neuron, alongside brisk reflexes in a wasted limb, a positive Hoffmann sign, extensor plantars and spasticity, which are upper motor neuron and should not coexist with wasting in a purely compressive radiculopathy. Then evidence of disease outside the cervical region: tongue fasciculations and wasting, dysarthria, dysphagia, a brisk jaw jerk, emotional lability, and weakness or fasciculation in the legs or trunk. And critically, a completely normal sensory examination. I would also ask about weight loss, cramps and respiratory symptoms, and check his forced vital capacity.
    q2: What do you request and what specifically do you ask for?
    a2: Electrodiagnostic studies, and I would tell the electromyographer the hypothesis: query motor neuron disease versus cervical radiculopathy, please sample multiple root and nerve territories in more than one body region including bulbar and thoracic paraspinal muscles, and assess sensory responses.
    p2: Why sample the thoracic paraspinals specifically?
    pa2: Because denervation in the thoracic paraspinal muscles is very difficult to explain by cervical spondylosis, so it is one of the most useful pieces of evidence for a diffuse anterior horn cell process. Cervical degenerative disease cannot denervate a thoracic myotome, and thoracic radiculopathy is rare. So finding fibrillations and positive sharp waves there, alongside denervation in cervical and lumbosacral segments, establishes involvement of multiple non-contiguous body regions, which is what the El Escorial and Awaji diagnostic criteria for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis require. Sampling only the cervical muscles would produce a picture entirely compatible with the MRI findings and would let the wrong diagnosis stand.
    q3: The study shows widespread denervation with fasciculations in cervical, thoracic and lumbosacral segments, and entirely normal sensory studies. What is the diagnosis and what do you do?
    a3: Motor neuron disease, not compressive myelopathy. Normal sensory studies with widespread multi-segment motor denervation is the pattern. I would refer to neurology rather than operate, because decompression will not help and surgery in this population carries real risk of accelerating decline.
    p3: How do you have that conversation with a man referred for an operation?
    pa3: Carefully, and not in a corridor. I would bring him and, if he wishes, a relative into a private room, establish what he already understands and what he has been told to expect, and warn him that the results have changed the picture. I would explain that the scan findings are common at his age and are not the cause of his weakness, that the electrical studies point to a problem in the nerve cells rather than in the neck, and that an operation would not help him and carries real risk. I would not name a terminal diagnosis I am not the right person to give: I would say that this needs a neurologist with expertise in these conditions, that I am referring him urgently, and that they will complete the diagnosis. Then I would make the referral myself that day, and make sure the referring clinician knows too.
    q4: Before you close the door on treatment, what else should you have considered?
    a4: Multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block, which mimics motor neuron disease with asymmetric painless weakness and normal sensation but is immune-mediated and treatable with intravenous immunoglobulin. It is distinguished by finding motor conduction block outside usual entrapment sites, and it is the must-not-miss in this presentation.
    p4: What else is on that list of treatable mimics?
    pa4: Cervical spondylotic myelopathy itself, which is the one I started with and which can genuinely coexist, so it must not be dismissed entirely. Then: Kennedy disease, or spinobulbar muscular atrophy, which is X-linked, slowly progressive, with gynaecomastia and perioral fasciculations and a much better prognosis. Inclusion body myositis, which causes asymmetric weakness of finger flexors and quadriceps and is myopathic on EMG. Myasthenia gravis and Lambert-Eaton syndrome, distinguished by repetitive stimulation. Hyperthyroidism and hyperparathyroidism. Lead or mercury toxicity. HIV and Lyme disease. Adult-onset spinal muscular atrophy and post-polio syndrome. And structurally, a foramen magnum or high cervical lesion, and syringomyelia, which classically produces wasted hands but with dissociated sensory loss rather than none.

Let's master electrodiagnostic interpretation for the oral boards.

Electromyography and nerve conduction studies are the laboratory of peripheral nerve surgery, and the examiner will hand you a report and ask what it means. Candidates who have never learned to read one flounder badly, which is unfortunate because the logic is simple and highly rule based. Let's build the vocabulary, then the interpretive rules, then the patterns.

Start with nerve conduction studies. A stimulating electrode depolarizes the nerve and a recording electrode captures the response. For motor studies, you stimulate the nerve and record over the muscle, obtaining the compound muscle action potential. For sensory studies, you stimulate and record over the nerve itself, obtaining the sensory nerve action potential. Three measurements matter.

Latency is the time from stimulus to response onset. It reflects the speed of the fastest conducting fibers across the segment, and it is prolonged in demyelination. Amplitude reflects the number of functioning axons and the muscle fibers they innervate, and it is reduced in axonal loss. Conduction velocity is calculated from the distance and the latency difference between two stimulation sites, and it is reduced in demyelination.

Now the master rule, which answers most questions asked of you. Demyelination slows conduction, so it prolongs latency and reduces conduction velocity while relatively preserving amplitude. Axonal loss removes conducting fibers, so it reduces amplitude while relatively preserving velocity, because the surviving fibers still conduct at normal speed. Say that rule out loud and then apply it to whatever numbers you are given.

Two refinements matter. Conduction block is a focal failure of transmission across a segment, producing a significant drop in amplitude when stimulating proximal to the lesion compared with distal to it, typically a drop of more than roughly fifty percent, with preserved distal responses. Conduction block is the electrophysiologic signature of neurapraxia and it predicts good recovery. Temporal dispersion describes a broadened, desynchronized response from differential slowing across fibers, characteristic of acquired demyelinating processes.

Know the late responses. The F wave is a late motor response produced by antidromic conduction to the anterior horn cell and a backfiring orthodromic response. Because it traverses the entire length of the nerve including the proximal segment and the root, it is useful for assessing proximal pathology, and it is classically abnormal early in Guillain Barré syndrome. The H reflex is the electrophysiologic equivalent of the ankle reflex, a true monosynaptic reflex most commonly recorded from the soleus, and it assesses the first sacral root pathway. A prolonged or absent H reflex supports an S one radiculopathy.

Now electromyography, which samples the muscle with a needle electrode in four conditions.

At rest, normal muscle is electrically silent apart from insertional activity and normal endplate noise. Abnormal spontaneous activity indicates denervation or muscle membrane instability. Fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves are the hallmark of denervation, representing spontaneously discharging individual denervated muscle fibers. These take time to appear, roughly ten to fourteen days in proximal muscles and up to three to six weeks in distal muscles, which is the reason for the three to four week rule on timing studies after an injury. Fasciculation potentials represent spontaneous discharge of a whole motor unit and, while they occur benignly, their presence with widespread denervation raises concern for motor neuron disease. Myotonic discharges wax and wane in amplitude and frequency, producing the characteristic dive bomber sound. Complex repetitive discharges indicate a chronic process. Myokymic discharges, grouped rhythmic bursts, are notable for their association with radiation induced plexopathy, which is a discriminator worth carrying.

With minimal voluntary contraction, you examine the morphology of individual motor unit potentials. Acute denervation initially shows normal morphology because the surviving units have not yet changed. Over weeks to months, surviving axons sprout to reinnervate orphaned muscle fibers, enlarging the motor unit territory and producing large amplitude, long duration, polyphasic motor unit potentials. That is the signature of chronic denervation with reinnervation, and it tells you the process is old. Early reinnervation produces small, short, polyphasic nascent units. Myopathic processes, in contrast, produce small amplitude, short duration, polyphasic units, because muscle fibers are lost from within the unit.

With maximal contraction, you assess the recruitment pattern. Neurogenic processes produce reduced recruitment: fewer motor units, each firing at a rapid rate to generate force, producing an incomplete interference pattern. Myopathic processes produce early or full recruitment: many small units recruited at low force because each contributes little. This neurogenic versus myopathic distinction is a favorite and is worth having crisply.

Now timing, which governs everything. Immediately after an injury, nerve conduction studies are normal distal to the lesion because Wallerian degeneration has not occurred. Distal motor responses fail by roughly seven to ten days and sensory responses by roughly ten to eleven days. Needle electromyographic denervation appears at roughly two to three weeks proximally and three to six weeks distally. Therefore the earliest study that meaningfully distinguishes neurapraxia from axonotmesis is at roughly three to four weeks, and that is the number to quote. A study done at forty eight hours can demonstrate conduction block across a lesion but cannot grade the injury.

Now the patterns you must recognize.

Radiculopathy. The defining features are denervation in muscles supplied by a single root but by different peripheral nerves, denervation in the paraspinal muscles, which are supplied by the dorsal ramus branching immediately at the root, and critically, a normal sensory nerve action potential. The sensory response is normal because the lesion is proximal to the dorsal root ganglion, so the peripheral sensory axon remains connected to its cell body and does not degenerate despite clinical numbness. Clinically numb with a normal sensory nerve action potential means the lesion is preganglionic, meaning root level or root avulsion. That single rule distinguishes radiculopathy from plexopathy and is among the most testable facts in electrodiagnosis.

Plexopathy. Denervation crosses multiple peripheral nerve and multiple root distributions, the sensory nerve action potentials are abnormal because the lesion is distal to the ganglion, and the paraspinal muscles are normal because the dorsal rami branch proximal to the plexus. Compare directly with radiculopathy: abnormal sensory responses and normal paraspinals means plexus; normal sensory responses and abnormal paraspinals means root.

Focal entrapment neuropathy. Findings are confined to the distribution of one nerve distal to a focal lesion, with focal slowing or conduction block across the entrapment site demonstrated by inching or short segment studies. For carpal tunnel syndrome, the earliest abnormality is prolonged distal sensory latency, with motor latency prolongation later and denervation of abductor pollicis brevis in severe disease. For ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, look for a conduction velocity drop across the elbow of more than roughly ten meters per second relative to the forearm segment, with conduction block.

Polyneuropathy. Findings are symmetric, distal, and length dependent, affecting the legs before the arms. Then subclassify. A predominantly axonal polyneuropathy with reduced amplitudes and relatively preserved velocities suggests diabetes, toxic, metabolic, or nutritional causes. A predominantly demyelinating polyneuropathy with markedly prolonged latencies and slowed velocities suggests Guillain Barré syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, or a hereditary neuropathy such as Charcot Marie Tooth type one. Uniform slowing suggests hereditary; non-uniform slowing with conduction block and temporal dispersion suggests acquired.

Motor neuron disease. Widespread denervation with fasciculations across multiple root and nerve territories and across multiple body regions, with entirely normal sensory studies. Normal sensation with widespread motor denervation should always prompt you to say motor neuron disease, and it is the diagnosis that must be excluded before you operate on a patient with painless progressive weakness and wasting. Examiners construct the scenario of a patient sent for cervical decompression who actually has amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and electrodiagnostics is how you catch it.

Neuromuscular junction disorders. Repetitive nerve stimulation at low frequency produces a decremental response in myasthenia gravis. High frequency stimulation or brief exercise produces marked facilitation, an incremental response, in Lambert Eaton myasthenic syndrome and in botulism.

Now the limitations, which you should state proactively, because the examiner respects a candidate who bounds their own evidence. Electrodiagnostics is operator dependent and its quality varies substantially. It cannot assess pure small fiber pathology, so a patient with burning neuropathic pain from small fiber neuropathy can have a completely normal study. It is uncomfortable, and patient tolerance limits how many muscles can be sampled. Limb temperature must be controlled, because cooling slows conduction and prolongs latency, producing false positives, so the limb should be warmed to roughly thirty two to thirty four degrees Celsius. Anticoagulation is a relative consideration for needle examination of deep muscles. And critically, electrodiagnostic findings must be correlated with the clinical picture and the imaging: an incidental electrical abnormality does not by itself justify an operation, and a normal study does not exclude a clinically convincing entrapment, with carpal tunnel syndrome being the classic example where roughly ten to fifteen percent of genuine cases have normal studies.

Finally, know the three reasons to order the study, and say them when asked why: to confirm and localize the lesion, to grade its severity and establish whether there is axonal loss, and to exclude alternative or superimposed diagnoses. Add a fourth in the surgical context, which is to establish a documented baseline against which postoperative change can be measured.

Be able to reason quantitatively when handed numbers. Normal motor conduction velocity in the arm is roughly fifty to sixty five metres per second and in the leg roughly forty to fifty five; velocities below about thirty five in the arm suggest a demyelinating process, whereas mild slowing in the low forties with reduced amplitude usually reflects loss of the fastest conducting large fibres from axonal dropout rather than true demyelination. That distinction, that axonal loss can mildly slow conduction by removing the fastest axons, is a frequent trap and worth stating pre-emptively. Distal motor latency for the median nerve is normally under roughly four point two milliseconds and for the ulnar under about three point four. Compound muscle action potential amplitudes are measured in millivolts and sensory nerve action potential amplitudes in microvolts, which is why sensory studies are the more sensitive early indicator in most compressive neuropathies.

Understand the technical pitfalls, because an examiner may use an artefact to test you. Limb temperature is the big one: cooling slows conduction and prolongs latency, producing a false positive study, so the limb must be warmed to roughly thirty two to thirty four degrees Celsius and you should ask whether it was. Excessive stimulation can spread to a neighbouring nerve and produce a spuriously large or early response, the classic example being co-stimulation of the median nerve during ulnar studies. Incorrect distance measurement directly corrupts the calculated velocity, which matters most across short segments such as the elbow, and is why inching studies use small fixed increments. Anomalous innervation, particularly the Martin Gruber anastomosis, produces a characteristic drop in proximal median amplitude with an unexpectedly large proximal ulnar response and will be misread as conduction block if it is not recognised.

Add the neuromuscular junction and myopathy patterns in more detail, since the discriminating question is often is this neurogenic at all. In myasthenia gravis, repetitive nerve stimulation at low frequency, typically two to three hertz, produces a decremental response greater than ten percent, and single fibre electromyography showing increased jitter is the most sensitive test. In Lambert Eaton myasthenic syndrome and in botulism, baseline compound muscle action potential amplitudes are low and brief maximal voluntary exercise or high frequency stimulation produces marked facilitation, often exceeding one hundred percent increment; Lambert Eaton is associated with small cell lung cancer and with voltage gated calcium channel antibodies. In myopathy, motor and sensory conduction studies are normal while the needle examination shows small, short duration, polyphasic motor unit potentials with early recruitment, and in inflammatory myopathy also abundant fibrillations.

Know the specific syndromes that produce diagnostic electrophysiology. Guillain Barré syndrome shows early loss or prolongation of F waves and H reflexes reflecting proximal demyelination, later conduction block and temporal dispersion, and sural sparing with abnormal median sensory responses is a characteristic pattern. Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy shows non-uniform slowing with conduction block and dispersion, in contrast with the uniform slowing of hereditary demyelinating neuropathy such as Charcot Marie Tooth type one. Multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block produces motor block without sensory abnormality and mimics motor neuron disease but is treatable, which makes it a must not miss. Radiation plexopathy characteristically shows myokymic discharges, which help distinguish it from neoplastic plexopathy.

Understand how the study informs surgery specifically. Pre-operatively it confirms the diagnosis, localises the lesion, grades severity and establishes a baseline, and it excludes the mimics, most importantly motor neuron disease before a cervical decompression and polyneuropathy before a tarsal tunnel release. Intra-operatively, nerve action potential recording across a lesion in continuity is the decisive test for neurolysis versus resection and grafting, and direct stimulation identifies functional fascicles before you divide anything. Post-operatively, serial studies track reinnervation, with nascent small polyphasic motor units appearing well before any clinically detectable movement, which is often the only objective evidence that a repair is working and is genuinely reassuring to a worried patient.

Finally, state the limitations before the examiner does. Electrodiagnosis assesses large myelinated fibres only, so pure small fibre neuropathy with burning pain and autonomic features gives a normal study and needs skin biopsy or quantitative sensory testing. It is operator dependent and its quality varies widely. It is uncomfortable, which limits how many muscles can be sampled in practice. Needle examination in an anticoagulated patient is generally safe in superficial muscles but deep muscles warrant caution. Roughly ten to fifteen percent of clinically genuine carpal tunnel syndrome has a normal study, so a negative result never overrides a convincing clinical picture. And an incidental electrical abnormality in an asymptomatic nerve is not an indication to operate. The report is evidence, not a verdict; the patient in front of you is the verdict.

One practical point on sequencing. Do not order electrodiagnosis as a reflex before you have a localising hypothesis, because the study is only as good as the question it is asked. Tell the electromyographer what you suspect and what you need excluded, because that determines which nerves and which muscles are sampled and whether the paraspinal muscles and the contralateral limb are examined at all. A study requested as query neuropathy will come back as a normal study far more often than one requested as query ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, please compare across the elbow and exclude C eight radiculopathy and motor neuron disease.

To summarize. Demyelination prolongs latency and slows velocity with preserved amplitude; axonal loss reduces amplitude with preserved velocity. Conduction block means neurapraxia and predicts recovery. Wait three to four weeks after injury for a meaningful study. Normal sensory response with denervation and abnormal paraspinals equals radiculopathy; abnormal sensory response with normal paraspinals equals plexopathy. Large polyphasic units with reduced recruitment means chronic denervation with reinnervation. Normal sensation with widespread denervation means motor neuron disease until proven otherwise. And always correlate with the clinical examination before you operate.
