Let's master the lower extremity entrapment neuropathies for the oral boards.

These lesions matter disproportionately in the examination because they are the great mimics of lumbar radiculopathy. The examiner presents a patient with foot drop, or with lateral thigh numbness, or with weakness after a spine operation, and watches whether you reflexively reach for a lumbar decompression or whether you localize properly. Getting this wrong means operating on the wrong structure. Let's build the anatomy and the discriminators.

Start with peroneal neuropathy at the fibular head, the most common lower extremity entrapment and the most important because it presents as foot drop.

The sciatic nerve divides into the tibial and common peroneal divisions, usually in the distal thigh, although the divisions are anatomically distinct throughout the sciatic nerve. The common peroneal nerve wraps around the neck of the fibula, where it is superficial, tethered, and directly against bone, making it extraordinarily vulnerable. It then divides into the deep peroneal nerve and the superficial peroneal nerve.

[[fig:1]]

The deep peroneal nerve supplies tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus, and peroneus tertius, producing ankle dorsiflexion and toe extension, and supplies sensation to the first dorsal web space only, that small patch between the great and second toes. The superficial peroneal nerve supplies peroneus longus and brevis, producing foot eversion, and supplies sensation over the dorsum of the foot and the lateral lower leg.

Now the crucial differential: peroneal neuropathy versus fifth lumbar radiculopathy. Both produce foot drop. Distinguish them with three findings. First, foot inversion, performed by tibialis posterior, which is tibial innervated but fifth lumbar supplied. Inversion is weak in an L five radiculopathy and preserved in a peroneal neuropathy. This is the single best discriminator and you should state it. Second, hip abduction, performed by gluteus medius, also L five supplied via the superior gluteal nerve, weak in radiculopathy and preserved in peroneal neuropathy. Third, sensory distribution and back pain: radiculopathy typically involves back and posterolateral leg pain in a dermatomal pattern, whereas peroneal neuropathy produces a peripheral nerve pattern without back pain. Add a Tinel sign at the fibular head and electrodiagnostic demonstration of conduction block across the fibular head, plus normal paraspinal electromyography, to complete the case.

Know the causes. External compression is the most common: prolonged leg crossing, squatting, habitual kneeling, tight casts and braces, and critically, positioning during surgery, particularly in lithotomy or lateral position, which makes this an iatrogenic complication you must be able to discuss. Rapid weight loss, sometimes called slimmer's palsy, removes the protective fat pad. Trauma to the fibular head and knee dislocation. And the intraneural ganglion cyst arising from the superior tibiofibular joint, which is the lesion you must not miss: it produces a progressive, often painful peroneal palsy, is diagnosed with magnetic resonance or ultrasound, and requires disconnection of the articular branch and treatment of the joint, because simple cyst decompression has a high recurrence rate.

Management is conservative for most compressive cases: remove the cause, use an ankle foot orthosis to prevent tripping and to preserve a plantigrade foot, initiate physical therapy to prevent contracture, and observe, because most positional palsies recover within weeks to a few months. Obtain electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks to grade the injury. Operate for a structural lesion such as a ganglion or mass, for failure to recover by roughly three to six months with electrical evidence of ongoing denervation, or for a known laceration. Decompression involves releasing the fascia of peroneus longus and the fibular tunnel. For late irreversible foot drop, the reconstructive option is a posterior tibial tendon transfer or a permanent orthosis.

Next, meralgia paresthetica, the entrapment of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. This nerve arises from the second and third lumbar roots, passes under or through the inguinal ligament near the anterior superior iliac spine, and is purely sensory. That last word is the key: it is a pure sensory nerve, so there is never weakness. If the patient has weakness, it is not meralgia paresthetica, and you should say so.

The presentation is burning, numbness, and dysesthesia over the anterolateral thigh, worsened by standing and walking and by hip extension, relieved by sitting. Causes include obesity, pregnancy, tight belts and clothing, tool belts, body armor, diabetes, and iatrogenic injury, notably from anterior iliac crest bone graft harvest, which is a neurosurgical complication you should be prepared to own, and from positioning in the prone position on certain frames. The differential includes upper lumbar radiculopathy, which would produce weakness and reflex changes, and a retroperitoneal or pelvic mass compressing the nerve, which is why imaging is warranted in an atypical case.

Management is overwhelmingly conservative: weight loss, removal of constricting garments, neuropathic pain medication, and a local anesthetic and steroid block near the anterior superior iliac spine, which is also diagnostically confirmatory. Most cases resolve. For refractory cases, surgical options are decompression or neurectomy; decompression preserves sensation but has a higher failure rate, whereas neurectomy is more reliable for pain relief but produces a permanent anesthetic patch, and the patient must consent to that explicitly.

Next, tarsal tunnel syndrome, compression of the tibial nerve beneath the flexor retinaculum posterior and inferior to the medial malleolus. Contents of the tarsal tunnel, from anterior to posterior, are tibialis posterior tendon, flexor digitorum longus, the posterior tibial artery and vein, the tibial nerve, and flexor hallucis longus, remembered by mnemonics such as Tom, Dick, and a very nervous Harry. The nerve divides into medial and lateral plantar branches and the calcaneal branch.

The presentation is burning pain and paresthesia of the sole, worse with activity and at night, with a Tinel sign behind the medial malleolus and a positive dorsiflexion eversion test. Causes include space occupying lesions such as ganglia, varicosities, and accessory muscles, post-traumatic deformity, tenosynovitis, and hindfoot malalignment. The important caution is that tarsal tunnel syndrome is overdiagnosed, and the differential of plantar foot pain is dominated by plantar fasciitis, S one radiculopathy, and peripheral polyneuropathy, particularly diabetic. Confirm with electrodiagnostics and imaging before operating, because outcomes of release in the absence of a demonstrable structural lesion are mediocre. Management is conservative first with orthotics, activity modification, and neuropathic agents, with release reserved for confirmed compression, ideally with an identified mass.

Next, femoral neuropathy. The femoral nerve arises from the second through fourth lumbar roots, passes between psoas and iliacus, and enters the thigh beneath the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery. It supplies quadriceps, producing knee extension, and iliopsoas, producing hip flexion, with sensation over the anterior thigh and, via the saphenous branch, the medial leg and foot. The presentation is knee buckling, quadriceps weakness and wasting, an absent patellar reflex, and anterior thigh numbness.

The high yield cause is a retroperitoneal or iliopsoas hematoma in an anticoagulated patient, which is a neurosurgical emergency of sorts because prompt recognition and reversal, and sometimes evacuation, preserves function. Other causes are iatrogenic injury from pelvic or hip surgery, from lithotomy positioning with prolonged hip flexion and abduction, from retractor placement in an anterior abdominal approach, and importantly from the lateral transpsoas approach to the lumbar spine, where the lumbar plexus lies within the psoas and is at risk. Diabetic amyotrophy, or diabetic lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy, presents with severe thigh pain followed by profound proximal weakness and weight loss, and is managed medically. Distinguish femoral neuropathy from an upper lumbar radiculopathy by the presence of hip adductor weakness, supplied by the obturator nerve, which indicates a plexus or root level lesion rather than a femoral nerve lesion.

Next, obturator neuropathy, uncommon but worth naming, producing medial thigh pain and adductor weakness, seen with pelvic surgery, obstetric injury, and pelvic masses.

Finally, piriformis syndrome and deep gluteal syndrome, sciatic nerve irritation in the subgluteal space. This is a contested diagnosis and you should treat it with appropriate skepticism. The presentation is buttock pain with radiation down the leg, worsened by sitting, with tenderness over the sciatic notch and positive provocative maneuvers such as the FAIR test with flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. It is a diagnosis of exclusion after lumbar pathology, hip pathology, and sacroiliac disease have been excluded. Management is physical therapy, image guided injection, and only rarely surgical release. Being appropriately cautious here, rather than enthusiastic, is the right examination posture.

Now some cross cutting principles to deploy. First, always ask whether this is truly a peripheral lesion or a root lesion, and use the muscles that share a root but differ in peripheral nerve to make the call, as with tibialis posterior in the foot drop case. Second, always consider a mass lesion, particularly in the retroperitoneum and pelvis, when a lower extremity nerve fails without a clear compressive posture, and image accordingly. Third, own the iatrogenic causes: positioning injuries, iliac crest harvest, lateral transpsoas approaches, and retractor injuries are neurosurgical complications, and the examiner may present one as your own complication to see how you handle disclosure and management. Say that you would recognize it promptly, examine and document, obtain electrodiagnostics at the appropriate interval, disclose honestly to the patient, initiate therapy and orthotics, and follow the standard observation and exploration timeline. Fourth, respect the timeline that governs all peripheral nerve work: electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks, reassess at three months, and explore by three to six months if there is no recovery, because of the motor endplate deadline.

Build out the sciatic nerve itself, because it sits behind several of these presentations. It arises from the fourth lumbar through third sacral roots, exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen usually beneath piriformis, and descends in the posterior thigh supplying the hamstrings before dividing into tibial and common peroneal divisions. The peroneal division is more vulnerable throughout its course for three structural reasons worth stating: it has fewer, larger fascicles with less protective epineurial connective tissue, it is more tethered at the fibular head and at the sciatic notch, and it is positioned laterally where compression and traction are greatest. That is why a sciatic injury, for instance after hip arthroplasty or a posterior hip dislocation, characteristically produces a peroneal predominant deficit with relative tibial sparing, and why a foot drop after hip surgery may be a sciatic lesion rather than a peroneal one. Examine the hamstrings and the tibial innervated muscles to make that call.

Add the pelvic and gluteal entrapments. Superior gluteal nerve injury weakens gluteus medius and produces a Trendelenburg gait, and is a recognised complication of a direct lateral or transgluteal hip approach and of poorly placed intramuscular injection. Inferior gluteal nerve injury weakens gluteus maximus and impairs rising from a chair and stair climbing. Pudendal neuralgia, from the second through fourth sacral roots, produces perineal pain worse on sitting and relieved by standing or sitting on a toilet seat, and may follow prolonged cycling or pelvic surgery; it is a diagnosis of exclusion supported by response to an image guided pudendal block. These are unglamorous but they are exactly what an examiner reaches for when they want to see whether you know the plexus below the waist.

Give saphenous and obturator entrapment their due. The saphenous nerve is the terminal sensory branch of the femoral nerve and is commonly injured during medial knee surgery, vein harvest for coronary bypass, and medial approaches to the tibia; its infrapatellar branch injury produces a painful numb patch over the anteromedial knee and is a frequent cause of persistent post-arthroscopy pain. Obturator neuropathy produces medial thigh pain and adductor weakness and follows pelvic surgery, obstetric injury, obturator hernia and pelvic malignancy.

Expand the diagnostic approach. Electrodiagnosis should compare the affected nerve with the contralateral side and should sample muscles that share a root but differ in peripheral nerve, which is how the foot drop question is settled: tibialis posterior is fifth lumbar but tibial innervated, so denervation there indicates a root or plexus lesion, whereas sparing points to a peroneal nerve lesion. Paraspinal denervation again indicates a root level process. Add high resolution ultrasound, which is now excellent for the superficial lower limb nerves: it shows focal enlargement at a compression point, identifies an intraneural ganglion or a nerve sheath tumour, demonstrates dynamic subluxation, and can guide a diagnostic block. Magnetic resonance neurography demonstrates nerve calibre and signal change and denervation oedema in the target muscles, and is the study of choice for suspected pelvic and sciatic pathology.

Deepen the peroneal section, because it is the one that matters most. The intraneural ganglion at the fibular head deserves emphasis: synovial fluid tracks from the superior tibiofibular joint along the articular branch and dissects proximally within the epineurium, so the durable operation is ligation and division of the articular branch plus addressing the joint, not simple decompression or aspiration, which recur. Deep peroneal entrapment beneath the extensor retinaculum at the ankle, the anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome, produces first web space numbness with weakness of extensor digitorum brevis. Superficial peroneal entrapment where the nerve pierces the deep fascia roughly ten to twelve centimetres above the lateral malleolus produces lateral leg and dorsal foot sensory symptoms, often exercise induced, and can coexist with chronic exertional compartment syndrome, which should be excluded with compartment pressure measurement.

Be explicit about the iatrogenic and positioning injuries, because an examiner may present one as your own complication. Lithotomy position threatens the common peroneal nerve at the fibular head against the stirrup and the femoral nerve through prolonged hip flexion and abduction. Prolonged lateral position threatens the dependent peroneal nerve. Improper padding in prone spinal surgery threatens the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve at the anterior superior iliac spine and the ulnar nerve at the elbow. Anterior iliac crest bone graft harvest threatens the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and, if the osteotomy strays posteriorly, the cluneal nerves. The lateral transpsoas approach to the lumbar spine threatens the lumbar plexus within psoas and the genitofemoral nerve on its surface, which is why directional electromyographic monitoring is used and why transient thigh numbness and hip flexor weakness are common afterwards. If such an injury occurs, the answer is prompt recognition, documented examination, honest disclosure to the patient, therapy and orthotics, electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks, and the standard observation and exploration timeline rather than defensiveness.

Finally, keep the general rules in front of you. Confirm that the lesion is peripheral rather than radicular using muscles that dissociate root from nerve. Consider a mass lesion, especially retroperitoneal or pelvic, whenever a lower limb nerve fails without a compressive posture to explain it. Give conservative management a genuine trial with orthoses, activity modification, padding and neuropathic agents. Operate for a structural lesion, for a documented laceration, or for failure to recover by three to six months with electrical evidence of continuing denervation. And respect the motor endplate clock of roughly twelve to eighteen months, because a foot drop referred at two years is a tendon transfer problem, not a nerve problem.

To summarize. Peroneal neuropathy at the fibular head causes foot drop with preserved inversion and preserved hip abduction, unlike L five radiculopathy; look for the intraneural ganglion and treat it by disconnecting the articular branch. Meralgia paresthetica is purely sensory, so weakness excludes it, and it is often iatrogenic from iliac crest harvest. Tarsal tunnel syndrome is overdiagnosed and needs a structural lesion to justify release. Femoral neuropathy should prompt you to think retroperitoneal hematoma in an anticoagulated patient and lumbar plexus injury after a lateral transpsoas approach, and adductor weakness moves the lesion proximal to the femoral nerve. And piriformis syndrome remains a diagnosis of exclusion.
