Welcome to this oral board review session on glioblastoma standard of care. This is one of the highest-yield topics you will encounter on the ABNS oral boards. Examiners love it because it spans surgical decision-making, molecular diagnostics, adjuvant therapy, and complication management. Let's build from the foundation up and then walk through a clinical case the way an examiner would push you.

Let's start with the fundamentals. Glioblastoma is a WHO grade 4 diffuse astrocytic glioma and remains the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults, with an incidence of approximately 3.2 per 100,000 person-years. The median age at diagnosis is 64. With standard-of-care treatment, median overall survival is 14 to 16 months. Without treatment, median survival drops to 3 to 4 months. These numbers frame the gravity of every decision you make in managing this disease.

Now, here is the classification point that is absolutely critical for boards. The 2021 WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors fundamentally changed how we define glioblastoma. Glioblastoma is now, by definition, an IDH-wildtype tumor. If a diffuse astrocytic tumor is IDH-mutant, it is never called glioblastoma regardless of its histological features. Even if it has necrosis. Even if it has microvascular proliferation. An IDH-mutant tumor with those high-grade features is classified as astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, WHO grade 4. Not glioblastoma. This is not just a naming convention. It reflects fundamentally different biology, different prognosis, and different treatment considerations. IDH-mutant grade 4 astrocytomas carry a median survival of 3 to 5 years, dramatically better than IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. The examiner will test whether you understand this distinction. Do not conflate the two.

The 2021 classification also introduced another key concept. An IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytoma that might look histologically like a grade 2 or grade 3 tumor gets upgraded to glioblastoma, WHO grade 4, if it carries any one of three specific molecular features: TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or combined whole-chromosome gain of chromosome 7 with loss of chromosome 10, written as plus 7 minus 10. These molecular markers confer a prognosis equivalent to histologically classic glioblastoma, so the 2021 classification recognizes the molecular biology as the defining feature, not just the histology.

On imaging, the classic appearance is a ring-enhancing mass with central necrosis, irregular enhancing margins, and surrounding T2 and FLAIR signal abnormality. Here is a teaching point that matters both clinically and on boards. The surrounding edema in glioblastoma is not benign vasogenic edema. Infiltrating tumor cells extend well beyond the enhancing margin into that T2 and FLAIR abnormality. This is why glioblastoma invariably recurs, typically at the resection margin, even after gross total resection. The enhancing rim represents the dense cellular tumor with neovascularization, the central necrosis reflects outgrowth of blood supply, and the surrounding signal abnormality harbors the infiltrative component that you cannot fully resect.

Let's discuss MGMT promoter methylation status because you must know this cold. The MGMT gene encodes O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase, a DNA repair enzyme. When the MGMT promoter is methylated, the gene is silenced, the repair enzyme is not produced, and the tumor becomes more susceptible to alkylating chemotherapy, specifically temozolomide. Approximately 40 percent of glioblastomas have MGMT promoter methylation. In the subanalysis of the landmark Stupp trial by Hegi and colleagues, patients with MGMT methylation who received temozolomide had a median overall survival of 21.7 months compared to 12.7 months in patients whose tumors were unmethylated. MGMT status is the single strongest molecular prognostic and predictive marker in glioblastoma. It influences treatment decisions, particularly in elderly patients, which we will cover shortly.

Now let's move into a clinical scenario. A 58-year-old right-handed man presents with 2 weeks of progressive left-hand weakness and one witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure. His wife reports subtle personality changes over the past month, which she attributed to work stress. On examination, he has 4 out of 5 strength in the left hand grip and left finger extension, with intact sensation and no aphasia. MRI brain with and without contrast reveals a 4-centimeter ring-enhancing mass in the right frontal lobe with central necrosis, surrounding T2 and FLAIR edema, and 8 millimeters of midline shift from right to left. The posterior margin of the tumor abuts the precentral gyrus. No other intracranial lesions are identified. CT chest is clean, and basic laboratory workup is unremarkable. The differential diagnosis includes high-grade glioma, metastatic disease, and less likely abscess or lymphoma, but the imaging characteristics here are classic for glioblastoma. Metastatic disease is less likely with a single lesion and clean chest CT, though a primary systemic malignancy workup is reasonable. This is your patient. Walk through the management.

Let's begin with initial medical management, because the examiner will ask what you do before the patient goes to the operating room. Start dexamethasone. The standard dosing is 10 milligrams intravenous loading dose followed by 4 milligrams every 6 hours. Dexamethasone reduces vasogenic edema and mass effect, often producing dramatic clinical improvement within hours. For the seizure, start levetiracetam. The typical dose is 500 to 1000 milligrams twice daily. Here is a board pearl. The AAN evidence-based guideline states that antiepileptic drugs are indicated after a brain tumor patient has had a seizure, but there is no benefit to prophylactic antiepileptic drugs in brain tumor patients who have not yet seized. If the examiner asks whether you would start prophylactic seizure medication in a brain tumor patient without seizures, the answer is no. This patient had a seizure, so antiepileptics are appropriate here. Also address VTE prophylaxis with sequential compression devices preoperatively and consider pharmacologic prophylaxis with subcutaneous heparin or enoxaparin starting 24 to 48 hours postoperatively, because brain tumor patients are at high risk for venous thromboembolism, with reported rates of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism as high as 20 to 30 percent in the perioperative period. The examiner may challenge you on the safety of anticoagulation after craniotomy. The data support starting pharmacologic prophylaxis at 24 hours postoperatively, and the risk of VTE complications outweighs the small risk of postoperative hemorrhage.

Now let's talk about the surgical plan. The goal is maximal safe resection. Let me emphasize both words. Maximal and safe. Multiple landmark studies have demonstrated that extent of resection directly correlates with survival. Lacroix and colleagues showed a survival benefit when resection exceeded 98 percent. Sanai and Berger's volumetric analysis confirmed a threshold effect at 78 percent but a continuous improvement in survival with increasing extent, with the greatest benefit above 98 percent. Steno and colleagues corroborated these findings. The data are clear. Gross total resection is superior to subtotal resection, which is superior to biopsy alone, and the benefit extends to patients who then go on to receive standard adjuvant therapy. Every additional percentage of tumor you remove translates to incremental survival benefit.

But here is the critical balance. This tumor abuts the precentral gyrus. You are operating adjacent to primary motor cortex in a patient who already has mild left-hand weakness. You must achieve maximal resection without creating a new permanent neurological deficit. A gross total resection that leaves the patient hemiplegic is not a good outcome. Functional outcome matters. How do you achieve this balance? Intraoperative adjuncts.

For a tumor involving the precentral gyrus, you have several tools. First, you can perform an awake craniotomy with direct cortical and subcortical stimulation mapping to identify the motor strip and corticospinal tract in real time. Alternatively, if the patient is not a candidate for awake surgery, you can perform the procedure under general anesthesia with direct cortical stimulation using a Penfield or Ojemann stimulator and continuous motor evoked potential monitoring. Both approaches allow you to identify functional boundaries and tailor your resection accordingly.

Neuronavigation with frameless stereotaxy is standard. Register the preoperative MRI, plan your trajectory, and use navigation to orient yourself relative to the tumor and eloquent structures, while recognizing that brain shift occurs after dural opening and CSF drainage, which degrades navigation accuracy as the case progresses.

5-ALA, or 5-aminolevulinic acid, is a fluorescence-guided surgery tool. The patient drinks an oral solution of 5-ALA, 20 milligrams per kilogram, approximately 3 hours before surgery. Tumor cells metabolize 5-ALA to protoporphyrin IX, which fluoresces pink-violet under blue-violet excitation light at 400 to 410 nanometers. The Stummer randomized controlled trial published in Lancet Oncology demonstrated that 5-ALA-guided resection achieved gross total resection in 65 percent of cases compared to 36 percent with conventional white-light surgery, and this translated into improved 6-month progression-free survival. This is a powerful tool for identifying tumor margins, particularly where infiltrating tumor blends with normal brain under white light.

Intraoperative MRI, when available, allows you to obtain imaging before closure to identify residual enhancing tumor and perform further resection in the same operative session. Studies have shown that intraoperative MRI increases gross total resection rates by identifying residual tumor that was not apparent to the surgeon under direct visualization. Intraoperative ultrasound is another readily available option for real-time assessment of residual tumor during the resection, and while it has lower spatial resolution than intraoperative MRI, it is widely accessible and does not require specialized operating room infrastructure.

Now the examiner will ask you to walk through the Stupp protocol. Know this cold because the doses and schedule matter. The landmark trial was published by Stupp and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005. The protocol is as follows. After maximal safe resection, the patient receives concurrent chemoradiation. Radiation is delivered at a total dose of 60 Gray in 30 fractions over 6 weeks, targeting the tumor bed plus a 2-centimeter margin. Concurrent with radiation, the patient receives daily temozolomide at 75 milligrams per square meter, 7 days per week, for the entire 6-week radiation course. During this concurrent phase, the patient must receive Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole because of the lymphopenia that temozolomide induces. After completing concurrent chemoradiation, there is a 4-week treatment break. Then the patient begins adjuvant temozolomide. The dose is 150 milligrams per square meter on days 1 through 5 of each 28-day cycle for the first cycle, escalating to 200 milligrams per square meter for subsequent cycles if tolerated. This is continued for a total of 6 adjuvant cycles. Complete blood counts must be monitored regularly. The primary toxicities are myelosuppression, specifically lymphopenia and thrombocytopenia, as well as hepatotoxicity. If the platelet count drops below 100,000 or the absolute neutrophil count drops below 1,500, you hold temozolomide and recheck.

The Stupp trial demonstrated a median overall survival of 14.6 months with combined chemoradiation compared to 12.1 months with radiation alone. The 2-year survival rate was 26.5 percent versus 10.4 percent. The 5-year survival rate was 9.8 percent versus 1.9 percent. These differences were statistically significant and established this protocol as the standard of care for newly diagnosed glioblastoma. The long-term follow-up data are particularly important because they demonstrate that a small but real fraction of patients achieve durable benefit, and these long-term survivors are disproportionately MGMT-methylated.

Now let's discuss special considerations for elderly patients. Patients over age 70 may not tolerate the full Stupp protocol due to its cumulative toxicity over the 6-week concurrent phase. Several alternatives have been studied. The Perry trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2017, demonstrated that adding temozolomide to hypofractionated radiation, 40 Gray in 15 fractions over 3 weeks, improved overall survival in elderly patients compared to hypofractionated radiation alone, with a median OS of 9.3 months versus 7.6 months. This is now a standard option for elderly patients. The Nordic trial provided additional guidance stratified by MGMT status. For elderly patients with MGMT-methylated tumors, temozolomide alone is a reasonable option, as these patients respond well to alkylating chemotherapy even without radiation. For elderly patients with MGMT-unmethylated tumors, radiation alone, either standard or hypofractionated, is preferred because unmethylated tumors derive minimal benefit from temozolomide.

Let's move to tumor treating fields, known by the brand name Optune. This is a relatively newer addition to the treatment armamentarium. Tumor treating fields deliver low-intensity, intermediate-frequency alternating electric fields, 200 kilohertz, via transducer arrays affixed to the shaved scalp. These fields disrupt mitotic spindle formation during cell division and interfere with organelle assembly, selectively affecting rapidly dividing tumor cells. The EF-14 randomized trial demonstrated that adding tumor treating fields to maintenance temozolomide improved median overall survival to 20.9 months compared to 16.0 months with temozolomide alone. Progression-free survival also improved. Tumor treating fields received FDA approval for newly diagnosed glioblastoma based on this trial. The critical caveat is compliance. The survival benefit is directly proportional to the number of hours worn per day, and the threshold for meaningful benefit is at least 18 hours per day. Patients who wear the device less than 18 hours per day do not derive the same benefit. This is worth mentioning to the examiner. You should be prepared to discuss the practical realities: the device requires a shaved head, the transducer arrays can cause contact dermatitis, and the system is cumbersome, affecting quality of life.

Now let's address recurrence, because glioblastoma essentially always recurs. Postoperative MRI surveillance is performed every 2 to 3 months. Here is one of the most important concepts in neuro-oncology and a classic board trap. Pseudoprogression. In the first 3 to 6 months after completing concurrent chemoradiation, new or increased enhancement on MRI may represent treatment effect rather than true tumor recurrence. This phenomenon, called pseudoprogression, occurs in approximately 20 to 30 percent of patients and is more common in patients with MGMT promoter methylation, precisely because their tumors are more responsive to treatment. The chemoradiation causes tumor cell death, breakdown of the blood-brain barrier, and inflammatory changes that mimic tumor progression on contrast-enhanced MRI. If the examiner shows you a 3-month post-treatment MRI with new enhancement and asks whether this is recurrence, do not jump to calling it recurrence. State that this falls within the classic pseudoprogression window. Your approach should be to continue current therapy and obtain repeat imaging in 4 to 6 weeks. Advanced imaging modalities can help distinguish pseudoprogression from true recurrence. MR perfusion will show elevated cerebral blood volume in true recurrence but not in pseudoprogression. MR spectroscopy will show elevated choline-to-creatine ratio and reduced NAA in recurrent tumor. FDG-PET or amino acid PET with FET or FDOPA can show hypermetabolism in recurrence.

When true recurrence is confirmed, options are limited and none are curative. Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor, is FDA-approved for recurrent glioblastoma. However, you need to know the evidence. The AVAglio trial and RTOG 0825 both demonstrated that bevacizumab does not improve overall survival. It does improve progression-free survival, reduces peritumoral edema, decreases steroid requirements, and can improve quality of life, but it does not extend life. The examiner may test whether you understand this distinction. Other options for recurrence include lomustine, also called CCNU, which is an alkylating agent that can be used as monotherapy or in combination with bevacizumab, although the CeTeG trial studied lomustine plus temozolomide in MGMT-methylated tumors as initial therapy and showed a survival benefit, but that was a front-line study, not a recurrence study. Re-resection is appropriate if the recurrent tumor is surgically accessible, the patient has good functional status with a Karnofsky performance score of at least 70, and there is a reasonable expectation that meaningful cytoreduction can be achieved without creating new deficits. Re-irradiation with stereotactic radiosurgery or hypofractionated stereotactic radiation can be considered in select patients with small-volume recurrence, taking into account cumulative radiation dose to surrounding brain. Clinical trial enrollment should always be discussed, and mentioning this to the examiner demonstrates that you understand the limitations of current salvage therapy and that you practice evidence-based medicine. The prognosis after recurrence is poor, with a median survival of approximately 6 to 9 months regardless of salvage strategy.

Let me now address the complications you must be prepared to manage and discuss. Surgical complications include postoperative hemorrhage in the resection cavity, which occurs in approximately 1 to 2 percent of cases and may require emergent reoperation. Surgical site infection, including meningitis, occurs in 1 to 5 percent. New neurological deficit is a risk proportional to the proximity of the resection to eloquent cortex. Venous infarction can occur if major draining veins are sacrificed or thrombosed during the approach. Medical complications of adjuvant therapy include temozolomide-induced myelosuppression, most critically thrombocytopenia and lymphopenia, which can be severe and may require treatment delays or dose reductions. Hepatotoxicity requires monitoring of liver function. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia will develop in patients who do not receive prophylaxis during the concurrent phase, which is why trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is mandatory. Radiation necrosis is a delayed complication occurring months to years after treatment, and on imaging it can be indistinguishable from tumor recurrence, requiring the same advanced imaging workup discussed for pseudoprogression.

Let's close with the key evidence you must know by name. The Stupp trial, New England Journal of Medicine 2005, established concurrent temozolomide and radiation followed by adjuvant temozolomide as the standard of care. The Hegi MGMT methylation subanalysis from the same trial established MGMT promoter methylation as the key predictive biomarker for temozolomide response. The EF-14 trial established tumor treating fields as an addition to maintenance temozolomide, improving survival to 20.9 months. The Perry trial established hypofractionated radiation plus temozolomide as the standard for elderly patients. The Nordic trial provided the MGMT-stratified approach for elderly patients. AVAglio and RTOG 0825 established that bevacizumab does not improve overall survival in glioblastoma, either as front-line or at recurrence. Sanai and Lacroix provided the extent-of-resection survival data with the volumetric thresholds. The Stummer 5-ALA randomized controlled trial demonstrated improved gross total resection rates with fluorescence-guided surgery.

Now let me rapid-fire the board pearls. Glioblastoma is by definition IDH-wildtype per the 2021 WHO classification. IDH-mutant tumors with high-grade features are astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4, not glioblastoma. MGMT promoter methylation is present in 40 percent of GBMs and predicts temozolomide response with a median survival of 21.7 months methylated versus 12.7 months unmethylated. The Stupp protocol uses 60 Gray in 30 fractions with concurrent temozolomide at 75 milligrams per square meter daily, followed by adjuvant temozolomide at 150 to 200 milligrams per square meter days 1 through 5 of 28-day cycles for 6 cycles. Pseudoprogression occurs in the first 3 to 6 months after treatment, is more common with MGMT methylation, and should not be mistaken for true recurrence. Do not start prophylactic antiepileptics in brain tumor patients who have not had a seizure. Bevacizumab improves progression-free survival but not overall survival. In elderly patients, use hypofractionated radiation with or without temozolomide based on MGMT status. Tumor treating fields require at least 18 hours of daily wear to be effective. Molecular classification now supersedes histology in defining glioblastoma. When a tumor that looks histologically low grade carries TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or plus 7 minus 10 chromosomal changes and is IDH-wildtype, it is glioblastoma.

That covers glioblastoma standard of care. You're ready for this one.
