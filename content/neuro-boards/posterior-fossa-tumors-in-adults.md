  - q: Give me the classic imaging of a haemangioblastoma.
    a: WHO grade 1, highly vascular, well circumscribed, and in approximately sixty percent of cases a cystic lesion with a brightly enhancing mural nodule. The nodule is the tumour — the cyst wall is not — which is precisely why you resect the nodule and do not chase the wall.
    probe: What is the commonest posterior fossa tumour in an adult overall?
  - q: What syndrome must you exclude, and how often?
    a: Von Hippel-Lindau. Twenty-five percent of all haemangioblastomas are VHL-associated, and a cerebellar haemangioblastoma may be the initial presenting feature of the syndrome. That obliges screening: retina, spine, kidneys, adrenals and pancreas, plus genetic counselling and family screening.
    probe: Which tumours would you screen for specifically?
  - q: A posterior fossa tumour with hydrocephalus. Shunt first or resect first?
    a: Neither in isolation. The most widely accepted approach, and the one to articulate on boards, is to place an external ventricular drain at the start of the case and then proceed with tumour resection in the same operative session. That controls pressure without committing to permanent diversion.
    probe: What is the danger of shunting alone and waiting?
  - q: Why not simply shunt and come back later?
    a: Two reasons. Relieving supratentorial pressure in the presence of a posterior fossa mass creates a gradient that can precipitate upward transtentorial herniation. And many patients will not need permanent diversion once the mass is removed, so a shunt commits them unnecessarily to lifelong dependence and its revision burden.
    probe: How would you set the drain to reduce that risk?
  - q: What is the risk of the sitting position and what does it demand?
    a: Venous air embolism, at ten to forty-five percent. It demands precordial Doppler, transoesophageal echocardiography, a right atrial catheter, and a preoperative bubble study to exclude a patent foramen ovale, which would allow paradoxical embolism. Those four are the answer.
    probe: You detect air. What do you do?
  - q: Which approach to the fourth ventricle, and why?
    a: The telovelar approach, opening the cerebellomedullary fissure through the tela choroidea and inferior medullary velum. It preserves the vermis and reduces the risk of cerebellar mutism compared with transvermian approaches, while giving comparable access to the fourth ventricle.
    probe: What is cerebellar mutism and who gets it?
  - q: What are the specific hazards of the floor of the fourth ventricle?
    a: The facial colliculus and the hypoglossal and vagal trigones sit in the floor, so dissection there risks facial palsy and lower cranial nerve injury with aspiration. I would use floor mapping with stimulation and avoid working on the floor wherever the plane allows.
    probe: How would you manage the airway risk postoperatively?
  - q: What is the immediate postoperative concern after posterior fossa surgery?
    a: Haematoma in a non-compliant compartment, which produces rapid deterioration, brainstem compression and obstructive hydrocephalus with little warning. Any decline demands immediate imaging and a low threshold for return to theatre. Swelling and delayed hydrocephalus are the other two.
    probe: How long would you keep the external ventricular drain?

Welcome back. Today we are covering posterior fossa tumors in adults. This is a high-yield oral board topic because it tests your differential diagnosis, your understanding of tumor biology, your surgical technique, and your ability to manage hydrocephalus all in one case. The examiners love it because there are several trap questions built into this topic, and if you do not know the cardinal rules of hemangioblastoma surgery, you will fail this station. So let us build this from the ground up.

Let us start with the foundation. The very first thing you need to internalize is that posterior fossa tumors in adults are fundamentally different from posterior fossa tumors in children. In the pediatric population, primary tumors dominate. You think of medulloblastoma, pilocytic astrocytoma, ependymoma, and diffuse midline glioma. In adults, that entire landscape shifts. Metastatic disease is the most common posterior fossa tumor in adults. That is a critical board distinction. When an adult presents with a cerebellar mass, your first thought must be metastasis until proven otherwise, especially in patients over forty with a known or occult primary malignancy. The most common sources are lung, breast, melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and colon.

Now, among primary intra-axial posterior fossa tumors in adults, hemangioblastoma holds the top position. It is the most common primary intra-axial posterior fossa tumor in adults. This is a board fact you need cold. Hemangioblastoma is a WHO grade 1 tumor, highly vascular, well-circumscribed, and in approximately sixty percent of cases it presents as a cystic lesion with a brightly enhancing mural nodule. That imaging appearance is nearly pathognomonic. Approximately seventy-five percent of hemangioblastomas are sporadic, while twenty-five percent are associated with von Hippel-Lindau disease. We will return to VHL in detail shortly because it is a major board topic unto itself.

Beyond hemangioblastoma, you must know the other primary posterior fossa tumors in adults. Vestibular schwannoma and meningioma are the major extra-axial tumors, but those are covered in their own dedicated sessions. For intra-axial tumors, adult medulloblastoma is rare, accounting for roughly one percent of adult brain tumors, but it shows up on boards precisely because it is uncommon and tests whether you know the molecular subgroups and how they apply differently in adults versus children. Ependymoma can occur in adults in the fourth ventricle. Epidermoid and dermoid cysts are also in the differential for posterior fossa masses, though they are not true neoplasms.

Let us go deeper on hemangioblastoma because this is where the board questions live. These tumors arise from stromal cells and are intensely vascular. On imaging, the classic appearance is a cystic lesion with an enhancing mural nodule, but about forty percent present as solid enhancing masses without a cyst. They occur most commonly in the cerebellar hemispheres, followed by the brainstem and spinal cord. A critical physiological feature is that hemangioblastomas can secrete erythropoietin, leading to secondary polycythemia in five to twenty percent of patients. If an examiner gives you a posterior fossa mass with an elevated hemoglobin or hematocrit, that is a direct clue pointing you toward hemangioblastoma.

Von Hippel-Lindau disease is autosomal dominant, caused by a mutation in the VHL tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 3p25-26. The VHL protein normally targets hypoxia-inducible factor for degradation. When VHL is mutated, HIF accumulates and drives angiogenesis and cell proliferation, which is why VHL produces highly vascular tumors. The clinical manifestations of VHL include cerebellar and spinal hemangioblastomas, retinal hemangioblastomas, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and cysts, endolymphatic sac tumors, and epididymal cystadenomas. Here is the board rule: if you find a hemangioblastoma, you must screen for VHL. If you find multiple hemangioblastomas, that is VHL until proven otherwise.

Now let us address adult medulloblastoma briefly in the foundation section. The molecular subgroups that apply in pediatric medulloblastoma also apply in adults but with different distribution. In adults, the SHH subgroup, which stands for Sonic Hedgehog, predominates. The WNT subgroup carries the best prognosis with greater than ninety percent five-year survival. Adults with medulloblastoma tend to present differently than children. The tumors are more often laterally located in the cerebellar hemispheres rather than arising from the midline vermis as in children. This matters surgically because a lateral tumor may be approached through a paramedian corridor rather than requiring a vermian split.

Now let us move to the clinical case. A thirty-five-year-old man presents with six weeks of progressive headache that is worse in the morning, associated with nausea and vomiting, and progressive gait unsteadiness. On examination, you find papilledema bilaterally, truncal ataxia with a wide-based gait, and bilateral upper extremity dysmetria that is worse on the left. MRI of the brain reveals a cystic lesion in the left cerebellar hemisphere with a brightly enhancing mural nodule, surrounded by peritumoral edema. There is obstructive hydrocephalus with dilation of the lateral and third ventricles. The fourth ventricle is compressed by the mass. His laboratory studies are notable for a hemoglobin of nineteen grams per deciliter. Take a moment to process this presentation because the examiner is about to start interrogating you.

The examiner opens with, "What is your differential diagnosis?" Your answer should be organized and confident. The leading diagnosis is hemangioblastoma. This is a young adult with a cystic cerebellar mass with a brightly enhancing mural nodule, which is the classic imaging appearance. The polycythemia with a hemoglobin of nineteen supports this diagnosis because hemangioblastomas secrete erythropoietin. Your differential should also include pilocytic astrocytoma, which can have a similar cystic-with-nodule appearance, but pilocytic astrocytoma is rare in adults and is primarily a pediatric tumor. You should mention metastasis with a cystic component, though this is less likely given the patient's age and absence of a known primary malignancy. You should also mention brain abscess, which can have a ring-enhancing appearance, but abscess characteristically shows restricted diffusion on DWI sequences, which would distinguish it. The polycythemia and the classic imaging pattern make hemangioblastoma your top diagnosis by a wide margin.

The examiner then asks, "This patient has obstructive hydrocephalus. How are you going to manage it?" This is a nuanced question and the examiner wants to see that you understand the options. Option one is to place an external ventricular drain preoperatively and then proceed to definitive tumor resection. This provides immediate intracranial pressure control and gives you a safety valve during the operation. Option two is to proceed directly to tumor resection without EVD placement, on the rationale that removing the obstructing mass will restore CSF flow. Option three, if surgery will be significantly delayed, is endoscopic third ventriculostomy. The critical point is that you should avoid placing a ventriculoperitoneal shunt as a first-line intervention. Tumor resection will almost always resolve the hydrocephalus, and a VP shunt commits the patient to a permanent device with its own complication profile. The most widely accepted approach, and the one you should articulate on boards, is to place an EVD at the start of the case and then proceed with tumor resection in the same operative session. This gives you ICP control intraoperatively and allows you to monitor CSF dynamics postoperatively before deciding whether the patient needs any further CSF diversion. You should also mention that you would administer dexamethasone, ten milligrams intravenously, to address peritumoral edema and its contribution to mass effect.

The examiner continues, "Describe your surgical approach." For a cerebellar hemisphere lesion, you would perform a midline or paramedian suboccipital craniotomy or craniectomy. Patient positioning is prone or lateral park bench. This naturally leads the examiner to ask about the sitting position, which is a classic board discussion point. The sitting position offers theoretical advantages including gravity-assisted drainage of blood and CSF from the operative field and improved visualization. However, it carries significant risks. Venous air embolism occurs in ten to forty-five percent of cases performed in the sitting position. Pneumocephalus is common. Hypotension from venous pooling can compromise cerebral perfusion. Perhaps most dangerously, paradoxical air embolism can occur through a patent foramen ovale, which is present in approximately twenty-five percent of the general population. A paradoxical air embolism can cause stroke. For these reasons, most modern centers have moved away from the sitting position for posterior fossa surgery. If the sitting position is used, mandatory monitoring includes precordial Doppler ultrasound, which is the most sensitive noninvasive monitor for venous air embolism, transesophageal echocardiography, and placement of a right atrial catheter for aspiration of entrained air. You should also mention that preoperative transthoracic echocardiography with bubble study should be performed to rule out a patent foramen ovale before using the sitting position.

If the examiner presents a scenario involving a fourth ventricle tumor, you need to know the telovelar approach. This approach accesses the fourth ventricle by dissecting the tela choroidea and the inferior medullary velum at the cerebellomedullary fissure. The key advantage is that it avoids splitting the cerebellar vermis. A transvermian approach carries the risk of cerebellar mutism syndrome, which is characterized by mutism, emotional lability, hypotonia, and ataxia, and is especially prevalent in the pediatric population. The telovelar approach provides excellent exposure of the fourth ventricle floor while preserving the vermis and minimizing the risk of cerebellar mutism.

Now here comes the most important surgical discussion, and this is where the trap questions live. The examiner asks, "Walk me through your resection of this hemangioblastoma." The cardinal rule is that hemangioblastomas must be resected en bloc. You must never biopsy a hemangioblastoma. You must never attempt piecemeal debulking. This tumor is essentially a ball of blood vessels, and violating its capsule will produce catastrophic hemorrhage that can be extremely difficult to control. The resection technique follows the same principles as arteriovenous malformation surgery. First, you circumferentially dissect around the nodule, identifying and coagulating the feeding arteries. You divide these feeding arteries as you work around the tumor. You progressively mobilize the nodule from the surrounding cerebellar parenchyma. Only after the nodule is fully mobilized and the arterial supply is controlled do you coagulate and divide the draining veins. Arteries first, veins last. If you take the veins before the arteries, the tumor will swell with trapped arterial blood and may rupture. The second critical point is that you resect only the enhancing mural nodule, not the cyst wall. The cyst wall is compressed cerebellar parenchyma. It is not tumor. After the nodule is removed, the cyst will typically collapse and resolve on its own.

The examiner will almost certainly hit you with at least one trap question on this topic. "You biopsy the mural nodule and it starts bleeding profusely. What happened?" Your answer: I violated the cardinal rule. Hemangioblastomas must be resected en bloc and must never be biopsied or debulked in a piecemeal fashion. The tumor is a dense tangle of vascular channels, and disrupting its architecture without circumferential arterial control produces hemorrhage that cannot be controlled with simple cautery. To manage this situation, I would apply hemostatic agents including thrombin-soaked gelfoam and oxidized cellulose, achieve temporary hemostasis, and then proceed with circumferential dissection to remove the entire nodule with its arterial feeders controlled. The examiner may also ask, "Would you resect the cyst wall?" Your answer is no. The cyst wall is compressed cerebellar parenchyma, not neoplastic tissue. Resecting it would cause unnecessary neural injury. You resect only the enhancing nodule, and the cyst resolves after nodule removal.

Another trap: "The patient has a single cerebellar hemangioblastoma. Do you need to test for VHL?" The answer is yes, absolutely. Twenty-five percent of all hemangioblastomas are VHL-associated, and a cerebellar hemangioblastoma may be the initial presenting feature of the syndrome. Genetic testing is indicated for all patients with hemangioblastoma, with particular urgency in young patients, those with multiple tumors, or those with a family history. VHL workup includes genetic testing for the VHL gene mutation on chromosome 3p25-26, ophthalmologic examination for retinal hemangioblastomas, abdominal imaging with CT or MRI to screen for renal clear cell carcinoma and pancreatic tumors, urine catecholamines and plasma metanephrines to screen for pheochromocytoma, and audiometry to evaluate for endolymphatic sac tumors. If VHL is confirmed, the patient requires lifelong surveillance imaging because new tumors will develop over time.

The examiner may pivot to adult medulloblastoma management. If you are presented with an adult medulloblastoma, the treatment paradigm is maximal safe resection followed by risk stratification. Standard risk is defined as near-total or gross-total resection with no evidence of disseminated disease on craniospinal MRI and negative CSF cytology. High risk includes subtotal resection, positive CSF cytology, or leptomeningeal dissemination. After resection, treatment consists of craniospinal irradiation at 23.4 Gray for standard risk or 36 Gray for high risk, with a posterior fossa boost to 54 to 55.8 Gray. Adjuvant chemotherapy follows radiation and typically includes cisplatin, vincristine, and cyclophosphamide. Molecular subtyping is increasingly important and may influence treatment decisions. The WNT subgroup has an excellent prognosis with greater than ninety percent five-year survival. The SHH subgroup is the most common in adults and is notable because in recurrence, hedgehog pathway inhibitors such as vismodegib can be used as targeted therapy. Group 3 carries the worst prognosis with high rates of leptomeningeal dissemination. Group 4 has an intermediate prognosis.

Let us discuss complications before moving on. Hemorrhage is the primary surgical complication with hemangioblastoma given its extreme vascularity. Cerebellar mutism syndrome can occur, especially with midline vermian approaches and particularly in the pediatric population, which is why the telovelar approach is preferred for fourth ventricle tumors. Lower cranial nerve palsies can result from surgery near the brainstem and fourth ventricle floor. CSF leak is a known complication of posterior fossa surgery, and meticulous dural closure, sometimes with dural grafting, is important. Persistent hydrocephalus can occur if the CSF pathways do not decompress adequately after tumor removal, requiring either repeat EVD placement or eventual VP shunt. Posterior fossa wound infections carry a higher risk than supratentorial wounds, in part due to the proximity to the nuchal musculature and the thicker soft tissue envelope.

Now let us cover the key evidence that supports these management principles. Lonser and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health have published extensively on the natural history and surgical management of VHL-associated hemangioblastomas. Their longitudinal studies, following patients for decades, established that these tumors grow in a saltatory pattern with periods of active growth alternating with prolonged periods of quiescence. The clinical implication is that not every hemangioblastoma in a VHL patient needs immediate resection. Intervention should be reserved for symptomatic tumors, tumors demonstrating progressive growth on serial imaging, or tumors abutting critical structures such as the brainstem or obex where further growth would make resection more dangerous. Asymptomatic, stable lesions can be followed with surveillance MRI, typically every six to twelve months. This is an important nuance on boards because it shows the examiner that you understand the difference between managing a sporadic hemangioblastoma, which you resect at diagnosis, versus managing VHL-associated hemangioblastomas, where a more conservative strategy is warranted because the patient will develop new tumors over their lifetime and you cannot resect every one of them.

Ammerman and colleagues published important surgical series on hemangioblastoma resection outcomes, reinforcing the en bloc resection principles and documenting that gross total resection rates exceed ninety percent with modern microsurgical technique, and that recurrence after complete resection of a sporadic hemangioblastoma is exceedingly rare. Recurrence in VHL patients is almost always a new tumor rather than true recurrence of a previously resected lesion.

For adult medulloblastoma, the work of Kool, Taylor, and colleagues on molecular subgrouping has fundamentally changed how we classify and treat these tumors. The WNT, SHH, Group 3, and Group 4 subgroups were defined through genomic profiling and carry distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications. Understanding these subgroups is essential because they inform not only prognosis but also therapeutic strategy. For instance, WNT-subgroup tumors may be candidates for treatment de-escalation given their excellent outcomes, sparing patients the long-term toxicity of full-dose craniospinal irradiation and chemotherapy. The PNET5 trial framework in Europe and the SJMB12 trial from St. Jude in the United States are key ongoing efforts to risk-stratify medulloblastoma treatment based on molecular subgroup. These trials are testing whether favorable-risk patients can receive reduced-dose craniospinal irradiation without sacrificing outcomes, and whether poor-risk patients benefit from intensified chemotherapy regimens. For the oral boards, you do not need to know the trial protocols in detail, but you need to demonstrate awareness that molecular subgrouping is the direction the field is moving and that it has concrete implications for treatment selection.

One additional evidence point worth knowing is the role of preoperative embolization for hemangioblastoma. Some centers advocate for preoperative angiography and embolization of large or solid hemangioblastomas to reduce intraoperative blood loss. The feeding arteries, most commonly branches of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery or the superior cerebellar artery, can be selectively embolized. However, this remains controversial. Embolization carries its own risks including stroke from nontarget embolization, and many experienced surgeons achieve excellent outcomes with microsurgical technique alone. On boards, it is reasonable to mention preoperative embolization as an option for large solid hemangioblastomas, but acknowledge that it is not universally practiced.

Let us close with the board pearls that you must have at your fingertips. Pearl number one: never biopsy a hemangioblastoma. These tumors are resected en bloc with circumferential arterial control before venous division, using the same principles as AVM surgery. Pearl number two: always screen for VHL in any patient with hemangioblastoma, even if it is a single sporadic-appearing tumor. Twenty-five percent are VHL-associated. Pearl number three: the cyst wall is not tumor. It is compressed cerebellar parenchyma. Resect only the enhancing mural nodule. Pearl number four: metastatic disease is the most common posterior fossa tumor in adults. Primary intra-axial posterior fossa tumors are led by hemangioblastoma. Pearl number five: the sitting position for posterior fossa surgery carries a ten to forty-five percent risk of venous air embolism and requires precordial Doppler, transesophageal echo, right atrial catheter, and preoperative bubble study to rule out patent foramen ovale. Most centers have moved away from it. Pearl number six: the telovelar approach to the fourth ventricle preserves the vermis and reduces the risk of cerebellar mutism compared to transvermian approaches. Pearl number seven: erythropoietin secretion by hemangioblastoma causes polycythemia in five to twenty percent of patients. An elevated hemoglobin in a patient with a posterior fossa mass is a board-level clue for hemangioblastoma. Pearl number eight: adult medulloblastoma is predominantly the SHH molecular subgroup, and WNT subgroup carries greater than ninety percent five-year survival. Know the four subgroups and their prognostic implications.

That covers posterior fossa tumors in adults. You know the differential, you know the surgical principles, you know the traps, and you know the evidence. You are ready for this one.
