questions:
  - q: What did Wiebe 2001 establish?
    a: The landmark NEJM randomised trial comparing surgery with continued medical therapy for temporal lobe epilepsy. Fifty-eight percent of surgical patients were seizure-free at one year against only eight percent in the medical group. It is the evidence that surgery is not a last resort.
    probe: What did Engel 2012 add?
    probeA: The ERSET trial, the Early Randomized Surgical Epilepsy Trial, published in JAMA in 2012. It randomised patients within two years of failing two drugs to surgery or continued medical therapy and found that none of the medical group were seizure-free at two years against about seventy-three percent of the surgical group. Its importance is twofold: it addresses early rather than long-standing refractory epilepsy, supporting prompt referral rather than years of drug cycling, and it had to be stopped early for slow recruitment, which is itself evidence of how reluctant the system is to refer these patients. It is a smaller trial than Wiebe and its numbers are correspondingly fragile, which I would acknowledge.
  - q: Recite the Engel classification.
    a: Class I: free of disabling seizures. Class II: rare disabling seizures, almost seizure-free. Class III: worthwhile improvement, a meaningful reduction in frequency. Class IV: no worthwhile improvement. When we quote surgical outcomes we almost always mean the percentage achieving Engel Class I.
    probe: Why does that convention matter when comparing series?
    probeA: Because series that quote Engel class I and II together, or that quote a favourable outcome, are reporting a materially different and more flattering figure than those quoting class I alone, and the difference can be ten or fifteen percentage points. Class II means rare disabling seizures, which sounds like success but still means the patient cannot drive and remains on medication, so from the patient's point of view it is not the same as freedom. Comparing a series reporting class I with one reporting class I and II is not a like-for-like comparison. The ILAE outcome classification exists partly to address this, and when quoting numbers I would always state which class and at what time point, since outcomes also decline with duration of follow-up.
  - q: Quote outcomes for temporal versus extratemporal surgery.
    a: Anterior temporal lobectomy achieves sixty to eighty percent Engel Class I. Extratemporal cases run forty to sixty percent — harder to localise, more variable semiology, and lower success rates. Quoting a single figure for epilepsy surgery misleads the patient.
    probe: Why are extratemporal cases harder?
    probeA: Several reasons together. The epileptogenic zone is less often defined by a discrete visible lesion, and MRI-negative cases are commoner. Scalp EEG localises the temporal lobe well but the frontal lobe poorly, because frontal seizures propagate extremely rapidly and bilaterally and can produce falsely lateralising or generalised patterns. Frontal semiology is brief, bizarre and often nocturnal, so it is frequently misdiagnosed as non-epileptic. The frontal lobe is large with extensive connections, so the resection required may be substantial. And eloquent cortex, whether motor, language or the supplementary motor area, constrains how much can be taken. The consequence is more frequent need for invasive monitoring and lower seizure freedom rates.
  - q: How does laser interstitial thermal therapy compare with open surgery?
    a: Minimally invasive with a typical hospital stay of one to two days, against several days for open surgery. Seizure-free rates are slightly lower — fifty-five to sixty-five percent Engel Class I against sixty to eighty percent for open anterior temporal lobectomy. It is a genuine trade-off of efficacy against morbidity.
    probe: Which patient would you steer toward laser therapy?
    probeA: Someone with a clear, well-localised mesial temporal target, particularly mesial temporal sclerosis, who places a high value on avoiding a craniotomy: an older patient or one with comorbidities that raise open surgical risk, a patient with a strong preference for a shorter recovery and faster return to work, and notably a patient with dominant-side disease and good preoperative verbal memory, because the neuropsychological data suggest laser ablation may be less damaging to naming and verbal memory than open anterior temporal lobectomy. It is also attractive for deep targets such as a hypothalamic hamartoma, periventricular nodular heterotopia, or an insular focus, where open surgery is difficult. I would steer away from it where the target is large, where there is a substantial neocortical component, or where I need tissue for histology, since ablation yields none.
  - q: What defines drug resistance, and why that threshold?
    a: Failure of two adequate trials of appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications. The threshold is probabilistic: roughly half respond to the first drug and about ten percent more to the second, after which the yield of each subsequent agent falls into the low single digits.
    probe: So what should happen the moment that threshold is crossed?
    probeA: Referral to a comprehensive epilepsy centre for presurgical evaluation. Not a third drug, and not a wait-and-see year. That is the entire practical purpose of defining drug resistance: it identifies the point at which the probability of success from another medication falls into the low single digits while the probability of benefit from surgery in a well-selected patient is sixty to eighty percent. The system fails at exactly this point, with median delays historically measured in many years, and ERSET had to be stopped early because patients were not being referred. So the answer is a referral, and the reason it is worth stating forcefully is that it is the commonest failure in the whole pathway.
  - q: What does a presurgical evaluation actually consist of?
    a: Convergence of independent lines of evidence: detailed semiology, video-EEG capturing habitual seizures, high-resolution MRI with an epilepsy protocol, PET showing interictal hypometabolism, ictal SPECT with SISCOM, magnetoencephalography, neuropsychology, and language lateralisation by functional MRI or the intracarotid amobarbital test.
    probe: What do you do when those lines disagree?
    probeA: I do not operate on discordant data. Discordance is itself the finding, and it means either that my hypothesis is wrong or that the epileptogenic zone is more complex than a single focus. In practice I would first re-review everything at a multidisciplinary epilepsy conference with the neurologists, neuroradiologists, neurophysiologists and neuropsychologists together, and re-examine the MRI against the electroclinical hypothesis, since targeted re-review converts a proportion of negative scans. Then I would add modalities that address the specific disagreement, such as FDG-PET, ictal SPECT with SISCOM, or magnetoencephalography. And if the noninvasive data still do not converge, that is the principal indication for invasive monitoring with stereo-EEG, which is designed exactly to adjudicate between competing hypotheses. Operating on discordant data is how you produce an Engel class IV outcome and a new deficit.
  - q: When do you go invasive?
    a: When the noninvasive data are discordant, when the MRI is negative, or when the suspected zone abuts eloquent cortex. Stereo-EEG samples deep and sulcal structures in three dimensions and allows bilateral exploration; subdural grids give contiguous surface coverage and extraoperative stimulation mapping.
    probe: What must you have before you implant electrodes?
    probeA: A specific anatomo-electro-clinical hypothesis, and for stereo-EEG a set of competing hypotheses that the implantation is designed to distinguish. Concretely: detailed semiology, scalp video-EEG capturing habitual seizures, an epilepsy-protocol MRI, and the relevant functional imaging, all reviewed together at a multidisciplinary meeting. Then vascular imaging co-registered with the planning MRI for trajectory planning, since avoiding vessels is what keeps the haemorrhage rate around one percent. And a clear plan for what each electrode is testing and what result would change the decision, because stereo-EEG only samples where you put it and cannot find what you did not suspect. I would also confirm that the patient understands a second operation will follow if a resectable focus is identified.
  - q: What are the specific risks of anterior temporal lobectomy?
    a: Visual field defect from injury to Meyer's loop, producing a contralateral superior quadrantanopia, which affects driving. Verbal memory decline after dominant-side resection, which is why neuropsychology is mandatory. Plus infection, haemorrhage, and the manipulation hemiparesis from retraction near the internal capsule.
    probe: How do you predict memory risk preoperatively?
    probeA: With formal neuropsychology first, establishing baseline verbal and non-verbal memory, since a patient with already poor verbal memory and a sclerotic dominant hippocampus has little further to lose, whereas a patient with intact verbal memory and a structurally normal-looking dominant hippocampus has a great deal to lose. That is the functional reserve principle: the better the ipsilateral hippocampus is working, the greater the risk of resecting it. Then language and memory lateralisation, historically by the intracarotid amobarbital or Wada test, which also assesses the contralateral hippocampus's capacity to support memory, and increasingly by functional MRI memory and language paradigms. MRI hippocampal volumetry and the presence or absence of mesial temporal sclerosis add further information. Combining baseline function, lateralisation and structural integrity gives a far better prediction than any one of them alone.
cases:
  - label: Refractory temporal lobe epilepsy
    stem: A 29-year-old woman has had seizures since age 14 despite trials of five antiseizure medications at adequate doses. Seizures begin with a rising epigastric sensation and déjà vu, followed by staring, lip-smacking and right hand dystonic posturing, lasting 90 seconds with postictal confusion. She has three to four per month. MRI shows left hippocampal atrophy with increased T2 signal.
    q1: Characterise the semiology and localise it.
    a1: Classical mesial temporal lobe seizures: an epigastric rising aura with déjà vu, oroalimentary automatisms, and contralateral dystonic posturing of the right hand, which lateralises to the left. With left hippocampal atrophy and T2 change, this is left mesial temporal sclerosis, and the semiology, imaging and lateralising sign are concordant.
    p1: Which lateralising signs are you relying on, and how reliable are they?
    pa1: Contralateral dystonic posturing of the limb is the strongest, with high lateralising value to the opposite hemisphere, so her right hand posturing points firmly to the left. Others with good reliability are postictal dysphasia, indicating a dominant hemisphere onset, ipsilateral automatisms particularly when combined with contralateral dystonia, and unilateral ictal eye blinking. Less reliable are head version, which lateralises contralaterally only when it is forced and sustained and occurs late before secondary generalisation, and postictal nose wiping, which is ipsilateral in most but not all. I would emphasise that no single sign is definitive and that their value lies in concordance with the EEG and the imaging, which is exactly what she has.
    q2: She has failed five drugs. What does that tell you and what should have happened sooner?
    a2: She met the definition of drug resistance — failure of two adequate trials of appropriately chosen, tolerated drugs — many years and three drugs ago. The yield of each further agent falls into the low single digits after the second. Wiebe 2001 showed fifty-eight percent seizure-free at one year with surgery against eight percent with continued medical therapy. She should have been referred years earlier.
    p2: Does five drugs make surgery more or less likely to work?
    pa2: Neither, and that is the important and counterintuitive point. The probability of a good surgical outcome depends on how well localised the epileptogenic zone is, not on how many drugs have failed, so a patient with concordant mesial temporal sclerosis has the same sixty to eighty percent chance of Engel class I whether she failed two drugs or five. What the extra drugs and the extra years have cost her is everything outside the seizures: fifteen years of driving restrictions, educational and occupational limitation, psychosocial harm, cumulative medication side effects, injury risk, and exposure to the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy. So the delay has not damaged her surgical prospects, it has damaged her life, and that is the argument for referring at the point of drug resistance rather than at the point of despair.
    q3: What does her presurgical workup need, and what specifically worries you?
    a3: Video-EEG capturing habitual seizures, epilepsy-protocol MRI, and neuropsychology with language and memory lateralisation by functional MRI or intracarotid amobarbital testing. What worries me is verbal memory: this is a dominant-hemisphere resection in a young woman, and decline in verbal memory is the principal cognitive risk, so I need to know her baseline and her contralateral reserve before committing.
    p3: Does she still need a Wada test, or is functional MRI enough?
    pa3: For language lateralisation, functional MRI has largely replaced the intracarotid amobarbital test and is generally sufficient in a cooperative patient with a clear result, particularly when it is concordant with handedness and the clinical picture. For memory the position is less settled: functional MRI memory paradigms are improving and have reasonable predictive value for postoperative verbal memory decline, but the Wada test retains a role because it uniquely tests whether the contralateral hippocampus can support memory when the ipsilateral one is anaesthetised, which is the specific question in a dominant resection. So in her case, with a dominant-side resection and intact preoperative verbal memory, which is the highest-risk combination, I would obtain functional MRI first and reserve Wada for a discordant, atypical or equivocal result, discussed at the multidisciplinary meeting.
    q4: Data are concordant. Offer her the options with honest numbers.
    a4: Anterior temporal lobectomy gives sixty to eighty percent Engel Class I seizure freedom, the highest figure available. Laser interstitial thermal therapy is minimally invasive with a one to two day stay, but Engel Class I rates are slightly lower at fifty-five to sixty-five percent. I would also warn her about a contralateral superior quadrantanopia from injury to Meyer's loop, which has implications for driving, and about verbal memory decline. Given concordant data and mesial temporal sclerosis, I would recommend open resection while presenting laser therapy as a genuine alternative.
    p4: She is 29 and drives. What do you tell her about that?
    pa4: That the driving question cuts both ways and she should have the facts before choosing. Currently, with three to four seizures a month, she should not be driving at all, and I would check whether she is and whether she has notified the licensing authority, which is her legal obligation. If surgery renders her seizure-free, most jurisdictions require a defined seizure-free interval, commonly one year, often with medication still being taken, before a licence can be restored, so surgery offers a genuine route back to driving that medication has not provided. Against that, the superior quadrantanopia from injury to Meyer's loop can itself be disqualifying in some jurisdictions depending on the extent of the field defect, so I would warn her that it is possible to become seizure-free and still fail the visual field standard. That is a specific and concrete reason to discuss the visual risk properly rather than as a footnote.

Let's work through epilepsy surgery evaluation. This is a core functional neurosurgery topic and a near-guaranteed appearance on your oral boards. You need to know the presurgical workup inside and out, understand when to move from Phase I to Phase II, know your surgical options and their outcomes cold, and be ready for the trap questions the examiners love to throw at candidates on this topic.

We'll start with the foundation, walk through a clinical case, then I'll hammer you with examiner-style questions the way they'll come at you in the viva.

Let's begin with the conceptual foundation. Drug-resistant epilepsy is defined as the failure of two adequate trials of appropriately chosen antiseizure medications at therapeutic doses. This is not two medications tried at subtherapeutic levels. This is not two medications from the same class. This means two drugs, appropriate for the seizure type, pushed to therapeutic or maximally tolerated doses, and the patient continues to have seizures. Roughly 30 percent of all epilepsy patients meet this definition, which is an enormous number of people.

Here is the critical point that examiners want you to understand: surgery for drug-resistant epilepsy is massively underutilized. This is not a controversial statement. Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the superiority of surgical intervention over continued medical therapy for drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy. The landmark trial is Wiebe 2001 in the New England Journal of Medicine. In that study, 58 percent of patients randomized to surgery were seizure-free at one year compared to only 8 percent in the continued medical therapy group. That is a staggering difference, and it is Level I evidence. Despite this, the average patient with drug-resistant epilepsy waits over 20 years before being referred for surgical evaluation. You should view epilepsy surgery not as a last resort but as an evidence-based treatment that should be offered early to appropriate candidates.

Now, mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, abbreviated MTLE, is the single most common surgically treatable epilepsy syndrome. The pathological hallmark is hippocampal sclerosis, which you identify on MRI as T2 and FLAIR hyperintensity of the hippocampus with associated volume loss. The seizure semiology of MTLE is classic and you must know it. The typical sequence is an aura, most commonly a rising epigastric sensation or a feeling of deja vu, followed by behavioral arrest, then oroalimentary automatisms such as lip smacking and chewing, contralateral hand dystonia, and in some cases progression to secondary generalization. When you see this semiology pattern, your differential diagnosis for the seizure focus starts and often ends with the mesial temporal lobe.

Extratemporal epilepsy encompasses frontal, parietal, occipital, and insular epilepsy. These cases are significantly more challenging to localize, the seizure semiology is more variable, and the surgical success rates are lower, typically in the range of 40 to 60 percent Engel Class I outcomes compared to 60 to 80 percent for temporal lobe epilepsy.

You need to know the Engel classification system completely because examiners will reference it and expect you to define it precisely. Engel Class I means seizure-free. Within Class I, IA is completely seizure-free, IB means auras only persist, IC means some seizures occurred in the early postoperative period but the patient has been seizure-free since, and ID means seizure-free only after a medication adjustment was made. Engel Class II means rare seizures. Engel Class III means worthwhile improvement, meaning a meaningful reduction in seizure frequency. Engel Class IV means no worthwhile improvement. When we talk about surgical outcomes, we are almost always talking about the percentage of patients achieving Engel Class I.

Now let's move into the clinical case. You are presented with a 32-year-old patient who has had epilepsy since age 14. That is 18 years of disease. The patient has failed three antiseizure medications at adequate doses: levetiracetam, lacosamide, and lamotrigine. Three failed medications clearly meets the definition of drug-resistant epilepsy. The seizure semiology is classic for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy: an epigastric aura followed by right hand automatisms, behavioral arrest, and occasional secondary generalization. The MRI shows left hippocampal sclerosis with volume loss and FLAIR hyperintensity. Scalp video-EEG monitoring captures four habitual seizures, all demonstrating left temporal onset.

Pause and take stock of what you have. You have a patient with drug-resistant epilepsy, classic MTLE semiology lateralizing to the left temporal lobe, MRI confirmation of left hippocampal sclerosis, and concordant scalp EEG showing left temporal seizure onset. This is a highly concordant dataset pointing to a single epileptogenic focus.

Now we enter the examiner interrogation phase, and this is where the depth of your knowledge will be tested.

The examiner asks you to walk through the Phase I evaluation for this patient. Phase I is the non-invasive presurgical workup, and you must know every component.

First, video-EEG monitoring. The patient is admitted to an epilepsy monitoring unit, medications are often weaned to provoke seizures, and you need to capture a minimum of three to five habitual seizures. You are looking for the ictal onset pattern and its localization, as well as interictal epileptiform discharges. In this case, four seizures were captured, all with left temporal onset.

Second, 3T MRI with a dedicated epilepsy protocol. This is not a standard brain MRI. The epilepsy protocol includes thin-cut coronal FLAIR and T2 sequences through the hippocampi, volumetric analysis for hippocampal asymmetry, and careful evaluation for hippocampal sclerosis, focal cortical dysplasia, low-grade tumors, and vascular malformations. The higher field strength of 3T compared to 1.5T significantly improves detection of subtle lesions, particularly cortical dysplasia.

Third, FDG-PET. This is an interictal study showing hypometabolism in the epileptogenic zone. PET is 70 to 80 percent sensitive for temporal lobe epilepsy and can lateralize the seizure focus even when the MRI is normal. Concordant PET hypometabolism improves surgical outcomes. In our case, you would expect to see left temporal hypometabolism, further confirming the lateralization.

Fourth, neuropsychological testing. This serves multiple purposes: establishing baseline cognitive function, helping lateralize language and memory dominance, and predicting postoperative cognitive deficits. For a left anterior temporal lobectomy, the primary cognitive risk is verbal memory decline. For a right anterior temporal lobectomy, the risk is visuospatial memory decline. The neuropsychological profile helps you counsel the patient and plan the surgical approach.

Fifth, the Wada test, also known as the intracarotid amobarbital test. You inject amobarbital into the internal carotid artery, producing temporary anesthesia of the ipsilateral hemisphere, and then test language and memory function. The Wada test answers two questions: which hemisphere is dominant for language, and can the contralateral hippocampus independently support memory if you resect the ipsilateral hippocampus. If the contralateral hippocampus cannot support memory independently, there is a high risk of global amnesia with anterior temporal lobectomy. For language lateralization, fMRI has largely replaced the Wada test at most centers because it is non-invasive and highly reliable for this purpose. However, the Wada test remains the gold standard for memory adequacy assessment. Know this distinction for your boards.

Sixth, magnetoencephalography, or MEG. This technique provides magnetic source imaging through dipole source localization and is especially useful in extratemporal epilepsy where the seizure focus may be harder to localize with scalp EEG alone.

Seventh, ictal SPECT. A radiotracer is injected during a seizure, and the scan shows ictal hyperperfusion at the seizure focus. The technique called SISCOM, which stands for subtraction ictal SPECT coregistered to MRI, subtracts the interictal SPECT from the ictal SPECT and overlays the result on the MRI to precisely localize the seizure focus.

Now the examiner asks: given your Phase I data, what is your plan? In this case, all data are concordant. The EEG shows left temporal onset, the MRI shows left hippocampal sclerosis, and you expect concordant PET hypometabolism. When your Phase I data are concordant, you proceed directly to surgery without Phase II intracranial monitoring. This is a key decision point and examiners want to hear you say it with confidence.

The examiner then asks: when would you need Phase II monitoring? You need Phase II when the non-invasive data are discordant, when there are bilateral EEG abnormalities that make lateralization uncertain, when the MRI is normal, meaning there is no structural lesion, when you are dealing with extratemporal epilepsy and localization is uncertain, or when the seizure focus is near eloquent cortex and you need functional mapping.

Now walk through Phase II options. Subdural grids and strips are placed via craniotomy. They provide excellent cortical surface mapping for motor and language function through direct cortical stimulation. However, they have limited depth penetration, meaning they cannot sample deep structures well. The complication rates are higher: infection in 3 to 5 percent, hemorrhage in 2 to 4 percent. They require a full craniotomy for placement and another for removal.

Stereo-EEG, abbreviated SEEG, uses robot-assisted placement of depth electrodes through small twist drill holes. The electrode placement is hypothesis-driven, meaning you develop a hypothesis about the epileptogenic network and place electrodes to test that hypothesis. SEEG is superior for sampling deep structures including the hippocampus, insula, cingulate gyrus, and orbitofrontal cortex. It allows bilateral hemispheric sampling through the same procedure. The complication rates are lower: hemorrhage approximately 1 percent, infection less than 1 percent. The limitation is that you cannot perform direct cortical stimulation mapping in the traditional sense, though you can do indirect mapping through the electrode contacts. SEEG has become the increasingly preferred approach at most epilepsy centers.

The examiner now asks about surgical options for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy.

Standard anterior temporal lobectomy, ATL, is the most studied procedure. On the dominant side, you resect 4 to 4.5 centimeters of temporal lobe measured from the temporal tip. On the non-dominant side, you can extend to 5 to 6 centimeters. The resection includes an amygdalohippocampectomy. Seizure-free outcomes are 60 to 80 percent Engel Class I. This is the benchmark against which all other procedures are compared.

Selective amygdalohippocampectomy, SAH, aims to remove only the mesial temporal structures, the amygdala and hippocampus, while preserving the lateral temporal neocortex. Approaches include the transsylvian, subtemporal, and transcortical routes. The theoretical advantage is reduced cognitive decline, particularly less naming difficulty, because the lateral temporal cortex is preserved. Some studies show equivalent seizure-free rates compared to standard ATL, and there may be less naming decline, though the data are mixed.

Laser interstitial thermal therapy, LITT, is an MRI-guided stereotactic procedure in which a laser fiber is placed into the amygdala and hippocampus and thermal energy is used to ablate the mesial structures. This is a minimally invasive approach with a typical hospital stay of just 1 to 2 days. Seizure-free rates are slightly lower than open ATL, in the range of 55 to 65 percent Engel Class I compared to 60 to 80 percent for open surgery. However, LITT offers several advantages: less cognitive decline, particularly less verbal memory decline after dominant-side procedures, faster recovery, and the ability to ablate targets identified by SEEG even in MRI-negative patients. LITT use is growing rapidly and is an increasingly important part of the epilepsy surgery armamentarium.

Responsive neurostimulation, or RNS, using the NeuroPace device is indicated for patients with bilateral temporal lobe epilepsy or seizure foci in eloquent cortex where resection would cause unacceptable neurological deficits. The device detects electrographic seizure onset patterns and delivers targeted stimulation to abort the seizure. Outcomes show a median 53 percent seizure reduction at 2 years and 66 percent at 6 years, with continued improvement over time. RNS is not curative. It is a palliative neuromodulation strategy.

Vagus nerve stimulation, VNS, is another palliative neuromodulation option for patients who are not candidates for resective surgery. It provides an average 50 percent seizure reduction in responders. It is adjunctive and not curative.

Now the examiner sets traps for you. Listen carefully to these because they test your judgment, not just your knowledge.

Trap question one. The patient has bilateral hippocampal sclerosis on MRI. Would you proceed with anterior temporal lobectomy? The answer is no, not without extensive further investigation. Bilateral mesial temporal sclerosis raises the concern for bilateral independent temporal lobe epilepsy. You need prolonged video-EEG monitoring to determine whether seizures arise from one side or both. If monitoring demonstrates bilateral independent seizure onset, the patient is not a candidate for unilateral ATL. In that scenario, you would consider RNS with electrodes in both temporal lobes. Bilateral anterior temporal lobectomy is absolutely contraindicated because bilateral hippocampal resection causes devastating global amnesia, the equivalent of a bilateral medial temporal lobe syndrome.

Trap question two. The Wada test shows the contralateral hippocampus cannot independently support memory. What do you do? This is a high-risk scenario for global amnesia if you proceed with standard ATL. You do not abandon the patient, but you pivot to less destructive surgical strategies. Consider LITT, which is more selective and associated with less memory decline than open resection. Consider selective amygdalohippocampectomy, which spares more tissue. Consider RNS as a neuromodulation alternative. The key board answer is: do not proceed with standard anterior temporal lobectomy if the Wada test demonstrates contralateral memory failure.

Trap question three. A 10-year-old child has drug-resistant epilepsy, daily seizures, and the MRI shows hemimegalencephaly. What procedure do you recommend? The answer is functional hemispherectomy, also called hemispheric disconnection. This is the most effective procedure for hemispheric epilepsy syndromes including hemimegalencephaly, Rasmussen encephalitis, Sturge-Weber syndrome, and large hemispheric cortical dysplasias. Seizure-free rates are 70 to 80 percent. The prerequisite is preoperative demonstration that the affected hemisphere has minimal useful function, which in hemimegalencephaly is typically the case because the malformed hemisphere functions poorly from the outset.

Before we leave the interrogation section, know the complications of anterior temporal lobectomy. Infection and hemorrhage are standard surgical risks. The specific neurological deficit to know is superior quadrantanopsia, which occurs from disruption of Meyer's loop, the anterior bundle of the optic radiation that curves forward into the temporal lobe. This visual field deficit occurs in approximately 50 percent of ATL patients. Verbal memory decline is the primary cognitive risk with dominant-side ATL. Naming difficulties can occur, particularly with dominant-side procedures. Mood changes including depression and anxiety are common postoperatively. And psychosocial adjustment issues can arise even when seizures are controlled, because the patient's identity and social structure may have been built around their epilepsy diagnosis.

Let's move to the evidence you should cite. Wiebe 2001 in the New England Journal of Medicine is the landmark randomized controlled trial comparing surgery to continued medical therapy for temporal lobe epilepsy. This is Level I evidence showing 58 percent seizure-free with surgery versus 8 percent with medical management. The ERSET trial, early randomized surgical epilepsy trial, reinforced the benefit of early surgical intervention. Engel 2012 in JAMA provided further evidence supporting early surgery for drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy. The Gross series established outcomes for LITT in mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. Heck and colleagues published the long-term outcomes data for RNS showing progressive improvement over years of treatment. Spencer and colleagues provided important comparison data for SEEG versus subdural grid monitoring approaches.

Let's close with the board pearls you must have at your fingertips. Drug-resistant epilepsy equals failure of two adequate medication trials. When Phase I data are concordant, proceed directly to surgery without intracranial monitoring. The Wada test is for memory assessment, fMRI is for language lateralization. Anterior temporal lobectomy achieves 60 to 80 percent Engel Class I seizure-free outcomes. Bilateral temporal lobe epilepsy is an indication for RNS, never bilateral resection. LITT offers slightly lower seizure-free rates than open ATL but with less cognitive morbidity and faster recovery. SEEG is increasingly preferred over subdural grids for intracranial monitoring because of lower complication rates and superior deep structure sampling. Superior quadrantanopsia from Meyer's loop disruption occurs in about half of ATL patients. And always remember: surgery for drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy is supported by Level I evidence and is dramatically underutilized.

That covers epilepsy surgery evaluation. You're ready for this one.
