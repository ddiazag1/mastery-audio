Let's work through meningioma grading and surgical planning. This is a high-yield oral board topic because meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumor, accounting for thirty-seven percent of all primary brain tumors. They arise from arachnoid cap cells of the meninges. There is a clear female predominance, roughly two to one, and incidence increases with age. Risk factors include prior ionizing radiation and neurofibromatosis type 2, the latter associated with multiple meningiomas. Now, understanding the WHO grading system and the Simpson grading system is absolutely essential, so let's build that foundation first.

The WHO 2021 classification divides meningiomas into three grades. Grade 1 meningiomas are the benign tumors, comprising eighty to eighty-five percent of all meningiomas. Histological subtypes within Grade 1 include meningothelial, fibrous, transitional, psammomatous, angiomatous, microcystic, secretory, lymphoplasmacyte-rich, and metaplastic. These subtypes are distinguished by their histological patterns, but all share the same favorable prognosis as long as they do not meet criteria for a higher grade.

Grade 2 meningiomas are atypical, representing fifteen to twenty percent of cases. This is where the grading criteria become critical for boards. A meningioma is classified as WHO Grade 2 if it meets any one of the following three criteria. First, a mitotic count of four to nineteen mitoses per ten high-power fields. Second, brain invasion, which was added as a standalone criterion in the WHO 2016 update and retained in 2021. Third, the presence of three or more of the following five histological features: increased cellularity, small cell change with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, sheeting or loss of the normal whorling architecture, and foci of spontaneous or geographic necrosis. Additionally, two specific subtypes are automatically classified as Grade 2 regardless of mitotic count: the clear cell variant and the chordoid variant.

Grade 3 meningiomas are anaplastic or malignant, comprising only one to three percent of cases. The defining feature is a mitotic count of twenty or more mitoses per ten high-power fields, or the presence of frank anaplastic features such as sarcomatous or carcinomatous morphology. The papillary and rhabdoid subtypes are automatically classified as Grade 3.

Now let's talk about where these tumors occur. Convexity meningiomas account for twenty to thirty-four percent, parasagittal and falcine meningiomas for twenty-two to thirty percent, sphenoid wing for seventeen to twenty-five percent, posterior fossa for about ten percent, and olfactory groove for eight to thirteen percent. Other important locations include tuberculum sellae, cerebellopontine angle, petroclival, tentorial, and foramen magnum. The location determines the surgical approach, the neurovascular structures at risk, and the likelihood of achieving a complete resection.

On imaging, the classic meningioma appearance is a dural-based extra-axial mass with broad dural attachment. On MRI, these tumors are typically isointense to gray matter on T1-weighted images and isointense to slightly hyperintense on T2. They show intense homogeneous enhancement with gadolinium. The dural tail sign, a linear enhancement of thickened dura extending away from the tumor margin, is present in sixty to seventy percent of cases. It is suggestive but not pathognomonic for meningioma. On CT, look for calcification within the tumor and hyperostosis of the adjacent bone. The CSF cleft sign, a rim of cerebrospinal fluid between the tumor and the underlying brain, confirms the extra-axial location and distinguishes meningiomas from intra-axial tumors.

Now let's lock in the Simpson grading system, which describes the extent of surgical resection. Simpson Grade I is gross total resection with complete excision of the dural attachment, any abnormal bone, and the involved venous sinus if applicable. The recurrence rate at ten years is nine percent. Simpson Grade II is gross total resection with coagulation of the dural attachment rather than excision. The recurrence rate rises to nineteen percent. Simpson Grade III is gross total resection of the tumor mass without any treatment of the dural attachment, carrying a twenty-nine percent recurrence rate. Simpson Grade IV is subtotal resection, meaning tumor is intentionally left behind, with a forty-four percent recurrence rate. Simpson Grade V is biopsy only or simple decompression. A critical nuance for boards is that the Simpson grade is most prognostically significant for WHO Grade 1 meningiomas. For Grade 2 and Grade 3 tumors, recurrence rates are high regardless of the Simpson grade achieved, and adjuvant therapy enters the picture.

Let's move into a clinical case. A fifty-two-year-old woman presents with progressive right-sided anosmia over the past year. Her family reports subtle personality changes, including disinhibition and poor judgment, which she herself does not recognize. She has had mild bilateral frontal headaches. A CT scan shows hyperostosis of the planum sphenoidale with a large calcified mass. MRI demonstrates a five-centimeter olfactory groove meningioma with homogeneous gadolinium enhancement, a broad dural base along the planum and cribriform plate, significant bilateral frontal lobe edema, and compression of both frontal lobes. The optic chiasm is displaced inferiorly but not encased. There is no evidence of encasement of the anterior cerebral arteries, though they are displaced posteriorly. This is a symptomatic tumor causing anosmia, personality changes from bifrontal compression, and mass effect. Surgery is indicated.

Now let's get into the examiner interrogation, because this is where the board examiners will probe your decision-making.

First, when is observation appropriate? You observe meningiomas that are small, generally less than three centimeters, asymptomatic, and discovered incidentally, particularly in elderly patients with significant medical comorbidities. The natural history of untreated meningiomas is a growth rate of approximately two to four millimeters per year, though many remain stable for years. Serial MRI surveillance is performed every six months for the first year and then annually if stable. The decision to intervene is triggered by the development of symptoms, evidence of accelerating growth, or significant peritumoral edema.

Second, when do you recommend stereotactic radiosurgery? SRS is appropriate for meningiomas less than three centimeters in maximum diameter, and ideally less than two and a half centimeters. The tumor must be at least two to three millimeters away from the optic chiasm and optic nerves, because the radiation tolerance of the anterior visual pathway is limited to eight to ten Gray in a single fraction. SRS is an excellent option for residual or recurrent tumor after surgery, for tumors in surgically challenging locations such as the cavernous sinus, and for patients who are poor surgical candidates due to age or comorbidities. The typical marginal dose is twelve to fourteen Gray delivered in a single fraction. Long-term tumor control rates are ninety to ninety-five percent at five to ten years for WHO Grade 1 meningiomas. Control rates are lower for Grade 2 and Grade 3 tumors. Fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy is preferred when the tumor is near the optic apparatus and single-fraction SRS cannot be safely delivered.

Third, let's plan the surgery for this olfactory groove case. The approach of choice is a bifrontal craniotomy, which provides wide bilateral exposure of the anterior cranial base. An extended subfrontal approach or a subfrontal interhemispheric variant can also be considered depending on surgeon preference and tumor extension. Some surgeons advocate a unilateral subfrontal approach for smaller olfactory groove meningiomas, but a five-centimeter tumor with bilateral frontal involvement warrants bilateral exposure. The key surgical steps begin with early devascularization. The primary blood supply to olfactory groove meningiomas comes from the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries, which are branches of the ophthalmic artery. These feeders enter the tumor along the planum sphenoidale and cribriform plate. You coagulate these dural feeders early in the operation by working along the skull base before addressing the tumor capsule. After devascularization, you debulk the center of the tumor using an ultrasonic aspirator or suction to create internal decompression. Once the tumor is reduced in volume, you carefully dissect the capsule away from the surrounding brain, working from the periphery inward. The critical neurovascular structures to protect are the A2 segments of the anterior cerebral arteries, which are typically displaced posteriorly and superiorly by the tumor, and the optic chiasm, which is pushed inferiorly. The frontopolar branches of the anterior cerebral arteries may be draped over the superior capsule and must be identified and preserved. After tumor removal, the skull base defect along the cribriform plate and planum must be reconstructed to prevent a postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leak. A vascularized pericranial flap is the workhorse for this reconstruction. The flap is harvested at the beginning of the operation, pedicled on the supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries, and laid over the skull base defect at closure.

Fourth, let's discuss preoperative embolization. The role of embolization depends entirely on the tumor location and the accessibility of the feeding vessels to the endovascular catheter. Embolization is most useful for convexity and parasagittal meningiomas that receive their blood supply from the middle meningeal artery, which is easily catheterized. It reduces intraoperative blood loss and can soften the tumor, making resection technically easier. Embolization is typically performed twenty-four to seventy-two hours before surgery. For skull base meningiomas like this olfactory groove case, embolization has limited utility because the ethmoidal artery feeders are too small and too dangerous to catheterize safely, and the risk of inadvertent embolization of the ophthalmic artery is unacceptable.

Fifth, parasagittal and falcine meningiomas deserve special attention because of their relationship to the superior sagittal sinus. The critical question is whether the sinus is patent or occluded. If the tumor has completely occluded the superior sagittal sinus and collateral venous drainage has developed, you can resect the involved segment of sinus along with the tumor to achieve a Simpson Grade I resection. However, if the sinus remains patent, you cannot sacrifice it, particularly in the posterior third. The posterior third of the superior sagittal sinus receives the bilateral cortical venous drainage from the parietal and occipital lobes and is the confluence point for the vein of Labbe and other critical bridging veins. Sacrifice of the posterior third of the superior sagittal sinus will cause bilateral venous infarction, massive brain swelling, and likely death. For tumors invading a patent sinus, the correct strategy is to resect all extrasinus tumor, leaving the intrasinus component, and follow with stereotactic radiosurgery or serial imaging. The anterior third of the superior sagittal sinus can be sacrificed with much less risk because venous drainage in this region is less critical and collateral pathways exist.

Sixth, let's run through skull base approach selection by location. For sphenoid wing meningiomas, the pterional craniotomy is the standard approach, with an orbitozygomatic extension for larger tumors or those with significant middle fossa extension. For tuberculum sellae meningiomas, you can use either a subfrontal approach through a craniotomy or an extended transsphenoidal endoscopic approach, which has gained popularity for smaller midline tumors with the advantage of avoiding brain retraction. For petroclival meningiomas, options include the retrosigmoid approach for laterally positioned tumors and the presigmoid transpetrosal approach for tumors with significant medial extension anterior to the brainstem. For foramen magnum meningiomas, the far-lateral approach provides exposure of the anterior foramen magnum while minimizing cerebellar retraction. For cerebellopontine angle meningiomas, the retrosigmoid approach is standard.

Now let's address the trap questions that examiners love to use.

The examiner says: you achieve a Simpson Grade II resection of a WHO Grade 2 atypical meningioma. Is the patient done with treatment? The answer is no. WHO Grade 2 atypical meningiomas have recurrence rates of thirty to forty percent at five years even after gross total resection. Adjuvant radiation therapy is indicated after subtotal resection of Grade 2 meningiomas, and it should be strongly considered even after gross total resection. The RTOG 0539 trial stratified meningiomas by risk: Grade 1 meningiomas with gross total resection were low risk, Grade 2 meningiomas with gross total resection were intermediate risk, and Grade 2 with subtotal resection or any Grade 3 meningioma were high risk. The intermediate risk group is the most debated. Current practice increasingly favors adjuvant radiation for Grade 2 meningiomas after gross total resection, and it is essentially mandatory after subtotal resection. For Grade 3 anaplastic meningiomas, adjuvant radiation with fifty-four to sixty Gray of fractionated external beam radiotherapy is the standard of care after any extent of resection.

The examiner then asks: the parasagittal meningioma invades the posterior third of the superior sagittal sinus. Would you resect the sinus? The answer is absolutely not. As we covered, the posterior third of the superior sagittal sinus is not sacrificeable. Bilateral cortical venous drainage, including the vein of Labbe, makes sinus sacrifice in this location catastrophic. You resect all accessible tumor outside the sinus, leave the intrasinus component, and treat the residual with stereotactic radiosurgery or monitor with serial imaging.

The examiner presents another scenario: a seventy-eight-year-old has a two-centimeter asymptomatic convexity meningioma found incidentally on imaging for an unrelated complaint. What do you recommend? The answer is observation with serial imaging. This is a small, asymptomatic, likely slow-growing tumor in an elderly patient. The risks of surgery, including general anesthesia, craniotomy complications, and recovery time, outweigh the benefits in this clinical context. You obtain MRI at six months and then annually. You intervene only if the patient develops symptoms, the tumor shows rapid growth beyond the expected two to four millimeters per year, or significant peritumoral edema develops.

Regarding complications, the major risks of meningioma surgery are location-specific neurological deficit, cerebrospinal fluid leak occurring in ten to fifteen percent of skull base cases, venous infarction from injury to the sagittal sinus or cortical bridging veins, postoperative hemorrhage, tumor recurrence, and seizures. Perioperative seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam is commonly used for supratentorial meningiomas, though the evidence supporting routine prophylaxis is debated.

Let's review the key evidence. The Simpson grading system comes from Donald Simpson's 1957 series of two hundred sixty-five meningiomas, which established the relationship between extent of resection and recurrence. This remains foundational to meningioma surgery. RTOG 0539 is the landmark trial for risk-adapted adjuvant radiation therapy after meningioma resection. It stratified patients into low, intermediate, and high risk groups and demonstrated that adjuvant radiation significantly reduces recurrence for intermediate and high risk groups. The intermediate risk group, which includes WHO Grade 2 meningiomas after gross total resection and recurrent WHO Grade 1 meningiomas, received fifty-four Gray of intensity-modulated radiation therapy with excellent local control. Kondziolka's long-term stereotactic radiosurgery series demonstrated ninety-three percent tumor control at ten years for benign meningiomas treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery at a median marginal dose of fourteen Gray, establishing SRS as a definitive treatment for small meningiomas. The 2021 EANO guidelines from Goldbrunner and colleagues provide the most current European consensus on meningioma management, including observation criteria, surgical indications, adjuvant radiation thresholds, and follow-up schedules stratified by WHO grade and Simpson grade.

Let's close with the board pearls you must have at your fingertips. Know every Simpson grade cold: Grade I is complete removal with dural and bone excision at nine percent recurrence, Grade II is complete removal with dural coagulation at nineteen percent, Grade III is complete removal without dural treatment at twenty-nine percent, Grade IV is subtotal resection at forty-four percent, Grade V is biopsy only. Know the WHO grading criteria: Grade 1 has fewer than four mitoses per ten high-power fields and no atypical features, Grade 2 requires four to nineteen mitoses or brain invasion or three of five histological criteria, Grade 3 requires twenty or more mitoses or anaplastic morphology. Know that SRS is limited to tumors under three centimeters with at least two to three millimeters of clearance from the optic apparatus. The dose limit for optic structures is eight to ten Gray single fraction. The standard SRS marginal dose is twelve to fourteen Gray. Never sacrifice the posterior third of the superior sagittal sinus. Grade 2 meningiomas need adjuvant radiation discussion regardless of resection extent. Grade 3 meningiomas always get adjuvant radiation. Observation is appropriate for small, asymptomatic tumors in elderly patients. Preoperative embolization is useful for convexity tumors fed by the middle meningeal artery but has limited utility for skull base tumors with ethmoidal artery feeders.
