Let's work through depressed skull fracture management. This is a bread-and-butter trauma topic for the oral boards, and the examiners expect you to move quickly through indications, operative technique, and the classic pitfalls. We'll build from foundational anatomy, walk through a clinical case, and then I'll push you with the kinds of interrogation questions that separate a pass from a fail.

Starting with the foundation. A depressed skull fracture is defined as a fracture in which bone fragments are displaced inward below the level of the surrounding calvarium. The critical first distinction is open versus closed. An open fracture, also called a compound fracture, means there is an overlying scalp laceration that communicates directly with the fracture site. This makes it a contaminated wound by definition and mandates surgical debridement. A closed fracture, also called a simple fracture, has intact overlying skin. This distinction drives nearly every management decision you will make.

Depressed skull fractures account for 5 to 10 percent of all skull fractures. The most common location is the frontoparietal convexity, which makes anatomic sense because this is where the skull is thinnest and most exposed. The typical mechanism is a focal impact from a small surface area object, something like a hammer, pipe, baseball bat, or the edge of a curb. This concentrates force into a small region and drives bone inward rather than producing a linear propagation.

Classification depends on the degree of depression, which is measured relative to the inner table of the surrounding intact skull. The depression becomes clinically significant when it exceeds the full thickness of the skull, which is one table width, typically 6 to 10 millimeters depending on the patient and the location on the calvarium. This is the threshold that drives surgical decision-making.

Imaging is straightforward. CT with bone windows and three-dimensional reconstruction is the best study. Plain films are obsolete for this purpose. On CT you are looking for several things simultaneously: the degree of depression in millimeters, whether fragments are comminuted or in a single piece, the presence of intracranial air which indicates dural violation until proven otherwise, underlying brain contusion or hematoma, and the relationship of the fracture to venous sinuses and the frontal sinus.

Associated injuries are common and must be actively sought. Dural tears are present in approximately 30 percent of depressed skull fractures. Underlying brain contusion is frequent given that the bone has been driven into the cortical surface. Epidural hematoma can form at the fracture margins. Pneumocephalus, meaning intracranial air, is a critical finding because it confirms that the dura has been violated and there is communication between the intracranial space and the external environment. For parasagittal fractures, you must evaluate the superior sagittal sinus for injury, and this has direct implications for operative planning that we will discuss in detail.

Before we leave the foundation, let me broaden the context with the other skull fracture types you need to know cold. Linear skull fractures are the most common type overall and are usually managed conservatively. They require intervention only when they cross a vascular channel and cause an epidural hematoma, or when they cross a venous sinus.

Basilar skull fractures are a clinical diagnosis. Anterior fossa fractures produce raccoon eyes, which are bilateral periorbital ecchymoses. Middle fossa fractures produce Battle sign, which is ecchymosis over the mastoid process. CSF rhinorrhea indicates a fracture through the cribriform plate or the sphenoid sinus. CSF otorrhea and hemotympanum indicate a temporal bone fracture. Cranial nerve seven and eight palsies point to a petrous bone fracture. Most basilar skull fractures heal spontaneously. CSF leaks associated with basilar fractures resolve in 85 percent of cases within 7 to 10 days with conservative management: head of bed elevation, strict instructions to avoid straining, sneezing, and nose blowing, and placement of a lumbar drain if the leak persists beyond 7 days. Prophylactic antibiotics for basilar skull fractures with CSF leak is a classic board question. The Cochrane review demonstrates no benefit from prophylactic antibiotics. Most centers do not give them. The correct approach is to withhold prophylactic antibiotics but treat meningitis aggressively if it develops.

One more entity to lock in before we move to the case. The growing skull fracture, also called a leptomeningeal cyst, is unique to children under 3 years of age. The pathophysiology is elegant and testable. A dural tear occurs in combination with a skull fracture. Normal brain pulsations then prevent both the dura and the bone from healing. Over weeks to months, progressive diastasis of the fracture occurs. Brain herniates through the widening gap and creates a palpable skull defect that enlarges over time. The parents notice a soft, growing lump at the old fracture site. Treatment is surgical: dural repair plus cranioplasty. This is a favorite board question and you should be able to describe the pathophysiology in one clean sentence.

Now let's move to the clinical case. A 25-year-old male is brought to the emergency department after being assaulted with a pipe wrench. On arrival, he has a 6-centimeter scalp laceration over the left parietal region. Through the laceration, you can see depressed bone fragments. His GCS is 14, broken down as E3 V5 M6. He has no focal neurological deficits. His pupils are equal and reactive. CT of the head with bone windows reveals a left parietal depressed skull fracture with depression measuring 12 millimeters, which is full thickness at this location. Intracranial air is present. There is a small underlying cortical contusion but no significant hematoma requiring evacuation. Bone fragments are visibly impinging on the cortical surface.

Take a moment and organize this case in your head. Identify the fracture type, list your indications for surgery, and formulate your operative plan. Then we will work through the examiner questions.

Moving into the interrogation phase. The examiner asks you: what is this fracture and does it require surgery? This is an open depressed skull fracture. It requires surgical management. You should be able to rattle off multiple indications without hesitation. First, it is open and compound, meaning it is contaminated through the scalp laceration. Second, the depression exceeds one table thickness at 12 millimeters. Third, pneumocephalus is present, which implies dural violation. Fourth, there is cosmetic deformity with bone visibly displaced into the intracranial compartment. Any single one of these indications is sufficient. This patient has four.

The examiner then asks: give me the complete list of surgical indications for depressed skull fractures. You operate if any of the following are present. One, open or compound fracture. Two, depression greater than the full thickness of the skull. Three, dural violation, evidenced by pneumocephalus or CSF leak. Four, significant underlying hematoma requiring evacuation. Five, frontal sinus involvement with posterior wall fracture. Six, cosmetic deformity, especially in the frontal region. Seven, neurological deficit attributable to the fracture, such as a motor deficit from cortical compression.

The examiner follows up: when do you not operate? Non-operative management is appropriate for a closed fracture with depression less than one table thickness, no evidence of dural violation, no cosmetic concern (particularly if the fracture is in a non-frontal location covered by hair), no underlying hematoma, and a neurologically intact patient. These patients are monitored with serial imaging.

Next question from the examiner: what is the timing of surgery? Open depressed skull fractures should be operated within 24 hours to reduce infection risk. Ideally, you are in the operating room within 8 to 12 hours. Delay beyond 24 hours significantly increases the risk of wound infection and osteomyelitis.

The examiner now wants your operative technique, step by step. You extend the existing scalp laceration to achieve adequate exposure of the entire fracture. You elevate the depressed bone fragments. You perform copious irrigation, using liters of saline, and meticulous debridement of all contaminated tissue. This means removing hair, foreign material, devitalized bone, and devitalized brain tissue. You inspect the dura carefully. If the dura is torn, and it will be in roughly 30 percent of cases, you repair it with primary suture if possible or with a dural substitute such as pericranium harvested from the operative field. You inspect the underlying brain for contusion or hematoma and evacuate any significant collections. Then you address the bone. If the bone fragments are clean and non-comminuted, you replace them and fix them with titanium plates and screws or with wire. If the fragments are contaminated, heavily comminuted, or have been driven into brain parenchyma, you remove them entirely and plan for a delayed cranioplasty at 3 to 6 months.

The examiner pivots to frontal sinus fractures: tell me about frontal sinus involvement. This is a common board sub-topic within depressed skull fractures. The key anatomic question is whether the posterior wall of the frontal sinus is intact or violated. A posterior wall fracture means intracranial communication. If the posterior wall is displaced or comminuted, you perform cranialization of the frontal sinus. This procedure has defined steps that you must know. You remove the posterior wall entirely. You strip all sinus mucosa completely, because any retained mucosa can form a mucocele years later, which is an expanding, potentially infected cystic mass within the cranium. You obliterate the nasofrontal outflow tract with muscle or fat to prevent ascending infection. You pack the sinus cavity with fat or bone. You repair the dura. If only the anterior wall is fractured and the posterior wall is intact, the management is either repair of the anterior wall for cosmesis or observation.

The examiner asks about antibiotics. For open depressed skull fractures, you give broad-spectrum prophylactic antibiotics. The standard regimen is cefazolin plus metronidazole, or alternatively ampicillin-sulbactam as monotherapy. Duration is 24 to 72 hours perioperatively. For heavily contaminated wounds, you extend the course. For closed depressed skull fractures, no prophylactic antibiotics are needed. Do not confuse this with the basilar skull fracture with CSF leak situation, where prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended per the Cochrane evidence. Open depressed fractures are a different entity and antibiotics are standard of care.

Now seizure prophylaxis. You initiate levetiracetam or phenytoin for 7 days, consistent with the Brain Trauma Foundation guideline. Open depressed skull fractures with dural violation carry a higher seizure risk than other traumatic brain injuries: 15 to 20 percent for early seizures and 25 to 35 percent for late post-traumatic seizures. The 7-day prophylaxis reduces early seizures but does not prevent late post-traumatic epilepsy. This is an important distinction. You are not preventing epilepsy. You are preventing early seizures that could cause secondary brain injury during the acute recovery period. Do not continue antiepileptic drugs beyond 7 days unless the patient has a clinical seizure.

Now we hit the trap questions. These are designed to see if you panic or if you have a systematic approach.

The examiner says: a depressed fracture is directly over the superior sagittal sinus. How does this change your approach? This is a high-stakes question. The bone fragment may be tamponading a tear in the sagittal sinus. If you blindly elevate that fragment, you can unleash catastrophic venous hemorrhage that is extremely difficult to control. Your answer must convey that you understand the danger. You exercise extreme caution. You position the patient and prepare for potential sinus repair before you touch the bone. You expose the sinus proximal and distal to the fracture site so you have control. You have hemostatic agents available, including surgical gelatin and oxidized cellulose. You have blood products typed, crossed, and in the room. If the patient is neurologically intact and there is no cosmetic concern, you strongly consider leaving the fragment in place rather than risking catastrophic hemorrhage from an operation that may not be necessary. If surgery is required because of neurological deficit or other compelling indication, you proceed with full preparation for sinus repair, with the understanding that you may need to repair the sinus with direct suture, muscle patch, or dural graft, and in extreme cases you may need to sacrifice the anterior third of the sinus if it cannot be repaired, though sacrifice of the posterior third carries a high risk of venous infarction.

Next trap: a child had a parietal skull fracture 6 months ago, and the parents now notice a growing soft lump at the fracture site. What is this? This is a growing skull fracture, also called a leptomeningeal cyst. It occurs in children under 3 years of age when a dural tear is present at the time of the original fracture. Brain pulsations prevent healing of both the dura and the skull. The fracture progressively widens, and brain herniates through the gap. The lump is soft, pulsatile, and enlarges over time. CT will show widening of the fracture with brain tissue within the gap. Treatment is surgical: dural repair to restore the intracranial barrier plus cranioplasty to reconstruct the skull defect.

Final trap: the open fracture was repaired, bone was replaced, and now 3 weeks later the patient returns with fever, wound swelling, and purulent drainage. What do you do? This is wound infection with osteomyelitis of the replaced bone fragments. You take the patient back to the operating room, remove all infected bone, obtain cultures, and initiate intravenous antibiotics for a minimum of 6 weeks guided by culture sensitivities. Local wound care follows. Cranioplasty to reconstruct the skull defect is deferred for a minimum of 3 to 6 months after confirmed infection clearance. This scenario illustrates why some surgeons prefer to discard bone fragments in heavily contaminated fractures rather than replanting them. The risk of osteomyelitis with contaminated replanted bone is significant, and the morbidity of a second operation for bone removal, followed by a third operation for cranioplasty, is substantial.

Complications you should be able to list on command: infection and osteomyelitis occurring in 2 to 5 percent of cases even with proper debridement, post-traumatic seizures both early and late, CSF leak from inadequate dural repair, wound dehiscence, cosmetic deformity from bone resorption or failure to reconstruct, and growing skull fracture in the pediatric population.

Now let's anchor the evidence. The Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines provide the framework for depressed skull fracture management, including the indications for operative versus non-operative treatment, the timing of surgery, and the 7-day seizure prophylaxis recommendation. The Cochrane review on antibiotics for basilar skull fractures is the definitive evidence that prophylactic antibiotics do not reduce meningitis risk in CSF leaks from basilar fractures. The Bullock surgical management guidelines for traumatic brain injury provide the surgical technique recommendations for fracture elevation and debridement. The Adelson pediatric TBI guidelines address growing skull fractures and the unique considerations in the pediatric population. The Chen frontal sinus fracture management algorithm provides the decision tree for anterior wall versus posterior wall fractures and the indications for cranialization versus observation. Know these references by name. The examiners may not ask you to cite specific papers, but demonstrating familiarity with the guideline literature conveys authority and confidence.

Let me close with the board pearls you need to carry into the examination room. Open fracture plus depression greater than one table thickness equals operate. Pneumocephalus on CT equals dural tear until proven otherwise. Frontal sinus posterior wall fracture equals cranialization with complete mucosal stripping. Growing skull fracture occurs in children under 3 years and requires dural repair plus cranioplasty. A fracture over the superior sagittal sinus means do not blindly elevate, because the fragment may be tamponading a sinus tear. Antibiotics for open depressed fractures: yes. Antibiotics for basilar fracture with CSF leak: no, per Cochrane. Seizure prophylaxis for 7 days reduces early seizures but does not prevent late epilepsy. Contaminated bone should be discarded with delayed cranioplasty at 3 to 6 months. If the patient with a closed depressed fracture is neurologically intact, has depression less than one table thickness, has no dural violation, and has no cosmetic concern, you observe. Know your indications cold and deliver them without hesitation. The examiners are testing whether you have a systematic, decisive approach to a common neurosurgical problem.

That covers depressed skull fracture management. You're ready for this one.
