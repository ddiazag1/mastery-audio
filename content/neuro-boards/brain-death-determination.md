questions:
  - q: What term do the current guidelines prefer, and why?
    a: Death by neurologic criteria, adopted in the 2023 joint AAN, American Academy of Pediatrics and Child Neurology Society consensus guidelines, rather than brain death. The reasoning is that the patient is dead, and the qualifier clarifies how death was determined rather than implying a special category of death.
    probe: Why does the wording matter when you talk to a family?
    probeA: Because the language shapes what the family believes has happened. Saying brain death invites the interpretation that there is some lesser category of death, or that the brain has died while the person has not, which is exactly the confusion that leads families to ask us to keep waiting. Saying that the patient has died, and that death was determined by neurological criteria, states the conclusion first and the method second. I would also avoid saying life support, and instead say the ventilator is moving air into his lungs, because life support implies there is a life being supported.
  - q: What must you establish before testing begins?
    a: An established, irreversible cause sufficient to explain the state; exclusion of confounders — sedatives, paralytics, severe metabolic and endocrine derangement, and hypothermia; and normal enough physiology to perform the examination, including adequate blood pressure. Testing a confounded patient is the commonest error.
    probe: How do you exclude a drug confounder specifically?
    probeA: By knowing exactly what was given, at what dose and when, and then either waiting five half-lives adjusted for organ function or measuring a level. I would check renal and hepatic function, because both prolong clearance, and remember that therapeutic hypothermia does too. For alcohol I would confirm a level below the legal driving limit. Where a drug effect cannot be excluded within a reasonable time, I use an ancillary test rather than guess, and I document the reasoning.
  - q: Your patient received a fentanyl infusion. How long do you wait?
    a: Either wait five half-lives of the agent in question, adjusted for organ dysfunction which prolongs clearance, or obtain serum levels demonstrating subtherapeutic concentrations. Renal or hepatic impairment substantially extends that window, and I would say so rather than counting hours mechanically.
    probe: What if he also received a neuromuscular blocker?
    probeA: Then I must confirm the blockade has gone before I can interpret any part of the motor examination, because a paralysed patient will have no motor response and no respiratory effort regardless of brain function. I would use a peripheral nerve stimulator and require a train-of-four with four twitches present. Rocuronium in particular is prolonged in renal and hepatic impairment. Until that is demonstrated, the examination is uninterpretable and the clock has not started.
  - q: He had targeted temperature management after cardiac arrest. What changes?
    a: You must wait until he is rewarmed to at least thirty-six degrees Celsius, and until sufficient time has passed for sedatives and paralytics used during the cooling protocol to clear. Hypothermia both confounds the examination and prolongs drug clearance, so the two effects compound.
    probe: What temperature threshold invalidates the examination?
    probeA: Below thirty-six degrees Celsius the examination cannot be relied on, and that is the threshold the current guidance uses: core temperature must be at or above thirty-six before testing. Hypothermia suppresses brainstem reflexes and respiratory drive directly, and it also slows drug clearance so the two confounders compound. I would rewarm and maintain normothermia for a period before testing, not simply check that the number has crossed the line at one moment.
  - q: Walk me through the apnoea test.
    a: Pre-oxygenate, confirm a normal starting carbon dioxide and adequate blood pressure, disconnect from the ventilator with oxygen delivered, and observe for respiratory effort while the carbon dioxide rises. A positive test is no respiratory effort with a carbon dioxide rise to sixty millimetres of mercury, or twenty above baseline in a chronic retainer.
    probe: He becomes hypotensive and desaturates halfway through. What do you do?
    probeA: Abort the test and reconnect the ventilator. Restoring the patient's physiology takes absolute priority over completing a protocol. Before I abandon it, I would draw an arterial blood gas at the moment of abortion, because if the carbon dioxide has already reached sixty or risen twenty above baseline with no respiratory effort observed, the test may still be interpretable. If it has not, the test is incomplete and I would move to an ancillary test rather than subject an unstable patient to a repeat apnoea trial. I would also review the preparation: adequate pre-oxygenation, a vasopressor running, and a normal starting carbon dioxide all reduce the chance of this happening.
  - q: When do you use an ancillary test?
    a: When the clinical examination or apnoea test cannot be completed or is confounded — severe facial trauma preventing cranial nerve testing, high cervical cord injury, severe chronic lung disease making apnoea testing unsafe, or unclearable drug effect. An ancillary test supplements the examination; it does not replace it as a shortcut.
    probe: Name two and their pitfalls.
    probeA: Cerebral angiography and nuclear medicine perfusion scanning. Catheter angiography is highly specific, showing absent intracranial filling at the level of the carotid bifurcation and foramen magnum, but it requires transport of an unstable patient, contrast load, and expertise in interpretation, and very high intracranial pressure from a decompressive craniectomy or an open fontanelle can give misleading flow. Radionuclide scanning showing the empty light bulb sign is less invasive and can be done portably, but it is less sensitive to posterior fossa flow. I would add that electroencephalography and transcranial Doppler are used in some jurisdictions but each has its own pitfalls: EEG is vulnerable to drug effect and electrical interference, and Doppler depends on an adequate bone window and on operator skill.
  - q: Which reflexes are you testing, and which findings do not exclude the diagnosis?
    a: Pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, oculovestibular, gag and cough, plus absence of motor response to central pain. Spinally mediated movements — the Lazarus sign, triple flexion, deep tendon reflexes — do not exclude the diagnosis, and families must be prepared for them because they are deeply distressing to witness.
    probe: How would you explain that to a family at the bedside?
    probeA: I would prepare them before it happens rather than explain it afterwards. I would say that the spinal cord can still produce movement even though the brain has died, in the same way that a reflex at the knee does not require thought, and that they may see the arm bend or the body move. I would say plainly that these movements do not mean he can feel, hear or respond, and that they are well described and expected. Then I would let them ask, and I would not rush it. Seeing this without warning is what destroys a family's trust in everything else we have told them.
  - q: The family refuses to accept the diagnosis. How do you proceed?
    a: With patience and clarity. Death by neurologic criteria is a determination of death, not a treatment decision, so it is not consented to. But I would ensure the determination is impeccable, involve senior colleagues and the ethics or palliative care team, allow reasonable accommodation of religious and cultural needs, and communicate repeatedly and consistently rather than confronting.
    probe: Does organ donation change how you have that conversation?
    probeA: It does not change the determination, and it must not. The determination of death is made independently and completely before donation is ever raised, by clinicians not involved in transplantation. What changes is the sequencing and who speaks: I would complete and communicate the determination, allow the family time, and then have the specialist nurse for organ donation introduce donation as a separate conversation. Collapsing the two is both an ethical error and, practically, the fastest way to make a family refuse.

Let's talk about brain death determination. This is one of the most procedurally rigorous things you will do as a neurosurgeon, and it is absolutely fair game on your oral boards. The examiners want to know that you understand every prerequisite, every reflex tested, every step of the apnea test, and every legal nuance. So let's go through this systematically.

First, some terminology. The American Academy of Neurology published its landmark practice parameters for determining brain death in adults in 2010. Those guidelines remain the clinical backbone of how we practice. However, in 2023, the AAN, the American Academy of Pediatrics, and the Child Neurology Society jointly published updated consensus guidelines that introduced the preferred term "death by neurologic criteria" rather than "brain death." The reasoning is straightforward. Brain death is legal death. Using the phrase "death by neurologic criteria" emphasizes that this is not a different kind of death or a lesser death. It is death, determined through neurologic assessment rather than cardiopulmonary cessation. For the boards, know both terms. Use them interchangeably, but demonstrate awareness of the 2023 update.

Now, prerequisites. Before you can even begin a brain death examination, you must satisfy several absolute prerequisites. If any one of these is not met, you cannot proceed. The examiners will test whether you know these cold.

First, there must be a known etiology sufficient to cause brain death. You need an established cause of devastating, irreversible brain injury. This could be massive traumatic brain injury, a large hemispheric or brainstem stroke, aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage with diffuse vasospasm, anoxic brain injury after cardiac arrest, or any other identifiable mechanism. If you do not have a clear etiology, you cannot declare brain death. You must pursue further workup. This prerequisite exists to prevent misdiagnosis in cases of unknown drug ingestion, locked-in syndrome, or other potentially reversible states.

Second, core body temperature must be greater than 36 degrees Celsius. Hypothermia depresses brainstem function and can mimic brain death. You must actively warm the patient to at least 36 degrees before testing. Some institutions require 36.5 degrees. Know your institutional policy, but for the boards, 36 degrees Celsius is the minimum threshold.

Third, systolic blood pressure must be at least 100 millimeters of mercury. Hypotension compromises cerebral perfusion and can suppress brainstem reflexes. You should use vasopressors if necessary to maintain adequate blood pressure before and during the examination. The most commonly used agents are norepinephrine and vasopressin, particularly vasopressin because brain death patients often develop diabetes insipidus from posterior pituitary failure, and vasopressin addresses both the hemodynamic and the endocrine problem simultaneously.

Fourth, you must exclude the effects of central nervous system depressant drugs. This is one of the most common pitfalls on the boards. You cannot declare brain death if the patient has received sedatives, narcotics, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, or any other CNS depressant that could confound the examination. The standard approach is to either wait five half-lives of the drug in question or obtain serum drug levels demonstrating subtherapeutic concentrations. For commonly tested drugs: midazolam has a half-life of 1.5 to 2.5 hours, so five half-lives is roughly 8 to 13 hours. Fentanyl has a half-life of 2 to 4 hours, so five half-lives is 10 to 20 hours. Propofol has an initial half-life of roughly 40 minutes but a context-sensitive half-life that can extend to hours after prolonged infusion, so be cautious. Pentobarbital, used in barbiturate coma for refractory intracranial hypertension, has a half-life of 15 to 50 hours, meaning five half-lives could be 75 to 250 hours. In pentobarbital cases, you will almost certainly need ancillary testing rather than waiting for drug clearance. The key point is that you must document that drug effects have been excluded, either by time elapsed or by drug levels.

Fifth, you must exclude severe metabolic derangements. Specifically, you need to rule out hepatic encephalopathy, uremic encephalopathy, severe hyponatremia or hypernatremia, hypoglycemia, and any other metabolic state that could suppress brainstem function. Correct these abnormalities first.

Sixth, you must confirm the absence of neuromuscular blockade. This is tested with a peripheral nerve stimulator using the train-of-four technique. You stimulate the ulnar nerve at the wrist with four supramaximal stimuli at 2 Hertz and observe for adductor pollicis contraction. All four twitches must be present, confirming that neuromuscular blockade has worn off. If the patient received paralytics and you cannot elicit four of four twitches, you cannot proceed with the motor examination.

Once all prerequisites are satisfied, you move to the clinical examination. The clinical exam for brain death has three components: brainstem reflex testing, motor response testing, and the apnea test.

Let's walk through brainstem reflex testing. You are testing for the complete absence of all brainstem-mediated reflexes. Every single one must be absent.

Start with the pupillary light reflex. Both pupils must be fixed and nonreactive to bright light. Use a focused, bright light source, not a dim penlight. Examine each eye individually. The pupils are typically mid-position or dilated, usually 4 to 9 millimeters. They do not need to be fully dilated, but they must be completely nonreactive. Mid-position fixed pupils are perfectly consistent with brain death because the sympathetic and parasympathetic inputs are both absent.

Next, the corneal reflex. Touch each cornea directly with a cotton wisp or a drop of saline. There should be no blink response, no grimace, and no eye movement in response to corneal stimulation. Test both eyes.

Then test the oculocephalic reflex, also known as the doll's eyes maneuver. Hold the eyelids open and briskly rotate the head from side to side and then flex and extend the neck. In an intact brainstem, the eyes would move conjugately in the opposite direction of head turning. In brain death, the eyes move with the head, like the painted eyes of a doll. There is no counter-rolling. Obviously, do not perform this test if there is a known or suspected cervical spine injury that has not been cleared.

The oculovestibular reflex, the cold caloric test, is one of the most powerful brainstem reflex tests and one the examiners love to ask about in detail. Here is the exact technique. First, inspect the ear canal with an otoscope to confirm the tympanic membrane is intact and the canal is free of cerumen or blood. If the membrane is perforated, the test may produce an unreliable result. Elevate the head of bed to 30 degrees. Using a large syringe and a small catheter or butterfly tubing, irrigate one ear canal with 50 milliliters of ice-cold water. Hold the eyelids open and observe for one full minute. In a patient with intact brainstem function, you would see tonic deviation of the eyes toward the irrigated ear. In brain death, there is absolutely no eye movement. Wait five minutes, then repeat on the opposite side. Both ears must show no response.

Test the gag reflex by stimulating the posterior pharynx with a tongue depressor or suction catheter. There should be no gag response.

Test the cough reflex by inserting a suction catheter deep into the trachea through the endotracheal tube, advancing it to the level of the carina. There should be no cough, no bucking, no respiratory effort in response to this stimulation.

Some protocols also include the jaw jerk reflex. Tap the chin with the mouth slightly open. There should be no reflex jaw closure. This is less universally included than the other reflexes but may appear on the boards.

Now, motor response testing. Apply a central painful stimulus and observe for any motor response. The key word here is central. You are looking for motor responses mediated by the brain, not the spinal cord. Apply nailbed pressure using a pen or similar hard object. Apply supraorbital pressure by pressing firmly on the supraorbital notch. Apply pressure to the temporomandibular joint by pressing anteriorly on the condyle. There should be no motor response whatsoever. No grimacing, no withdrawal, no posturing, no limb movement in response to these central stimuli.

Now here is a classic trap question, and I guarantee you will face some version of this on the oral boards. The patient has triple flexion withdrawal of the lower extremities. Is this compatible with brain death? The answer is yes. Spinal reflexes are compatible with brain death and do not preclude the diagnosis. Triple flexion, plantar flexion responses, finger jerks, undulating toe movements, and even the Lazarus sign, where the arms flex and rise toward the chest when the patient is sat up, are all spinal cord-mediated reflexes that can occur in a brain-dead patient. They originate from the spinal cord, not the brainstem or cerebrum. You must be absolutely clear on this distinction. These movements do not represent purposeful or brain-mediated activity. They are spinal automatisms. Document them, explain them to the family if they witness them, but do not let them derail the brain death determination.

Now the apnea test. This is the final and arguably most critical component of the clinical exam. Here is the step-by-step procedure.

Step one: preoxygenate the patient with 100 percent FiO2 for 10 minutes. The goal is to maximize the oxygen reserve so the patient can tolerate disconnection from the ventilator without rapid desaturation.

Step two: obtain a baseline arterial blood gas. Document the baseline PaCO2. Ideally, you want the baseline PaCO2 to be in the normal range, around 35 to 45 millimeters of mercury. If the patient is chronically hypercapnic, you will need to account for that.

Step three: disconnect the patient from the ventilator.

Step four: immediately place an oxygen cannula or catheter through the endotracheal tube and deliver oxygen at 6 liters per minute. This provides passive oxygenation through apneic diffusion, allowing oxygen to reach the alveoli even without ventilatory effort. This is critical. Without passive oxygenation, the patient will desaturate rapidly, and you will have to abort the test.

Step five: observe the patient closely for any respiratory effort for 8 to 10 minutes. Look at the chest and abdomen. Any respiratory movement, any chest rise, any abdominal excursion that represents a respiratory effort means the test is negative, the patient is not brain dead. You are watching for complete absence of any attempt to breathe despite rising carbon dioxide levels, which should be providing the most powerful respiratory drive possible.

Step six: after 8 to 10 minutes, obtain a repeat arterial blood gas.

Step seven: interpret the results. The apnea test is positive, meaning it supports brain death, if the PaCO2 is greater than or equal to 60 millimeters of mercury OR if the PaCO2 has risen by 20 or more millimeters of mercury above the baseline value, AND there was no respiratory effort during the observation period. Both conditions must be addressed: the carbon dioxide threshold and the absence of respiratory effort.

Here is another trap question. The apnea test shows a PaCO2 of 55 millimeters of mercury. Is the test positive? The answer depends on the baseline. If the baseline PaCO2 was 35 or lower, then the rise is 20 or more, and the test is positive even though the absolute value has not reached 60. But if the baseline was 40, then the rise is only 15, the absolute value is below 60, and the test is not yet positive. You would need to continue the test longer or repeat it. The key teaching point: the test is positive if PaCO2 reaches 60 or rises by 20 from baseline. These are "or" criteria, not "and" criteria. Either one is sufficient, but you must meet at least one of them.

When must you abort the apnea test? Two primary indications. First, hemodynamic instability, meaning sustained hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, or cardiovascular collapse. Second, oxygen desaturation below 85 percent despite passive oxygenation. If you must abort the test, reconnect the ventilator immediately. You can reattempt the apnea test later after stabilizing the patient, or you can proceed to ancillary testing as an alternative.

Ancillary testing. Ancillary tests are not required for brain death determination in adults in most jurisdictions if the full clinical examination, including the apnea test, can be completed. However, ancillary tests become necessary when components of the clinical exam cannot be performed, for instance facial trauma preventing corneal reflex testing, when the apnea test cannot be completed, when there is uncertainty about drug confounders, or when institutional or state policy mandates them.

The four main ancillary tests are as follows.

Electroencephalography, or EEG. You are looking for electrocerebral silence, defined as no electrical activity greater than 2 microvolts over a 30-minute recording using a minimum of 8 scalp electrodes with interelectrode distances of at least 10 centimeters. The recording must be performed at maximum sensitivity. The limitation of EEG is that it only measures cortical activity and can be confounded by drug effects, hypothermia, and electrical interference in the ICU.

Nuclear cerebral perfusion scan, also called a radionuclide cerebral blood flow study. Technetium-99m HMPAO or Technetium-99m ECD is injected intravenously, and planar or SPECT images are obtained. In brain death, there is no intracranial uptake. You see the classic "hollow skull" or "empty light bulb" sign, with perfusion to the scalp but complete absence of intracranial blood flow. This is one of the most definitive ancillary tests because it directly demonstrates the absence of cerebral perfusion and is not confounded by drugs.

CT angiography, or CTA. This is increasingly used. Four-point scoring systems assess opacification of the intracranial internal carotid arteries and the middle cerebral arteries bilaterally. Absent intracranial opacification of these vessels on CTA supports brain death. The advantage is that CT scanners are widely available and the test is fast.

Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography. You are looking for specific waveform patterns in the intracranial arteries. Reverberating flow, which shows equal forward and backward flow, or small systolic spikes with absent diastolic flow are consistent with brain death. The absence of any detectable signal can also support brain death, but only if the patient had a previously documented acoustic window. If you have never been able to insonate the vessels, the absence of signal is not interpretable. Transcranial Doppler is noninvasive and can be performed at the bedside, but it is operator-dependent and requires adequate acoustic windows.

Number of examinations. This varies by state law and institutional policy. Many states require two separate clinical examinations performed by two different attending physicians. In some states, only one examination is required. At least one of the examining physicians is typically required to be a neurologist or neurosurgeon. Know the requirements of the state where you practice, but for the boards, be prepared to discuss the two-examination standard.

Observation period between examinations. For adults, the recommended observation period is typically 6 hours, though some institutions and states require up to 24 hours. The observation period exists to ensure that the findings are irreversible.

Pediatric considerations are important and commonly tested. Children require longer observation periods because of the developing brain's greater resistance to injury and potential for recovery. For neonates, defined here as 37 weeks gestational age to 30 days of life, the recommended observation period between examinations is 24 hours. Two examinations and two apnea tests are required. For infants 30 days to 18 years, the recommended observation period is 12 hours. Again, two examinations and two apnea tests are required. Some guidelines extend the neonatal observation period to 48 hours for term neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. The 2023 guidelines provide more detailed age-stratified recommendations, so be aware of these longer intervals in the pediatric population.

Documentation requirements are extensive and must be meticulous. You need to document the established etiology of the brain injury and its irreversibility. Document that all prerequisites were met, including the specific core temperature, blood pressure, drug clearance information or drug levels, metabolic parameters, and train-of-four results. Document each brainstem reflex tested and confirm it was absent. Document the motor examination findings. Document the apnea test in full detail: the preoxygenation period, the baseline ABG values, the duration of observation, the repeat ABG values, whether any respiratory effort was observed, and whether the test met criteria. Document the exact time of death. In brain death, the legal time of death is the time the last confirmatory test or examination is completed, not the time the ventilator is eventually discontinued. This is a commonly tested nuance. Document the names and credentials of all examining physicians.

Organ donation pathway. Once brain death is declared, you have a legal obligation in most jurisdictions to notify the local organ procurement organization, the OPO. In the United States, this is mandated by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. The OPO, not the treating physician, should approach the family about organ donation. This separation is critical to avoid any appearance of conflict of interest. The treating team should never be the ones requesting organ donation. Your role as the neurosurgeon is to declare death, provide compassionate communication to the family, and notify the OPO. Maintain the patient on ventilatory and hemodynamic support until the OPO has completed its evaluation and the family has made a decision about donation.

Now let's address common pitfalls and legal considerations.

Here is the third trap question. The family disagrees with the brain death determination. They do not accept that their loved one is dead. What are your legal obligations? This is legally and ethically nuanced and varies significantly by state. In most states, brain death equals legal death, period. The family's disagreement does not change the legal reality. The physician is under no legal obligation to continue mechanical ventilation on a legally dead person. However, practice sensitivity. In New Jersey, the state law includes a religious exemption. If the family objects to brain death determination on religious grounds, the physician must continue to maintain cardiopulmonary support. New York does not have a formal statutory religious exemption but has a regulation requiring hospitals to make reasonable accommodation for religious or moral objections. A small number of other states have similar provisions. For the boards, state clearly that brain death is legal death in all 50 states under the Uniform Determination of Death Act, but acknowledge that New Jersey and New York have specific religious or moral exemption provisions. In clinical practice, even in states without formal exemptions, most institutions will provide a brief period of support to allow families to come to terms with the diagnosis, arrange for family to visit, and address organ donation.

Other pitfalls to be aware of. Do not confuse brain death with persistent vegetative state or minimally conscious state. A patient in a vegetative state has brainstem function. They have sleep-wake cycles, they breathe spontaneously, they have intact brainstem reflexes. They are not brain dead. Do not confuse brain death with locked-in syndrome. A locked-in patient is fully conscious but quadriplegic and anarthric, usually from a ventral pontine lesion. They can typically communicate through vertical eye movements or blinking. The clinical exam prerequisites, particularly the requirement for a known etiology and the comprehensive testing of brainstem reflexes, should prevent these misdiagnoses, but the examiners may present a scenario designed to test whether you can distinguish these entities.

Be aware of the confounding effects of therapeutic hypothermia. If a patient underwent targeted temperature management after cardiac arrest, you must wait until the patient has been rewarmed to at least 36 degrees Celsius and sufficient time has passed for any sedative or paralytic drugs used during the cooling protocol to clear before performing brain death testing. This often means waiting 24 to 72 hours after rewarming.

Board pearls to carry with you. One, prerequisites are non-negotiable. Temperature above 36, systolic blood pressure above 100, no drug effects, no metabolic derangement, no neuromuscular blockade. If any prerequisite is not met, stop. You cannot proceed. Two, spinal reflexes are compatible with brain death. Triple flexion, the Lazarus sign, plantar responses. These are spinal automatisms and do not preclude the diagnosis. Three, the apnea test is positive if PaCO2 reaches 60 or rises by 20 from baseline, with no respiratory effort. Either threshold is sufficient. Four, time of death is the time the last confirmatory test is completed, not the time the ventilator is turned off. Five, the treating physician never approaches the family for organ donation. That is the OPO's role. Six, New Jersey has a religious exemption. New York has a reasonable accommodation regulation. Know these for the boards. Seven, ancillary tests are needed when the clinical exam or apnea test cannot be completed. The nuclear perfusion scan is the gold standard ancillary test because it is not confounded by drugs. Eight, pediatric patients require two examinations, two apnea tests, and longer observation periods. Neonates need 24 hours. Infants and children need 12 hours. Nine, the 2023 guidelines prefer the term "death by neurologic criteria." Use it.

That covers brain death determination. You're ready for this one.
