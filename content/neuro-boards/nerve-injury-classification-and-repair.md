questions:
  - q: Map Seddon onto Sunderland and tell me where the surgical line falls.
    a: Neurapraxia is Sunderland one, axonotmesis is two, neurotmesis covers four and five, with three sitting between. The line is between three and four: degrees one to three recover spontaneously to varying extent, four and five will not and need surgery. Mackinnon added a sixth, a mixed-injury pattern, which is what you actually see.
    probe: You cannot tell which degree at the bedside. So what governs your algorithm?
    probeA: Time and mechanism, because those are the two things I do know. A sharp transection I explore now. A closed injury in continuity I cannot grade clinically, so I observe with serial examination and an advancing Tinel sign, obtain a baseline study at three to four weeks, and set a deadline: if there is no clinical or electrical recovery by three to six months, I explore and let the intraoperative nerve action potential grade it for me. The algorithm exists precisely because the bedside cannot distinguish a second degree injury that will recover from a fourth degree injury that will not.
  - q: Why can you not electrodiagnose a nerve injury on day two?
    a: Wallerian degeneration has not happened. The distal segment still conducts, so motor responses persist to roughly seven to ten days and sensory to ten or eleven. Needle denervation, fibrillations and positive sharp waves, appears at two to three weeks proximally and three to six distally. Baseline study at three to four weeks.
    probe: What can a day-two study still tell you?
    probeA: Quite a lot, provided you know what you are asking. It establishes a baseline for comparison. Stimulating distal to the lesion still produces a response, so if stimulating distally gives nothing at all on day two, the nerve was already degenerated and this is not an acute injury. It can localise a conduction block across the lesion if you can stimulate above and below. And it can identify a pre-existing neuropathy that changes the interpretation of everything later. What it cannot do is tell you the degree of injury, because Wallerian degeneration has not yet occurred.
  - q: Give me the two numbers that govern every peripheral nerve decision.
    a: Regeneration at roughly one millimetre per day, or an inch a month; and the motor endplate deadline at roughly twelve to eighteen months, beyond which denervated muscle will not accept reinnervation usefully. Everything else follows from the arithmetic between them.
    probe: Injury at the elbow, target twenty five centimetres distal. Do the sum out loud.
    probeA: Twenty-five centimetres is two hundred and fifty millimetres, so at a millimetre a day that is two hundred and fifty days, roughly eight months, before regenerating axons reach the target. Add a month or so for the initial delay at the repair site. If I repair at three months, the axons arrive at about eleven to twelve months, which is just inside the twelve to eighteen month endplate window. If I wait six months to operate, they arrive at fourteen to fifteen months and the result is marginal. That arithmetic is why the deadline for exploration is three to six months rather than a year.
  - q: Sharp laceration, ragged avulsion, and a closed traction injury. Three different timings. Give them.
    a: Sharp clean transection: primary repair acutely, ideally within seventy two hours. Blunt or ragged: tag the ends and repair at about three weeks once the zone of injury demarcates. Closed traction injury in continuity: observe with serial examination, baseline electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks, explore at three to six months if no recovery.
    probe: Why wait three weeks for the ragged one?
    probeA: Because the zone of injury is longer than it looks on the day. In a blunt or avulsion injury the nerve is damaged well beyond the visibly disrupted segment, and at the time of injury I cannot tell where healthy fascicles begin. Waiting about three weeks lets the injury demarcate, so that when I resect back I am confident I am reaching normal pouting fascicles rather than repairing damaged nerve to damaged nerve, which is the commonest reason a technically perfect repair fails. Tagging the ends at the first operation stops them retracting and scars less than hunting for them later.
  - q: You have explored. What single test makes the decision?
    a: A nerve action potential recorded across the lesion. Present means meaningful regeneration is occurring, the injury is functionally second or third degree, and I perform neurolysis only. Absent means fourth or fifth degree, so I resect back to healthy pouting fascicles and reconstruct.
    probe: What does that test prevent you from doing wrongly in each direction?
    probeA: In one direction it stops me resecting and grafting a nerve that was going to recover on its own. A present nerve action potential means regeneration is already crossing the lesion, and if I resect that I convert a recovering second or third degree injury into a graft with two coaptations and a far worse ceiling. In the other direction it stops me performing a neurolysis on a nerve that will never recover, leaving the patient to wait out months that consume the endplate window before anyone realises. It converts a guess into a decision made on the table.
  - q: Talk me through repair technique and the one thing that ruins it.
    a: Tension ruins it, by compromising the microcirculation. Repair epineurially with eight zero to ten zero monofilament, aligning by epineurial vessels and fascicular pattern, using as few sutures as will hold, under magnification, in a well vascularised bed. Group fascicular repair where motor and sensory groups separate, as in the ulnar nerve at the wrist.
    probe: The gap will only close with the elbow flexed. What do you do?
    probeA: I do not accept a repair that only holds in flexion, because the patient will extend the elbow and the coaptation will fail or scar under tension. My options in order: mobilise the nerve proximally and distally to gain length, though that costs its segmental blood supply if overdone; transpose the ulnar nerve anteriorly at the elbow, which genuinely shortens the required distance; and if the gap still will not close without tension, accept it and graft, usually with sural nerve, or use an acellular allograft for a shorter gap. A tension-free graft outperforms a tight direct repair, which is counterintuitive but well established.
  - q: Name the nerve transfers you would actually use.
    a: Oberlin: an ulnar fascicle to the biceps branch for elbow flexion, often doubled with a median fascicle to brachialis. Spinal accessory to suprascapular for shoulder abduction and external rotation. Triceps branch of radial to axillary. And anterior interosseous terminal branch to the deep ulnar motor branch for intrinsics in a high ulnar injury.
    probe: What is the principle that unites all of them?
    probeA: Take an expendable donor fascicle with a redundant function, close to the target, and reroute it to a denervated recipient near its motor endplate, so the regenerating axons have a short distance to travel and arrive inside the endplate window. That is the whole idea: you convert a proximal injury with an impossible regeneration distance into a distal repair with a short one, and you accept a small, well-compensated donor deficit in exchange for restoring a critical function.
  - q: When do you decide not to operate on the nerve at all?
    a: Presentation beyond the endplate window with established fibrosis; progressive systemic neuropathy; an unreconstructable avulsion with no donors; or a patient better served by tendon transfer, free functioning muscle transfer or arthrodesis. Recognising futility and pivoting is a judgement, not a concession.
    probe: What do you offer that patient instead?
    probeA: Reconstruction that does not depend on the nerve. Tendon transfers, which are reliable and immediate, so for a high radial palsy a pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis, flexor carpi radialis or ulnaris to the finger extensors, and palmaris longus to the thumb extensor. Free functioning muscle transfer, typically gracilis, for elbow flexion in a late-presenting plexus injury. Arthrodesis or tenodesis for a flail joint. Orthotics to position the limb usefully. And realistic goals discussed honestly, with occupational therapy and vocational assessment. Recognising that the nerve operation has passed is not giving up, it is choosing the operation that will work.

    stem: A 26-year-old chef lacerates his volar forearm on broken glass. In the emergency department he cannot flex the thumb interphalangeal joint or the index distal interphalangeal joint, and has numbness over the radial three and a half digits sparing the thenar eminence. The wound is clean.
    q1: Localise the lesion precisely and say why thenar sensation matters.
    a1: Median nerve injury in the forearm. Loss of flexor pollicis longus and the index profundus indicates anterior interosseous involvement. Sparing of thenar eminence sensation tells me nothing here about level, because the palmar cutaneous branch arises five to seven centimetres proximal to the wrist, so a forearm lesion distal to that branch spares it; the sensory pattern fits a lesion distal to the branch origin.
    q2: What do you do, and when?
    a2: This is a sharp clean transection: explore and repair primarily, ideally within seventy two hours and certainly within the first week, before the ends retract and scar. Delay makes fascicular matching harder and may convert a direct repair into a graft.
    q3: At exploration there is a two centimetre gap after trimming back to healthy fascicles. Now what?
    a3: Do not close it under tension. A two centimetre gap in a mixed motor nerve is a graft: sural nerve interposition, reversed so branching does not divert regenerating axons. Acellular allograft is a reasonable alternative at this length, but for a motor nerve I would favour autograft.
    q4: He asks when his hand will work. What do you tell him?
    a4: Regeneration at about a millimetre a day across two coaptations, so months rather than weeks, and I would follow an advancing Tinel sign. I would grade recovery with the Medical Research Council scale and set the realistic target at protective sensation and useful grip rather than normality, and start hand therapy immediately for range of motion, splinting and later sensory re-education.

Let's master peripheral nerve injury classification and repair for the oral boards.

Peripheral nerve is the domain candidates neglect most and examiners probe most predictably, precisely because they know it is neglected. The general session will contain a nerve case. The reason the topic is tractable is that it is governed by a small number of principles about structure, degeneration, and regeneration, and once you own those principles the management decisions follow almost automatically. Let's build from the anatomy upward.

Start with the microanatomy, because every classification system is built on it. The axon is the conducting element. Surrounding each axon is the endoneurium. Axons are gathered into fascicles, each wrapped in perineurium, which is the strongest layer and the one that provides the blood nerve barrier and most of the tensile strength. Fascicles are gathered into the whole nerve, wrapped in epineurium, which is divided into the interfascicular or internal epineurium between fascicles and the external epineurium surrounding the trunk. Remember perineurium as the critical layer: it determines whether the architecture that guides regeneration survives.

Now the two classifications, which you must be able to map onto each other.

Seddon described three categories. Neurapraxia is a conduction block without axonal disruption, typically from focal demyelination or ischemia. The axon is intact, so there is no Wallerian degeneration, and recovery is complete, usually within days to weeks up to about three months. Axonotmesis is axonal disruption with preservation of the connective tissue scaffold. Wallerian degeneration occurs distally, but because the endoneurial tubes remain intact, regenerating axons are guided back to their original targets and recovery can be good, though it takes time. Neurotmesis is complete disruption of the nerve including the connective tissue. Spontaneous meaningful recovery does not occur and surgical repair is required.

Sunderland refined this into five degrees. First degree corresponds to neurapraxia. Second degree is axonal disruption with intact endoneurium, corresponding to axonotmesis, with good recovery. Third degree adds endoneurial disruption with intact perineurium, so regenerating axons may be misdirected within the fascicle and recovery is incomplete and variable. Fourth degree adds perineurial disruption with only the epineurium in continuity, producing a neuroma in continuity with essentially no useful spontaneous recovery, requiring resection and grafting. Fifth degree is complete transection. Mackinnon added a sixth degree describing a mixed injury in which different fascicles within the same nerve sustain different degrees of injury, which is the common real world pattern and explains why a nerve can partially recover while remaining functionally useless.

The critical examination point is the divide between second and third degree on one side and fourth and fifth on the other. Degrees one through three will recover spontaneously to varying extent and should generally be observed. Degrees four and five will not and require surgery. Since you usually cannot tell which you are dealing with at the bedside, the entire clinical algorithm is built around serial examination and electrodiagnostics over time.

Now Wallerian degeneration and the biology of regeneration, because the timing questions depend on it. After axonal disruption, the distal segment degenerates over roughly the first several days, with axonal and myelin breakdown cleared by Schwann cells and macrophages. This matters for electrodiagnosis: nerve conduction studies performed too early will still show distal responses because the distal segment has not yet degenerated, so a study performed in the first week cannot distinguish neurapraxia from axonotmesis. Distal motor responses typically disappear by roughly seven to ten days and sensory responses by roughly ten to eleven days. Electromyographic denervation changes, meaning fibrillations and positive sharp waves, do not appear until roughly two to three weeks in proximal muscles and three to six weeks in distal muscles. Therefore the standard teaching is to obtain a baseline electrodiagnostic study at roughly three to four weeks after injury, and that is a number the examiner will want.

Regeneration proceeds at roughly one millimeter per day, or about an inch per month. You should use this to calculate expected reinnervation times out loud. If a nerve is injured at the elbow and the target muscle is twenty five centimeters distal, you expect roughly two hundred fifty days, or about eight months, before reinnervation could occur. This arithmetic drives the decision about when to stop waiting.

And that brings us to the single most important concept in peripheral nerve surgery: the motor endplate clock. Denervated muscle undergoes progressive, irreversible atrophy and fibrosis, and the motor endplates degrade. Beyond roughly twelve to eighteen months of denervation, reinnervation will not restore useful motor function no matter how well the nerve regenerates. This is why peripheral nerve surgery is time critical in a way that most elective neurosurgery is not. Sensory recovery is more forgiving and can occur after longer intervals, but motor recovery is not. Say the words the motor endplate will not accept reinnervation after roughly a year to eighteen months, and therefore I would not wait beyond a window that allows regeneration to reach the target within that period.

Now the clinical algorithm, which follows directly.

For a sharp, clean transection, such as a knife or glass laceration, perform primary repair acutely, ideally within roughly seventy two hours and certainly within the first week. The wound is clean, the nerve ends are identifiable, and the fascicular anatomy can be matched before retraction and scarring occur.

For a blunt or ragged transection, such as a chainsaw or avulsive injury, tag the nerve ends at the initial exploration and perform delayed repair at roughly three weeks. Waiting allows the zone of injury to demarcate, so that you resect back to healthy fascicles rather than repairing into damaged tissue.

For a closed injury in continuity, such as a gunshot wound without transection or a traction injury, observe with serial clinical examination and obtain a baseline electrodiagnostic study at three to four weeks, repeating at roughly three months. If there is clinical or electrical evidence of recovery, continue to observe. Look for an advancing Tinel sign, which is percussion tenderness that migrates distally over time and indicates regenerating axons progressing along the nerve. A Tinel sign that advances is reassuring; a Tinel sign that remains fixed at the injury site is ominous. If there is no evidence of recovery by roughly three to six months, explore the nerve.

At exploration, the key intraoperative decision tool is the nerve action potential recorded across the lesion. Expose the nerve proximal and distal to the injury, stimulate proximally and record distally across the segment. If a nerve action potential is present across the lesion, meaningful regeneration is occurring, the injury is functionally second or third degree, and you should perform neurolysis only, releasing the scar and leaving the nerve in continuity. If no nerve action potential is recorded across the lesion, the injury is fourth or fifth degree and you must resect the damaged segment back to healthy fascicles and reconstruct. This single test prevents both the error of resecting a recovering nerve and the error of leaving a hopeless neuroma in continuity.

Now the reconstructive options, in ascending order of complexity.

Direct end to end repair is preferred when it can be done without tension. Tension is the enemy: it compromises the microcirculation and reliably produces failure. The rule is that if the gap can be bridged with the joint in a neutral or near neutral position and the repair holds with fine suture without blanching, repair directly. Repair epineurially with fine monofilament suture, typically eight zero to ten zero nylon, aligning the fascicular pattern using the epineurial vessels and the fascicular topography as landmarks. Group fascicular repair is used when the fascicular groups have distinct functions, as in the ulnar nerve at the wrist where motor and sensory groups are separable.

When a gap exists, use an interposition graft. The workhorse donor is the sural nerve, which yields roughly thirty to forty centimeters, is easily harvested, and costs the patient a patch of lateral foot numbness that is generally well tolerated. Other donors include the medial and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerves. Because a graft requires the axons to cross two suture lines, outcomes are inferior to direct repair, and grafts longer than roughly six to eight centimeters have progressively poorer results. Reverse the graft so that branching does not divert regenerating axons.

For short gaps in small sensory nerves, typically under roughly three centimeters, a conduit, either a vein graft or a synthetic bioabsorbable tube, is an acceptable alternative that avoids donor morbidity. Conduits do not perform well for motor nerves or long gaps. Processed acellular nerve allograft is an increasingly used option in the intermediate range.

Nerve transfer, also called neurotization, is the technique that has most changed the field. The principle is to take a redundant or expendable donor fascicle with a strong motor supply and coapt it directly to the denervated distal target near the muscle, converting a long proximal injury into a short distal repair. This dramatically shortens the regeneration distance and therefore beats the endplate clock. The canonical examples are the Oberlin transfer, in which a fascicle of the ulnar nerve supplying flexor carpi ulnaris is transferred to the biceps branch of the musculocutaneous nerve to restore elbow flexion; the spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve transfer for shoulder abduction and external rotation; and the triceps branch of the radial nerve to the axillary nerve. Being able to name Oberlin and the spinal accessory to suprascapular transfer is close to mandatory for this topic.

For the patient who presents late, beyond the endplate window, nerve reconstruction is futile and you should say so and pivot. The options become tendon transfer, free functioning muscle transfer, arthrodesis, and targeted management of neuropathic pain. Recognizing when not to operate on the nerve is itself a scored judgment.

Finally, principles that apply throughout. Handle the nerve atraumatically and preserve its longitudinal blood supply. Repair in a healthy, well vascularized bed, and if the bed is scarred consider a vascularized flap. Perform the repair without tension, under magnification, with meticulous fascicular alignment. Manage neuropathic pain proactively with agents such as gabapentinoids and tricyclics. Involve hand therapy early for range of motion, splinting to prevent contracture, and sensory reeducation, because a perfectly regenerated nerve into a stiff contracted hand yields nothing.

Understand the blood supply, because it governs how much you may mobilise a nerve. Peripheral nerves receive segmental regional vessels that join a longitudinal intraneural plexus running in the epineurium and perineurium. That longitudinal network tolerates a surprising amount of mobilisation, which is what permits transposition and short gap closure, but circumferential stripping of the epineurium devascularises the segment and guarantees a poor result. So handle the nerve by its epineurium with fine instruments, never crush it with forceps, and preserve the surrounding vascular bed wherever you can.

Have the intraoperative sequence ready in order. Position and prepare so that both a proximal and a distal exposure are possible and so that a graft donor site, usually the leg for sural nerve, is in the field. Expose normal nerve proximal and distal to the injury and work toward the lesion from healthy tissue, which is the same principle you use for a tethered cord. Avoid long acting neuromuscular blockade, because you need to stimulate. Use magnification, ideally an operating microscope. Record a nerve action potential across the lesion to make the neurolysis versus resection decision. If resecting, cut back serially with a sharp blade until you see healthy pouting fascicles on both faces rather than scar, and accept that this will lengthen the gap. Then coapt without tension.

Know the repair techniques and their indications. Epineurial repair aligns the outer sheath using the epineurial vessels and the fascicular pattern as landmarks and is the workhorse for most mixed nerves, performed with eight zero to ten zero monofilament nylon and as few sutures as will hold. Group fascicular repair separates functionally distinct fascicular groups and is used where the internal topography is known and separable, classically the ulnar nerve at the wrist where motor and sensory groups diverge, and the median nerve distally. Fibrin glue can supplement or in small nerves replace suture and reduces handling trauma. The governing principle is unchanged: tension is the enemy, and a repair that requires joint flexion to hold will fail when the joint is mobilised.

Know the graft and transfer options in more depth. Sural nerve is the workhorse donor, harvested posterior to the lateral malleolus, yielding roughly thirty to forty centimetres, at the cost of an area of lateral foot numbness and a small risk of painful neuroma. Medial and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerves, the superficial radial and the anterior interosseous nerve terminal branch are alternatives. Because a graft forces axons across two coaptations, results are inferior to direct repair, and grafts beyond roughly six to eight centimetres perform progressively worse. Reverse the graft so that its branch points do not divert regenerating axons away from the target. Processed acellular allograft avoids donor morbidity and performs acceptably for short and intermediate sensory gaps, less predictably for long motor gaps. Conduits, whether vein or synthetic bioabsorbable, are reasonable for small sensory nerves across gaps under roughly three centimetres and poor for motor nerves and long gaps. Vascularised nerve grafts are reserved for long gaps in scarred, poorly vascularised beds.

Expand the transfer repertoire beyond the two you must name. For elbow flexion, the Oberlin transfer takes a redundant ulnar fascicle to the biceps branch, and the double fascicular transfer adds a median fascicle to the brachialis branch. For shoulder abduction and external rotation, spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve, often combined with a radial triceps branch to axillary nerve transfer. For wrist and finger extension, median nerve branches to flexor digitorum superficialis and flexor carpi radialis can be transferred to the posterior interosseous and extensor carpi radialis brevis branches. For intrinsic hand function, the terminal anterior interosseous nerve branch to pronator quadratus can be transferred to the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve, which is the standard answer for a high ulnar injury where the regeneration distance would otherwise defeat the endplate clock. State the general principle as well: use an expendable donor with abundant motor axons, coapt close to the target, and match motor to motor.

Grade the outcome with a recognised scale rather than adjectives. The Medical Research Council motor scale runs from M zero, no contraction, through M three, movement against gravity, to M five, normal power; useful functional recovery is conventionally M three or better for proximal muscles and M four for hand function. The sensory scale runs S zero to S four, with protective sensation at S three being the realistic and important goal in an insensate hand. Counsel families accordingly: sensation for protection, elbow flexion for function, and intrinsic recovery rarely.

Address pain and rehabilitation explicitly, because they are commonly forgotten and frequently probed. Neuropathic pain is managed with gabapentinoids, tricyclics, serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors and, where appropriate, desensitisation therapy; a painful neuroma may need excision and transposition of the stump into muscle or bone, or a modern technique such as targeted muscle reinnervation or regenerative peripheral nerve interface. Hand therapy must start immediately and continue throughout: passive and active range of motion to prevent contracture, static and dynamic splinting to hold the limb in a functional position while awaiting reinnervation, oedema control, scar management, and later motor re-education and sensory re-education. A perfectly regenerated nerve entering a stiff contracted hand achieves nothing, and saying that sentence demonstrates you understand the whole problem rather than only the operation.

Finally, know the situations where you should not operate on the nerve at all. Presentation beyond the endplate window with established muscle fibrosis, a patient with progressive systemic neuropathy, an unreconstructable avulsion with no available donors, and a patient whose functional goals are better served by tendon transfer, free functioning muscle transfer or arthrodesis. Recognising futility and pivoting to reconstruction is a scored judgement, not a concession.

To summarize. Know Seddon and Sunderland and know that degrees one through three recover and four and five do not. Nerves regenerate at one millimeter per day. Electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks. Explore at three to six months if no recovery. Use the intraoperative nerve action potential to decide neurolysis versus resection and reconstruction. Repair without tension, graft with sural nerve when a gap exists, and reach for nerve transfers to beat the twelve to eighteen month motor endplate deadline.
