questions:
  - q: What is the NIHSS and what is it for?
    a: A 42-point scale quantifying stroke severity across level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor strength in all four limbs, limb ataxia, sensation, language, dysarthria and extinction. It stratifies severity, guides eligibility, and provides a reproducible serial measure.
    probe: What NIHSS threshold appears in the thrombectomy trials?
    probeA: Most of the 2015 trials required an NIHSS of six or more, and DAWN used NIHSS as part of its clinical-core mismatch strata, requiring ten or more, with twenty or more permitting a larger core. But I would treat the threshold as a trial entry criterion rather than a rule for the patient in front of me: a low NIHSS with a proximal occlusion and a disabling deficit, such as isolated aphasia, can still justify thrombectomy.
  - q: Explain ASPECTS and the number that matters.
    a: A 10-point score applied to non-contrast CT, subtracting a point for each middle cerebral artery region showing early ischaemic change, so ten is normal. A score below six indicates a large established core and is a relative contraindication to thrombectomy, although SELECT2 in 2023 has begun to challenge that.
    probe: Which trial required an ASPECTS of six or greater plus good collaterals?
    probeA: ESCAPE. It required an ASPECTS of six or greater on non-contrast CT together with moderate to good collateral filling on multiphase CT angiography, which is what made it distinctive, because it selected on collateral status rather than on perfusion mismatch and achieved very fast workflow times.
  - q: Give me the thrombolysis windows and where they came from.
    a: NINDS in 1995 established benefit within three hours. ECASS III in 2008 extended to four and a half hours, with additional exclusions: age over eighty, NIHSS over twenty-five, combined history of diabetes and prior stroke, and oral anticoagulant use regardless of INR.
    probe: Does thrombolysis change whether you proceed to thrombectomy?
    probeA: No. If the patient is eligible for thrombolysis, they should receive it and then proceed to thrombectomy without waiting to assess the response. That is the bridging approach the 2015 trials used, and delaying transfer to see whether lysis works costs penumbra. The non-inferiority trials of direct thrombectomy versus bridging have been mixed and have not displaced bridging as standard where the patient is eligible.
  - q: Name the five 2015 trials and what they collectively established.
    a: MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME and REVASCAT. Together they established thrombectomy within six hours as standard of care for anterior circulation large vessel occlusion. MR CLEAN showed an absolute difference of thirteen and a half percentage points in functional independence at ninety days.
    probe: What does functional independence mean in those trials?
    probeA: A modified Rankin Scale score of zero to two at ninety days, meaning the patient is independent in their own activities of daily living even if they cannot resume all previous activity. It is the standard primary outcome across the thrombectomy literature, and quoting it correctly matters because a thirteen point absolute difference in independence is a far stronger claim than a difference in recanalisation rates.
  - q: How did the window get to twenty-four hours?
    a: Two trials with different selection logic. DAWN, 2018, extended to twenty-four hours using clinical-core mismatch, with core volume thresholds of twenty-one, thirty-one or fifty-one millilitres depending on the stratum. DEFUSE 3 extended to sixteen hours using perfusion mismatch with a ratio of one point eight or greater.
    probe: So is selection now chronological or physiological?
    probeA: Physiological, beyond six hours. Inside six hours the decision is largely chronological and anatomical. Beyond it, tissue clocks diverge from wall clocks because collateral status differs enormously between patients, so selection depends on demonstrating salvageable tissue by perfusion or clinical-core mismatch. The slow progressor with good collaterals at twenty hours is a better candidate than the fast progressor with poor collaterals at five.
  - q: Aspiration or stent retriever first?
    a: Either. ASTER in 2017 randomised contact aspiration versus stent retriever as first-line and COMPASS in 2019 showed non-inferiority of direct aspiration. So the choice is operator preference and clot characteristics rather than evidence favouring one.
    probe: What do you do when the first pass fails?
    probeA: Change something rather than repeat the same manoeuvre. Switch technique, move from aspiration to a stent retriever or combine the two, reconsider the guide catheter position and support, and reassess whether there is underlying intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis rather than embolus, which may need angioplasty or a glycoprotein inhibitor. I would also keep the total number of passes in mind, because first-pass effect predicts outcome and successive passes carry falling yield and rising haemorrhage risk.
  - q: Basilar occlusion. What does the evidence actually say?
    a: Mixed. BASICS in 2021 compared thrombectomy plus best medical therapy with medical therapy alone within six hours and did not show clear benefit. ATTENTION in 2022 enrolled within twelve hours and demonstrated clear benefit. Given the natural history is devastating with very high mortality on medical therapy alone, most major centres offer thrombectomy within twelve hours.
    probe: Why is perfusion imaging less useful in the posterior circulation?
    probeA: Because the posterior fossa is a small volume close to bone, so beam hardening and partial volume artefact degrade the maps, and because the standard perfusion thresholds were derived and validated in anterior circulation strokes. The brainstem also has a very low tolerance for small infarct volumes, so a core measured in millilitres that would be trivial in the hemisphere can be catastrophic here. Practically, I rely more on the clinical picture, the time course and the imaging of the vessel than on perfusion numbers.
  - q: How distal will you go?
    a: Proximal M2 occlusions with significant clinical deficit are supported by ASTER 2 and registry data, and are widely treated. Beyond that the risk-benefit narrows, because vessel calibre falls and perforation risk rises, and the evidence thins considerably.
    probe: What is your decision rule for an M2 in a patient with a low NIHSS?
    probeA: I treat the deficit, not the number. If the deficit is disabling for that person, I would offer thrombectomy even with a low NIHSS: an isolated aphasia in a teacher scores poorly on NIHSS and is devastating in life. I weigh the accessibility of the M2, whether it is a dominant or inferior division, the size of the territory at risk, and the patient's baseline function, and I make it a shared decision rather than applying a cut-off.

    stem: A 68-year-old man was last seen well 11 hours ago and woke with a dense right hemiplegia and global aphasia. NIHSS is 20. Non-contrast CT shows no haemorrhage with an ASPECTS of 8. CT angiography shows a left M1 occlusion. He is not a thrombolysis candidate.
    q1: Is he eligible for thrombectomy, and on what basis?
    a1: Potentially yes, in the extended window. He is beyond six hours, so selection is physiological rather than chronological. I need CT perfusion to define core and penumbra and apply DAWN or DEFUSE 3 criteria: DAWN uses clinical-core mismatch out to twenty-four hours with core thresholds of twenty-one, thirty-one or fifty-one millilitres by stratum; DEFUSE 3 uses perfusion mismatch with a ratio of one point eight or more out to sixteen hours.
    q2: Perfusion shows a 15-millilitre core with a 90-millilitre penumbra. Interpret and act.
    a2: A mismatch ratio of six with a small core and a large penumbra: substantial salvageable tissue. He qualifies comfortably under both DAWN and DEFUSE 3. With an NIHSS of twenty, an ASPECTS of eight and an M1 occlusion, I would proceed to thrombectomy urgently, because the penumbra is dying while we discuss it.
    q3: Had his ASPECTS been 4 with a 90-millilitre core, what would you have said?
    a3: Standard teaching is that an ASPECTS below six indicates a large established core and is a relative contraindication, because reperfusing infarcted tissue risks haemorrhagic transformation without functional gain.     q4: After successful recanalisation he deteriorates at 18 hours with a falling GCS. What are you thinking and what do you do?
    a4: Haemorrhagic transformation of the infarct, or malignant middle cerebral artery oedema with herniation. Immediate non-contrast CT. If haemorrhagic, reverse any anticoagulation and control blood pressure. If malignant oedema in a large territory infarct, this is where decompressive hemicraniectomy is considered, and the discussion about age, dominant hemisphere involvement and acceptable disability must happen quickly with the family.

This is ischemic stroke thrombectomy criteria, one of the highest-yield vascular topics on the ABNS oral boards. You will be expected to know the pathophysiology cold, recite trial data by name, apply inclusion and exclusion criteria to a clinical scenario, and manage complications. Let's build this from the ground up.

We start with the foundation. Ischemic stroke from large vessel occlusion is fundamentally a time-dependent emergency built around two tissue compartments: the ischemic core and the ischemic penumbra. The core is brain tissue that is already irreversibly infarcted. It has lost its electrical and membrane function. It cannot be saved. The penumbra is the tissue surrounding the core that is ischemic but still viable. It is surviving on collateral blood flow, but it is at risk. Every minute that passes without reperfusion, approximately 1.9 million neurons die. The entire goal of acute stroke intervention, whether pharmacologic or mechanical, is to salvage the penumbra before it converts to core.

Large vessel occlusion patterns that you must know include the internal carotid artery terminus, the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery, the M2 segment, and the basilar artery. The ICA terminus occlusion is the most devastating supratentorial pattern because it cuts off flow to the entire MCA and often the ACA territory. The M1 occlusion is the most common target for thrombectomy and produces a classic syndrome of contralateral hemiplegia, hemianesthesia, gaze preference toward the lesion, and, if dominant hemisphere, global aphasia, or if nondominant hemisphere, hemispatial neglect. M2 occlusions produce more limited but still potentially disabling deficits. Basilar artery occlusion threatens the brainstem and can produce coma, locked-in syndrome, or death.

You need to know the NIHSS, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale. This is a 42-point scale that quantifies stroke severity across domains including level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor strength in all four extremities, limb ataxia, sensory function, language, dysarthria, and extinction or inattention. For thrombectomy candidacy, the threshold is generally an NIHSS of 6 or greater. An NIHSS of 1 to 4 is a minor stroke. An NIHSS of 5 to 15 is moderate. An NIHSS of 16 to 20 is moderate to severe. An NIHSS above 20 is severe. The case you will encounter on boards will typically have an NIHSS in the 15 to 22 range to make the decision straightforward.

Now, ASPECTS. The Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score is a 10-point scoring system applied to a noncontrast CT of the head. A normal scan receives a score of 10. Each region that shows early ischemic changes, meaning loss of gray-white differentiation, sulcal effacement, or hypoattenuation, loses 1 point. The ten regions are as follows: C is the caudate nucleus, L is the lentiform nucleus, IC is the internal capsule, I is the insular ribbon, and then there are six cortical MCA regions labeled M1 through M6, where M1, M2, and M3 are at the level of the basal ganglia and M4, M5, and M6 are at the level above the basal ganglia corresponding to the superior slices. An ASPECTS of 6 or greater is generally considered favorable for thrombectomy. An ASPECTS of 5 or below suggests a large established infarct core, and thrombectomy in that setting carries high risk of hemorrhagic transformation with low likelihood of meaningful recovery.

For IV tPA, you must know the criteria precisely. Intravenous alteplase is indicated within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. The original NINDS trial in 1995 established benefit within 3 hours. The ECASS III trial in 2008 extended the window to 4.5 hours, though with additional exclusion criteria including age over 80, NIHSS over 25, history of both diabetes and prior stroke, and oral anticoagulant use regardless of INR. The dose is 0.9 milligrams per kilogram with a maximum total dose of 90 milligrams. You give 10 percent as an IV bolus over 1 minute and infuse the remaining 90 percent over 60 minutes. Major contraindications include active internal bleeding, recent intracranial surgery or serious head trauma within 3 months, intracranial neoplasm, platelet count below 100,000, INR above 1.7, and blood pressure that cannot be controlled below 185 over 110.

Now we move to the clinical case. A 67-year-old right-handed man presents to the emergency department with acute onset left-sided weakness and neglect. His wife reports he was last seen normal 6 hours ago. On examination, he has a dense left hemiplegia, right gaze preference, left hemianopia, left facial droop, left-sided neglect, and dysarthria. His NIHSS is 18. A noncontrast CT head shows no hemorrhage. His ASPECTS is 7, indicating early ischemic changes in three regions but preserved tissue in the remaining seven. A CT angiogram demonstrates a right M1 occlusion with good collaterals filling the distal MCA territory. CT perfusion shows a core infarct volume of 22 milliliters and a penumbral volume of 140 milliliters, giving a large mismatch. This patient has a salvageable brain. The question is what you do about it, and this is where the examiner begins.

The examiner asks, is this patient a tPA candidate. The answer is no. His last known normal was 6 hours ago. IV tPA is only indicated within the 4.5-hour window. He is outside the treatment window for thrombolysis. Do not give tPA. If you say yes, you have just failed the question.

The examiner then asks, is this patient a thrombectomy candidate. The answer is yes. He meets all criteria. He has a documented large vessel occlusion, specifically a right M1 occlusion on CTA. His NIHSS is 18, which is well above the threshold of 6. His ASPECTS is 7, which is above the threshold of 6. And his CT perfusion demonstrates a large penumbra with a small core, which qualifies him for extended-window thrombectomy under the DAWN and DEFUSE 3 paradigms. He is within 24 hours of last known normal.

The examiner will now expect you to walk through the landmark trial data. In 2015, five randomized controlled trials were published in rapid succession, and all five demonstrated clear benefit of mechanical thrombectomy plus best medical management over best medical management alone for large vessel occlusion stroke within 6 hours.

MR CLEAN was the first, published from the Netherlands. It enrolled patients with anterior circulation large vessel occlusion within 6 hours and showed an absolute difference of 13.5 percentage points in the rate of functional independence at 90 days, defined as a modified Rankin Score of 0 to 2.

ESCAPE, from Canada, required moderate to good collateral circulation on CTA and an ASPECTS of 6 or greater. It showed a number needed to treat of approximately 4 and demonstrated a dramatic reduction in mortality from 29 percent in the control group to 10 percent in the intervention group.

EXTEND-IA, from Australia, used CT perfusion to select patients with a significant mismatch between core and penumbra. It showed 71 percent reperfusion in the intervention arm and a striking improvement in functional outcomes.

SWIFT PRIME used the Solitaire stent retriever and enrolled patients with small core infarcts confirmed on imaging. It showed a 25 percentage-point absolute increase in functional independence.

REVASCAT, from Spain, enrolled patients up to 8 hours from onset and also demonstrated significant benefit. All five trials were stopped early because of overwhelming efficacy.

The HERMES meta-analysis pooled individual patient data from all five trials and confirmed a number needed to treat of 2.6 to achieve one additional patient with functional independence. That is one of the most powerful treatment effects in all of medicine. The benefit was consistent across subgroups including age, sex, and stroke severity, though the effect was attenuated in patients with ASPECTS of 5 or below.

Now the examiner will push you into the extended window. DAWN was published in 2018 in the New England Journal of Medicine. It enrolled patients 6 to 24 hours from last known normal who had a mismatch between clinical deficit severity and infarct core volume, assessed using the RAPID automated perfusion software. The trial had three strata. Group A was patients aged 80 or older with an NIHSS of 10 or greater and a core volume less than 21 milliliters. Group B was patients under 80 with NIHSS of 10 or greater and core volume less than 31 milliliters. Group C was patients under 80 with NIHSS of 20 or greater and core volume between 31 and 51 milliliters. DAWN was stopped early for efficacy. The rate of functional independence at 90 days was 49 percent in the thrombectomy group versus 13 percent in the control group. The number needed to treat was approximately 2.8.

DEFUSE 3 was also published in 2018. It enrolled patients 6 to 16 hours from last known normal who had a perfusion mismatch ratio of 1.8 or greater, an ischemic core volume less than 70 milliliters, and a mismatch volume of at least 15 milliliters, all assessed on CT perfusion or MRI perfusion. DEFUSE 3 was also stopped early. Functional independence at 90 days was 45 percent versus 17 percent. These two trials fundamentally changed stroke care by extending the thrombectomy window from 6 hours to 24 hours for selected patients.

The examiner will now ask about procedural technique. You should be familiar with two primary approaches. Stent retriever thrombectomy uses a self-expanding stent, most commonly the Solitaire or the Trevo device, that is deployed across the thrombus, allowed to integrate with the clot for 3 to 5 minutes, and then retrieved along with the clot into an aspiration catheter positioned in the proximal vessel. Direct aspiration, also called the ADAPT technique, which stands for A Direct Aspiration first Pass Technique, uses a large-bore aspiration catheter advanced directly to the face of the clot, and suction is applied to extract the thrombus without deploying a stent. The combined approach, sometimes called Solumbra, uses both a stent retriever and simultaneous aspiration.

The ASTER trial, published in 2017, randomized patients to contact aspiration versus stent retriever as the first-line approach. It found no significant difference in successful reperfusion rates, which were approximately 85 percent in both groups. The COMPASS trial in 2019 similarly showed noninferiority of direct aspiration compared to stent retriever. In practice, most neurointerventionalists choose their technique based on clot location, vessel anatomy, and personal experience. The key board answer is that both approaches are acceptable first-line strategies, and the choice does not significantly impact outcomes.

The examiner then asks, what if it is a basilar occlusion. This is an important question because the evidence base for posterior circulation thrombectomy has historically been weaker. The BASICS trial, published in 2021, was a randomized trial comparing thrombectomy plus best medical therapy versus best medical therapy alone for basilar artery occlusion within 6 hours. It failed to show a statistically significant benefit for thrombectomy. However, the trial was widely criticized for being underpowered, having a high crossover rate, including patients with low NIHSS scores who had relatively mild posterior circulation strokes, and enrolling over a very long period during which background practice changed substantially.

The BEST trial from China also failed to show benefit, but it too was plagued by early termination and high crossover from the medical arm to the thrombectomy arm. However, the ATTENTION trial, published in 2022, enrolled patients with basilar artery occlusion within 12 hours and demonstrated a clear benefit for thrombectomy. Functional independence at 90 days was 46 percent in the thrombectomy group versus 23 percent in the medical group. Mortality was also significantly reduced.

On boards, the answer is that basilar artery occlusion is devastating with an extremely high mortality rate under medical management alone, the ATTENTION trial supports thrombectomy within 12 hours, and most major stroke centers offer thrombectomy for basilar occlusion despite the mixed earlier trial data, because the natural history is so poor that intervention is warranted.

Now the examiner will try to trap you. The first trap question is, would you give tPA before thrombectomy if the patient is within the tPA window. The answer is yes. This is called bridging therapy. If a patient presents within 4.5 hours with a large vessel occlusion, you administer IV tPA immediately and simultaneously activate the thrombectomy team. You do not delay tPA while waiting for the interventionalist to arrive. However, and this is equally important, you also do not delay thrombectomy to complete the 60-minute tPA infusion. If the interventionalist is ready and the patient is on the table, you proceed with thrombectomy even if the tPA infusion is still running. The tPA infusion can be paused or completed during the procedure. The principle is parallel processing: start both treatments as fast as possible without one delaying the other.

The second trap question is, the ASPECTS is 4, would you still intervene. The answer is generally no. An ASPECTS of 4 indicates a large established infarct core involving 6 of the 10 ASPECTS regions. The HERMES meta-analysis subgroup analysis showed that patients with ASPECTS of 5 or below did not derive significant benefit from thrombectomy and had substantially higher rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Thrombectomy in a large completed infarct risks massive hemorrhagic transformation. The board-safe answer is that you would not offer thrombectomy for an ASPECTS of 4 or below in the standard scenario. You should note, however, that the SELECT2 trial published in 2023 did explore thrombectomy in patients with large core infarcts and showed some benefit, but this is considered evolving evidence and the standard teaching remains that ASPECTS below 6 is a relative contraindication.

The third trap question is, would you treat an M2 occlusion. The answer is nuanced. The original 2015 trials predominantly enrolled patients with ICA and M1 occlusions. M2 occlusions were not consistently included. However, there is growing evidence from observational studies and subgroup analyses that thrombectomy for M2 occlusions can be beneficial when the patient has a disabling deficit. The ASTER 2 trial and other registry data support intervention for proximal M2 occlusions with significant clinical deficits. The board answer is that M2 thrombectomy is an evolving indication, it is reasonable to offer in the setting of a disabling deficit from a proximal M2 occlusion, but it was not included in the pivotal trials that established the evidence base.

Now let's discuss complication management. The most feared complication of thrombectomy is symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, which occurs in approximately 5 percent of patients. Hemorrhagic transformation exists on a spectrum from petechial hemorrhagic infarction, types HI-1 and HI-2, to parenchymal hematoma, types PH-1 and PH-2. PH-2, defined as a dense hematoma exceeding 30 percent of the infarct area with significant mass effect, is the most clinically significant and is associated with neurological deterioration and increased mortality. Management includes immediate reversal of any anticoagulation, blood pressure control, and consideration of surgical evacuation if the hematoma is large with significant mass effect and the patient is deteriorating.

Other procedural complications include vessel perforation, which can cause subarachnoid hemorrhage and requires immediate recognition and potential coiling or flow diversion. Distal embolization occurs when clot fragments break off and occlude previously unaffected distal branches. This can be managed with additional aspiration passes or deployment of a stent retriever into the distal vessel. Vasospasm can occur from catheter manipulation and is typically managed with intra-arterial verapamil or nicardipine.

Moving to the evidence summary. The five 2015 trials, MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME, and REVASCAT, established thrombectomy within 6 hours as standard of care for anterior circulation large vessel occlusion. The HERMES meta-analysis confirmed a number needed to treat of 2.6. DAWN extended the window to 24 hours using clinical-core mismatch. DEFUSE 3 extended the window to 16 hours using perfusion mismatch. ATTENTION established benefit for basilar thrombectomy within 12 hours. The ASTER and COMPASS trials demonstrated equivalence between aspiration and stent retriever techniques.

Now for the board pearls. These are the rapid-fire points you need to have memorized.

The tPA dose is 0.9 milligrams per kilogram, maximum 90 milligrams, 10 percent bolus, remainder over 60 minutes. The tPA window is 4.5 hours. The thrombectomy window is up to 24 hours with appropriate imaging selection.

ASPECTS of 6 or greater is the threshold for thrombectomy candidacy in the standard paradigm. NIHSS of 6 or greater. Core volume less than 70 milliliters for DEFUSE 3. Core volume less than 21, 31, or 51 milliliters depending on the DAWN stratum.

The number needed to treat in the 0 to 6 hour window is 2.6. The rate of symptomatic ICH is approximately 5 percent. The target for successful reperfusion is TICI 2b or 3, which means reperfusion of at least 50 percent of the downstream territory.

Never delay thrombectomy for tPA completion. Never give tPA outside the 4.5-hour window. Always get a noncontrast CT before any intervention to rule out hemorrhage. Always get a CTA to confirm large vessel occlusion. In the extended window, always get perfusion imaging.

Three things that will fail you on this question. First, giving tPA outside the 4.5-hour window. The examiner will tempt you with a 5-hour onset. Do not take the bait. Second, offering thrombectomy for an ASPECTS of 4 without acknowledging the poor evidence and high complication risk. The standard teaching is ASPECTS of 6 or above. Third, not knowing the difference between DAWN and DEFUSE 3. DAWN uses clinical-core mismatch and extends to 24 hours. DEFUSE 3 uses perfusion mismatch with a ratio of 1.8 or greater and extends to 16 hours. If you conflate them, the examiner will know you memorized a summary rather than learning the trials.

Know your five 2015 trial names in order: MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME, REVASCAT. Know that HERMES pooled them. Know that ATTENTION is the basilar trial that showed benefit. Know that stent retriever and aspiration are equivalent first-line approaches. Know your tPA dose and contraindications cold.

That covers ischemic stroke thrombectomy criteria. You're ready for this one.
