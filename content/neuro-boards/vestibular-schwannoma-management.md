Welcome to this viva-style teaching session on vestibular schwannoma management. This is a high-yield oral board topic that demands fluency in classification systems, a clear management algorithm, and nuanced surgical decision-making. Let's build from the foundation up.

A vestibular schwannoma is a benign tumor arising from the Schwann cells of the vestibular division of cranial nerve eight. It originates from either the superior or inferior vestibular nerve, most commonly at the Obersteiner-Redlich zone, which is the transition point between central and peripheral myelin within the internal auditory canal. This is the single most common tumor of the cerebellopontine angle, accounting for approximately eighty percent of all CPA lesions. The annual incidence is roughly one per one hundred thousand. Ninety-five percent are sporadic and unilateral. The remaining five percent are associated with neurofibromatosis type two and present bilaterally, which is essentially pathognomonic for NF2. We will come back to that distinction because it is a guaranteed trap question on oral boards.

The classic presentation is unilateral sensorineural hearing loss, which is the most common symptom at approximately ninety-five percent. This hearing loss is typically progressive over months to years. Tinnitus is the second most common complaint. Patients often report a vague sense of imbalance or unsteadiness, but true episodic vertigo is uncommon because the slow tumor growth allows central vestibular compensation. With larger tumors, patients may develop facial numbness from compression of the trigeminal nerve, cranial nerve five, particularly the sensory root. Facial weakness at presentation is rare because the facial nerve is displaced by the tumor, not invaded. That distinction matters. The tumor pushes the nerve; it does not infiltrate it, which is why the facial nerve can almost always be dissected free at surgery.

On workup, audiometry reveals asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss with speech discrimination scores that are disproportionately reduced relative to the pure tone average. This is a hallmark finding. Auditory brainstem response testing shows prolonged interpeak latencies, specifically the one-to-three and one-to-five intervals. MRI with gadolinium is the gold standard imaging study. On T1 post-contrast, the tumor enhances avidly and you will see an intracanalicular component with or without CPA extension. High-resolution T2 sequences, including CISS or FIESTA, are excellent for delineating the tumor from surrounding CSF and cranial nerves.

Now let's establish the classification systems because you must know these cold.

The Koos grading system classifies vestibular schwannomas by size and anatomic extent. Grade one is a purely intracanalicular tumor, less than one centimeter, confined to the internal auditory canal. Grade two extends into the cerebellopontine angle but is less than two centimeters and does not contact the brainstem. Grade three fills the CPA cistern, contacts the brainstem, and measures two to three centimeters. Grade four compresses the brainstem and displaces the fourth ventricle, measuring greater than three centimeters. Know all four grades and be able to assign them on imaging.

Hearing is classified by the Gardner-Robertson scale. Class one is serviceable hearing, defined as a pure tone average of thirty decibels or better and a speech discrimination score of seventy percent or better. Class two is also considered serviceable, with a pure tone average of thirty-one to fifty decibels and a speech discrimination score of fifty to sixty-nine percent. Classes three through five represent non-serviceable hearing with progressively worse thresholds and discrimination. The AAO-HNS classification uses classes A through D and is an alternative system, but Gardner-Robertson is more commonly referenced on neurosurgery boards. The key clinical question is whether hearing is serviceable or non-serviceable, and that distinction drives the surgical approach.

The House-Brackmann facial nerve grading scale is a six-point system that you must recite perfectly. Grade one is normal facial function. Grade two is slight weakness noticeable only on close inspection, with complete eye closure and slight asymmetry of smile. Grade three is obvious but not disfiguring weakness, with complete eye closure achieved with effort and slight mouth asymmetry. Grade four is obvious disfiguring weakness with incomplete eye closure and asymmetric mouth movement. Grade five is barely perceptible motion, with incomplete eye closure and slight movement at the corner of the mouth. Grade six is total paralysis, no movement whatsoever. Grades one and two are considered good outcomes. Grade three is acceptable. Grade four and beyond are poor outcomes that require intervention.

Now let's move into the clinical scenario.

A forty-five-year-old man presents with progressive right-sided hearing loss over two years and persistent right-sided tinnitus. He denies vertigo. Neurological examination is intact. Audiometry reveals a right pure tone average of forty-five decibels and a speech discrimination score of fifty-two percent. By Gardner-Robertson criteria, this is Class two, which is serviceable hearing. MRI with gadolinium shows a two-and-a-half-centimeter enhancing mass in the right cerebellopontine angle with extension into the internal auditory canal. The tumor contacts the brainstem without overt compression. There is no displacement of the fourth ventricle. This is Koos grade three. The facial nerve is not clearly visualized as a discrete structure separate from the tumor on imaging, which is typical at this size. So the question becomes: what do you do?

Let's now move into the examiner interrogation phase, which is the heart of the oral board encounter.

Three management options exist for vestibular schwannoma: observation with serial imaging and audiometry, stereotactic radiosurgery, and microsurgical resection. For this patient with a Koos grade three tumor and serviceable hearing, all three strategies are defensible, and the examiner wants to hear you discuss each one thoughtfully before arriving at a recommendation.

Starting with observation. Natural history data, including the Copenhagen series, shows that the average growth rate is one to two millimeters per year, and forty to fifty percent of vestibular schwannomas do not grow at all over the surveillance period. Observation is most appropriate for small tumors, particularly Koos grade one, for elderly patients with significant comorbidities, and for patients who decline intervention. The protocol is serial MRI every six months for the first two years and then annually thereafter, paired with serial audiometry. If the tumor grows or hearing deteriorates significantly, you intervene. For a Koos grade three tumor in a forty-five-year-old, pure observation is less ideal because the tumor is already contacting the brainstem, but it remains an option if the patient fully understands the trajectory.

Next, stereotactic radiosurgery. The standard marginal dose is twelve to thirteen Gray in a single fraction. This has been reduced from the historical doses of fourteen to sixteen Gray that were used in earlier series, and the dose reduction has significantly improved hearing preservation and facial nerve outcomes without sacrificing tumor control. Long-term data from Kondziolka and Lunsford at Pittsburgh demonstrate tumor control rates of ninety-two to ninety-eight percent at ten years. Hearing preservation is fifty to seventy-five percent at three years, though it continues to decline over time with longer follow-up. Facial nerve preservation with House-Brackmann grade one or two is achieved in greater than ninety-five percent of patients. SRS is best suited for tumors measuring two-and-a-half centimeters or less, though some experienced centers treat tumors up to three centimeters. For tumors larger than that, or for tumors in close proximity to the brainstem, hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy delivered in five fractions is an alternative that reduces the single-fraction dose to critical structures. For our patient with a two-and-a-half-centimeter tumor, SRS is a reasonable option, right at the upper size threshold.

Now microsurgery. There are three surgical approaches, and each has specific indications. You must know all three and be able to match the right approach to the right clinical scenario.

The translabyrinthine approach sacrifices hearing because you drill through the labyrinth, but it provides the best visualization of the facial nerve. You identify the nerve early in the lateral internal auditory canal before you even encounter the tumor, which gives you a clear anatomic roadmap. This approach works for any size tumor and requires no cerebellar retraction. It is the preferred approach when hearing is non-serviceable, meaning Gardner-Robertson class three or worse. In that setting, there is no hearing to preserve, so you trade hearing access for superior facial nerve safety.

The retrosigmoid approach, also called the suboccipital approach, is the workhorse for hearing preservation surgery. You perform a retrosigmoid craniotomy, retract the cerebellum, and approach the CPA from behind the sigmoid sinus. This approach is versatile and suitable for medium to large tumors, Koos grades two through four. It allows access to the CPA component and you can drill the posterior wall of the internal auditory canal to access the intracanalicular portion, though lateral access to the fundus is more limited than with the middle fossa approach. Known downsides include the need for cerebellar retraction and a higher rate of postoperative headache, which is attributed to bone dust in the CPA cistern and dural defects over the mastoid air cells. Meticulous irrigation and waxing of air cells mitigates this.

The middle cranial fossa approach is a hearing preservation approach specifically designed for small intracanalicular tumors, Koos grade one, generally one-and-a-half centimeters or less. It provides the best access to the lateral internal auditory canal and fundus, which is where these small tumors reside. It requires temporal lobe retraction and carries a higher risk of facial nerve injury in inexperienced hands because the facial nerve sits directly in the operative corridor at the level of the labyrinthine segment and geniculate ganglion. This approach has a limited role for tumors with a significant CPA component.

For our specific patient, a Koos grade three tumor with serviceable hearing, the retrosigmoid approach is the leading surgical option because it offers the possibility of hearing preservation while providing adequate exposure for a two-and-a-half-centimeter CPA tumor. Alternatively, if the patient's priority is the best possible facial nerve outcome and he accepts hearing loss, the translabyrinthine approach is preferred. Stereotactic radiosurgery is also reasonable at this size. This is where shared decision-making enters. You present all three options with their risk-benefit profiles and let the patient's priorities guide the final plan. The examiner wants to see that you can articulate this decision tree, not that you rigidly pick one answer.

Facial nerve management during microsurgery is a critical topic. You must use a facial nerve stimulator throughout the case. You identify the nerve as early as possible, trace it along the tumor capsule, and preserve anatomical continuity. The nerve is typically displaced anteriorly and inferiorly by the tumor. With large tumors, the nerve is stretched into a thin ribbon and may be nearly transparent, making it extremely vulnerable to inadvertent injury. At the end of resection, you stimulate the nerve to confirm continuity and estimate the degree of function.

If the facial nerve is severed intraoperatively, you perform primary reanastomosis if the ends can be approximated without tension. If there is a gap, you interpose a cable graft using either the sural nerve from the leg or the great auricular nerve from the neck. If the patient wakes up with House-Brackmann grade five or six despite anatomical continuity of the nerve, this represents neuropraxia or axonotmesis from manipulation and stretching. The nerve is in continuity but not conducting. Immediate management focuses on eye protection: a moisture chamber, frequent artificial tears, lubricating ointment at night, and taping the eye closed during sleep. You can place a gold weight in the upper eyelid or perform a tarsorrhaphy for corneal protection. Then you wait. Many patients recover to House-Brackmann grade three or four over the subsequent twelve months. Obtain an EMG at three to six months to look for early signs of reinnervation. If there is no recovery by twelve months, consider a hypoglossal-facial nerve anastomosis, which is a twelve-to-seven jump graft that reroutes motor fibers from the hypoglossal nerve to the distal facial nerve to restore tone and some voluntary movement.

Now let's handle the trap questions because these are what separate a pass from a fail.

The examiner says: a twenty-two-year-old presents with bilateral vestibular schwannomas. What is the diagnosis and how does management differ? The answer is neurofibromatosis type two. This is an autosomal dominant condition caused by a mutation on chromosome twenty-two q twelve, which encodes the tumor suppressor protein merlin, also called schwannomin. NF2 management is fundamentally more conservative than sporadic vestibular schwannoma management. The overriding goal is to preserve hearing in at least one ear for as long as possible because bilateral deafness is devastating in a young patient. You observe tumors that are not growing. Surgery is reserved for tumors causing brainstem compression or progressive hearing loss in the better-hearing ear when the contralateral ear is already deaf. SRS is used cautiously in NF2 because there are rare reports of malignant transformation to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, though this risk is low. Bevacizumab, a VEGF inhibitor, has shown efficacy in NF2-associated vestibular schwannomas based on the work of Plotkin and colleagues. It can reduce tumor volume and stabilize or improve hearing. If bilateral hearing is lost, options include cochlear implantation if the cochlear nerve is intact, or an auditory brainstem implant placed at the cochlear nucleus if the cochlear nerves are not functional. Know this pathway.

Second trap question. The examiner says: you complete resection and the facial nerve is anatomically intact, but the patient has House-Brackmann grade six on postoperative exam. What happened and what do you do? This is neuropraxia or axonotmesis. The nerve is in continuity but is not conducting due to manipulation, stretching, thermal injury, or ischemia during the dissection. The key is that anatomical continuity does not guarantee immediate functional recovery. Your immediate response is eye protection with a moisture chamber, lubrication, and taping. Do not rush to reinnervation procedures. Wait twelve months and follow with serial clinical exams and EMG at three to six months to assess for reinnervation potentials. Many patients will recover to House-Brackmann three or four. If there is no recovery at twelve months, proceed with hypoglossal-facial anastomosis.

Third trap question. The examiner says: you performed a subtotal resection to preserve the facial nerve. Now the residual is growing on surveillance imaging. Would you reoperate? The preferred approach is to treat the growing residual with stereotactic radiosurgery before considering reoperation. Reoperation in a previously operated field carries significantly higher facial nerve morbidity because of scarring and loss of the normal arachnoid dissection plane. SRS can control a residual effectively. You reserve reoperation for cases where SRS fails to control growth or where the residual tumor is too large for safe radiosurgery dosing.

Let's move into the evidence base. The Samii series from Hannover represents one of the largest microsurgical experiences in the world and has established benchmarks for facial nerve preservation and hearing outcomes across all Koos grades. The Kondziolka and Lunsford data from Pittsburgh provide the strongest long-term evidence for SRS efficacy, demonstrating durable tumor control rates exceeding ninety percent at more than a decade of follow-up with low cranial nerve morbidity at modern dosing. The Copenhagen vestibular schwannoma management studies provide essential natural history data on growth rates and support the role of observation for small tumors in the initial management algorithm. The Plotkin study on bevacizumab for NF2-associated vestibular schwannomas demonstrated tumor shrinkage in over fifty percent of patients and hearing improvement in over a third, establishing a medical treatment option for a disease historically managed only with surgery and radiation. The House-Brackmann scale remains the universally accepted standard for facial nerve grading and is validated across disciplines.

Now for the board pearls to lock this down.

Know all four Koos grades and be able to assign them from a single MRI image. Grade one is intracanalicular and less than one centimeter. Grade two enters the CPA but stays under two centimeters without brainstem contact. Grade three fills the CPA and contacts the brainstem at two to three centimeters. Grade four compresses the brainstem and displaces the fourth ventricle at greater than three centimeters.

For approach selection, use this algorithm. Non-serviceable hearing at any tumor size: translabyrinthine. Serviceable hearing with a small intracanalicular tumor: middle cranial fossa. Serviceable hearing with a medium to large tumor and CPA component: retrosigmoid. Tumor two-and-a-half centimeters or less with serviceable hearing and patient prefers non-surgical management: stereotactic radiosurgery at twelve to thirteen Gray. Small tumor, elderly patient, or patient declines treatment: observation with serial MRI and audiometry.

Know the House-Brackmann scale from one to six. One is normal. Two is slight. Three is obvious but not disfiguring with complete eye closure. Four is disfiguring with incomplete eye closure. Five is barely perceptible motion. Six is nothing.

NF2 red flags: bilateral vestibular schwannomas, young patient, family history, chromosome twenty-two q twelve, merlin mutation, and the management pivot toward hearing preservation, conservative surgery, bevacizumab, and auditory brainstem implants.

The marginal dose for SRS is twelve to thirteen Gray. Tumor control exceeds ninety-two percent at ten years. Facial nerve outcomes with SRS are greater than ninety-five percent House-Brackmann one or two.

If the facial nerve is anatomically intact but not functioning postoperatively, protect the eye, wait twelve months, and consider hypoglossal-facial anastomosis only if there is no recovery.

For growing residual after subtotal resection, radiosurgery first, reoperation second.

That covers vestibular schwannoma management. You're ready for this one.
