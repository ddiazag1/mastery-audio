questions:
  - q: Give me a structured approach to postoperative fever.
    a: Work through by timing and source. Early, within forty-eight hours: atelectasis, inflammatory response to surgery, transfusion, drug fever, and malignant hyperthermia. Later: wound, urinary tract, lines, chest, and venous thromboembolism. Then the neurosurgery-specific causes — meningitis, ventriculitis, shunt or drain infection, and chemical meningitis.
    probe: Which of those is most often missed?
    probeA: Venous thromboembolism, because it presents as an isolated low-grade fever in a patient in whom everyone is looking for an infection. Non-convulsive seizure activity and drug fever are the other two that get missed, drug fever particularly because the patient is on several agents and nobody reviews the chart. I would add deep infection under an apparently clean wound, especially with instrumentation, which is missed because the wound is inspected and pronounced fine.
  - q: What proportion of deep vein thromboses cause fever?
    a: Approximately thirty percent of patients with deep vein thrombosis have an associated low-grade fever. That is why thromboembolism belongs on the fever differential rather than being considered only when the leg swells, particularly in an immobile neurosurgical patient.
    probe: So what would you order in an immobile day-five craniotomy patient with a low-grade fever?
    probeA: Bilateral lower limb venous duplex, and a CT pulmonary angiogram if there is any tachycardia, hypoxia, pleuritic pain or unexplained tachypnoea rather than waiting for the legs to be positive. Alongside that a standard septic screen: blood cultures, urine, chest imaging, wound inspection, inflammatory markers, and cerebrospinal fluid if there is any indwelling device or any change in conscious level. The point is that the thromboembolism workup runs in parallel with the infection workup, not after it has come back negative.
  - q: Quote the spinal wound infection rates.
    a: Two to five percent, higher with instrumentation. The presentation is often subtle rather than florid: persistent wound drainage, increasing back pain out of keeping with the postoperative course, and low-grade fever. A clean-looking wound does not exclude deep infection.
    probe: What would you do about persistent drainage at ten days?
    probeA: Treat it as a deep infection until proven otherwise rather than as a wound that is taking its time. Persistent drainage at ten days, particularly with instrumentation, is the classic subtle presentation. I would examine for fluctuance and tenderness, send inflammatory markers serially, obtain contrast-enhanced MRI looking for a deep collection or discitis, and take the patient back for formal exploration, debridement and deep tissue cultures with a low threshold. I would not swab the surface and start oral antibiotics, which contaminates the culture, treats the wrong organism and delays the definitive operation.
  - q: The Gram stain is negative. How much does that reassure you?
    a: Not much. Gram stain sensitivity is only sixty to ninety percent, so a negative result does not rule out bacterial infection. I would treat on the overall picture — cell count, glucose, protein, clinical trajectory — and wait for culture rather than being falsely reassured.
    probe: What cerebrospinal fluid parameters would you weigh instead?
    probeA: The cell count and differential, the cerebrospinal fluid to serum glucose ratio, which is more informative than the absolute glucose, the protein, and above all the trajectory over serial samples. A ratio below about half with a neutrophil predominance and rising protein supports bacterial infection. Lactate is useful where available, since it tends to be higher in bacterial than in chemical meningitis. I would also weigh the cell count against the amount of blood in the sample, because blood causes a reactive pleocytosis, and I would use molecular testing where available since it is not degraded by prior antibiotics the way culture is.
  - q: How do you distinguish chemical from bacterial meningitis after posterior fossa surgery?
    a: It is genuinely difficult, because both give neutrophilic pleocytosis. Chemical meningitis typically appears in the first days, is associated with blood or tumour contents in the cerebrospinal fluid, has a lower cell count with a normal or near-normal glucose, and settles with steroids. Bacterial infection has lower glucose, higher protein, and a positive culture — but culture takes time, so many are treated empirically.
    probe: So what do you actually do on the day?
    probeA: I treat empirically and let the trajectory decide. In a patient who is declining, with an indwelling drain and a neutrophilic pleocytosis, I start culture-appropriate empirical antibiotics with vancomycin plus an agent with good gram-negative and central nervous system penetration, after taking cultures. Then I follow serial samples: a rising cell count, falling glucose and rising protein confirm infection, while chemical meningitis peaks early, plateaus and settles. The error is to wait for certainty before treating, because ventriculitis treated late does badly, and the cost of two or three days of antibiotics in a patient who turns out to have chemical meningitis is small.
  - q: A patient with an external ventricular drain spikes a fever. Approach it.
    a: Sample the cerebrospinal fluid from the drain and send cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain and culture, while looking for every other source. Rising cell count, falling glucose and rising protein over serial samples matter more than any single value, because the drain itself causes a reactive pleocytosis.
    probe: Would you routinely sample daily?
    probeA: No. Routine daily sampling increases the infection risk through repeated breaches of the closed system and generates results that are difficult to interpret, since a drain reliably produces a reactive pleocytosis. I sample when there is a clinical trigger and then follow serially once a question has been raised. The distinction is between surveillance sampling, which I avoid, and serial sampling to track a suspected infection, which is useful.
  - q: Which fever demands action within minutes rather than hours?
    a: Fever with a declining conscious level, new deficit, or meningism in a patient with recent instrumentation — that is ventriculitis or an intracranial infection until proven otherwise. And malignant hyperthermia intraoperatively or immediately postoperatively, which is an anaesthetic emergency requiring dantrolene.
    probe: What else accompanies malignant hyperthermia?
    probeA: A rising end-tidal carbon dioxide, which is often the earliest sign, together with unexplained tachycardia, masseter or generalised muscle rigidity, tachypnoea, mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and a rising creatine kinase, and arrhythmia. Temperature rise is a late feature, which is why waiting for fever is dangerous. Treatment is to stop the trigger agent, give dantrolene at two point five milligrams per kilogram repeated as needed, hyperventilate with one hundred percent oxygen, cool actively, and treat the hyperkalaemia and arrhythmia.
  - q: The wound looks clean externally but he has had low-grade fevers for three days. What do you think?
    a: A clean-looking wound does not exclude a deep infection, particularly with instrumentation. I would examine for fluctuance, tenderness and drainage, image with contrast-enhanced MRI to look for a deep collection or discitis, send inflammatory markers serially, and have a low threshold for exploration, because deep infection around hardware will not resolve with antibiotics alone.
    probe: Do you remove the instrumentation?
    probeA: Not necessarily, and this is a genuine judgement. If the infection is early, the organism is sensitive, and the construct is still needed for stability, current practice is to retain the hardware, debride thoroughly, and give prolonged culture-directed antibiotics with suppression, because removing instrumentation from an unfused spine creates instability and deformity that cause more harm than the infection. If the infection is late with an established fusion, the hardware is loose, the organism is resistant or biofilm-forming, or the patient fails repeated debridement, then I would remove it. I would make that decision jointly with infectious diseases rather than alone.

Let's master postoperative fever workup in neurosurgery for the oral boards.

Fever in a neurosurgical patient is never an afterthought. It is a direct threat to your patient's brain. The examiner expects you to define fever precisely, explain why it is harmful in neurological patients specifically, differentiate central from infectious causes, walk through a timeline-based differential, and demonstrate a systematic workup protocol. This topic intersects with nearly every neurosurgical subspecialty, so you will see it embedded in case after case. Let's build the framework from the ground up.

Start with the definition. In the neurosurgical population, fever is defined as a core body temperature of 38.3 degrees Celsius or higher, which is 101 degrees Fahrenheit. Some intensivists use a lower threshold of 38.0 degrees Celsius for neurologically injured patients, but 38.3 is the standard definition used in most guidelines and clinical trials. You must state this number precisely on the oral boards.

Now, why fever matters in neurosurgery more than in other surgical specialties. Fever is actively harmful in neurologically injured patients for three interconnected reasons. First, fever increases cerebral metabolic demand. For every degree Celsius above normal, cerebral metabolic rate increases by approximately six to ten percent. In a brain that is already injured, whether from trauma, hemorrhage, tumor, or surgical manipulation, this increased metabolic demand outstrips the available oxygen and glucose supply, worsening secondary brain injury. Second, fever raises intracranial pressure. The mechanism is cerebral vasodilation in response to increased metabolic demand, which increases cerebral blood volume. The ICP increase is approximately 0.5 to 1 mmHg per degree Celsius above normal. In a patient already at the edge of their intracranial compliance curve, this small increase can be the difference between compensation and herniation. Third, fever worsens cerebral edema through blood-brain barrier disruption and exacerbates excitotoxic neurotransmitter release. The clinical data confirm this: febrile episodes in traumatic brain injury and SAH patients are independently associated with worse functional outcomes and higher mortality.

This is why the neuro ICU standard is aggressive normothermia targeting. You do not wait for fever to become a problem. You actively prevent it. Temperature goals in the neuro ICU are typically 36.0 to 37.5 degrees Celsius, maintained with scheduled acetaminophen 650 to 1000 milligrams every six hours, surface cooling blankets, and in refractory cases, intravascular cooling devices such as the Arctic Sun or Thermogard systems. Tell the examiner this proactively. It demonstrates you understand that fever control is neuroprotective.

Now let's move to the core of this topic: the timeline-based differential diagnosis. This is how you organize your thinking, and it is how the examiner expects you to present it.

Postoperative day zero through two, the immediate postoperative period. The most commonly cited cause of early postoperative fever is atelectasis. You will hear this stated as fact throughout your training, and you should be aware that recent evidence has challenged this dogma. Multiple studies have shown that atelectasis does not reliably cause fever, and the association may be coincidental rather than causal. However, for the boards, you should acknowledge the traditional teaching while noting the controversy. Early fever is common, occurring in up to forty percent of postoperative patients in the first forty-eight hours, and the majority of these fevers are noninfectious. Other causes in this window include blood product transfusion reactions, which can produce febrile nonhemolytic reactions or, more dangerously, acute hemolytic reactions. Drug fever is another common culprit, particularly from anesthetics, beta-lactam antibiotics given as perioperative prophylaxis, and anticonvulsants such as phenytoin. Drug fever typically presents as an isolated fever without other systemic signs of infection, and it resolves within forty-eight to seventy-two hours of discontinuing the offending agent.

You must also know malignant hyperthermia for this window. It is rare but a board favorite. It is triggered by halogenated inhalational anesthetics such as sevoflurane or desflurane combined with succinylcholine, in genetically susceptible individuals with RYR1 mutations. Presentation includes rapidly rising temperature exceeding 40 degrees Celsius, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, hypercarbia with end-tidal CO2 above 55 mmHg, metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hyperkalemia. Treatment is immediate discontinuation of the triggering agent and dantrolene 2.5 milligrams per kilogram IV, repeated every five minutes up to 10 milligrams per kilogram total, plus aggressive cooling and hyperkalemia management. Dantrolene inhibits calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. If the examiner asks about malignant hyperthermia, saying "dantrolene" immediately is essential.

Postoperative day two through five. This is when infectious causes become the primary concern. Pneumonia is the most common nosocomial infection in the ICU, and in intubated patients, ventilator-associated pneumonia is the specific entity. VAP is defined as pneumonia developing more than forty-eight hours after endotracheal intubation. Diagnosis requires clinical criteria including fever, purulent secretions, leukocytosis, and a new or worsening infiltrate on chest X-ray. Incidence is roughly ten to twenty percent in patients ventilated for more than forty-eight hours. Urinary tract infection from catheter-associated bacteriuria is the second most common nosocomial infection. CAUTI risk increases approximately three to seven percent per day of catheterization. Diagnosis requires pyuria and a positive urine culture with greater than 100,000 colony-forming units per milliliter. Central line-associated bloodstream infection, or CLABSI, should be considered in any patient with a central venous catheter. Risk increases with duration of catheterization, with femoral lines carrying higher risk than subclavian or internal jugular lines. Diagnosis requires blood cultures drawn from both the central line and a peripheral site, with differential time to positivity. If the line culture turns positive two or more hours before the peripheral culture, this suggests the line is the source. Deep vein thrombosis also belongs in this window. Approximately thirty percent of patients with DVT will have an associated low-grade fever. This is easy to miss if you are focused only on infectious causes.

Postoperative day five through seven and beyond. This is when surgical site infections declare themselves. For craniotomy, the wound infection rate is one to three percent. For spinal surgery, it is two to five percent, with higher rates in instrumented fusion cases, posterior approaches, and patients with diabetes, obesity, or prolonged operative times. You will also see meningitis and ventriculitis in this window, particularly in patients with external ventricular drains or those who have undergone craniotomy with dural opening. Clostridium difficile colitis is an increasingly common cause of late postoperative fever, driven by perioperative antibiotic exposure. Risk factors include broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly fluoroquinolones and clindamycin, prolonged ICU stay, and proton pump inhibitor use. Suspect C. difficile in any postoperative patient with fever and diarrhea. Diagnosis is by stool testing for C. difficile toxin or PCR for the toxin gene. Finally, abscess formation can present in this late window, including epidural abscess after spinal surgery and brain abscess after open craniotomy, particularly if there has been a CSF leak or wound breakdown.

Now let's discuss the neurosurgery-specific causes of fever that distinguish our workup from that of any other surgical specialty.

Central fever. This is a diagnosis of exclusion, but you must understand it thoroughly. Central fever results from hypothalamic dysfunction, most commonly seen after subarachnoid hemorrhage, diffuse axonal injury, surgery near the hypothalamus such as craniopharyngioma resection, and occasionally with large thalamic or brainstem tumors. The characteristics that suggest central fever are as follows: onset is early, typically within seventy-two hours of the insult, particularly after SAH. The fever may be very high, exceeding 40 degrees Celsius. It does not respond well to antipyretics such as acetaminophen. There is an absence of an infectious source after a thorough and complete workup. Central fever cannot be diagnosed without ruling out every other cause first. Treatment of central fever relies on external cooling methods: surface cooling with blankets or pads, intravascular cooling catheters, and scheduled antipyretics even if their effect is modest. Bromocriptine, a dopamine agonist, has anecdotal support for refractory central fever in some case series but is not standard.

Meningitis and ventriculitis. If your patient has an external ventricular drain, the risk of ventriculitis is approximately one to two percent per day of EVD placement, with cumulative risk increasing over time, though the data on whether routine EVD exchange reduces infection are mixed. The critical challenge is distinguishing bacterial meningitis from chemical or aseptic meningitis. Chemical meningitis results from blood breakdown products irritating the meninges after hemorrhage or surgery and from intrathecal agents. Both bacterial and chemical meningitis can present with CSF pleocytosis, elevated protein, and low glucose. Here is how you differentiate them. In bacterial meningitis, the CSF glucose is typically less than 40 milligrams per deciliter or less than forty percent of the serum glucose. The protein is markedly elevated, often above 200 milligrams per deciliter. The cell count shows a neutrophilic predominance, and gram stain may be positive, though sensitivity of gram stain is only sixty to ninety percent depending on the organism. In chemical meningitis, you may see a similar cell count and even neutrophilic predominance, but the glucose is usually preserved or only mildly depressed, and gram stain and cultures are negative. The key board pearl: in post-hemorrhagic or post-surgical CSF, a declining glucose trend on serial samples is more concerning for infection than a single value. If the CSF glucose is dropping over consecutive days, treat empirically for bacterial meningitis until cultures result. Treatment for post-neurosurgical meningitis is vancomycin plus either cefepime or meropenem to cover gram-positive organisms including MRSA and gram-negative organisms including Pseudomonas. If the patient has an EVD, intrathecal vancomycin at 10 to 20 milligrams per day may be added for refractory cases.

Wound infection. Inspect the wound on every encounter. Look for erythema, warmth, induration, fluctuance, and drainage. Distinguish between superficial infection above the galea and deep infection that is subgaleal or epidural. Superficial infections may respond to antibiotics alone. Deep infections require operative washout, debridement, and removal of any implanted hardware including bone flaps, plates, and screws. The bone flap is replaced with a cranioplasty after infection clearance, typically at three to six months. Post-spine wound infection rates are two to five percent, higher with instrumentation, and the presentation may be subtle: persistent wound drainage, increasing back pain, and low-grade fever. The wound may look clean on the surface while harboring deep infection around the hardware. MRI with gadolinium is the study of choice, looking for enhancing epidural or paraspinal collections, hardware-associated enhancement, and phlegmonous changes.

CSF leak. A CSF leak is not a fever cause per se, but it dramatically increases meningitis risk, making it essential to this discussion. Look for subcutaneous CSF collection, clear wound drainage, rhinorrhea after transsphenoidal or anterior skull base surgery, and otorrhea after temporal bone surgery. Confirm with the beta-2 transferrin test, which is highly specific for CSF. For cranial CSF leaks, first-line treatment is a lumbar drain at 10 to 15 milliliters per hour for three to five days. Persistent leaks require operative revision and dural repair. As long as the leak persists, the meningitis risk remains elevated.

Now let's talk about the systematic workup protocol. When your patient spikes a fever, you execute the following in a standardized fashion every single time. Blood cultures: two sets drawn from two separate peripheral venipuncture sites, and one set drawn from any central venous catheter that is in place. Urinalysis and urine culture. Chest X-ray, or CT chest if you have high clinical suspicion for pulmonary embolism. CSF analysis if an EVD is in place: send for cell count with differential, glucose, protein, gram stain, and culture. Wound inspection: physically examine every surgical wound, every drain site, every pin site. Review the medication list systematically for drugs that cause fever. Check stool for Clostridium difficile toxin if the patient has diarrhea and has received antibiotics in the preceding four to eight weeks. This is a protocol, not a menu. You do all of these, not some of these.

Let's move to the clinical case.

A fifty-eight-year-old man is postoperative day three after a suboccipital craniotomy for resection of a posterior fossa meningioma. The surgery involved dural opening and the dura was closed primarily with a dural substitute graft. He has an EVD in place for postoperative hydrocephalus management. You are called because his temperature is 39.2 degrees Celsius. He is complaining of worsening headache and photophobia. His heart rate is 105, blood pressure 145 over 88. On exam he has nuchal rigidity.

Examiner: "What is your differential and what do you do?"

Your answer: On postoperative day three after a posterior fossa craniotomy with dural opening and an EVD in place, my primary concern is meningitis or ventriculitis. However, I do not anchor to a single diagnosis. My differential includes bacterial meningitis or ventriculitis, chemical or aseptic meningitis from surgical manipulation and blood products in the CSF, wound infection with deep extension, pneumonia given his recent general anesthesia and recumbent positioning, urinary tract infection if he has an indwelling catheter, line infection if he has a central line, drug fever from perioperative antibiotics, and DVT. I execute my full fever workup protocol immediately. I draw two sets of peripheral blood cultures and one set from his central line. I send a urinalysis and urine culture. I obtain a chest X-ray. I draw CSF from his EVD and send it for cell count with differential, glucose, protein, gram stain, and culture. I examine his posterior fossa wound for erythema, drainage, or fluctuance. I review his medication list and check for recent bowel changes.

Examiner: "The CSF comes back with a white blood cell count of 200 with eighty percent neutrophils, protein is elevated at 180, glucose is 32, and gram stain shows no organisms. But the patient had a craniotomy three days ago and there is blood in the CSF. Is this infection or chemical meningitis?"

Your answer: This CSF profile is concerning for bacterial meningitis despite the recent surgery and blood in the CSF. Let me explain my reasoning. The CSF glucose of 32 milligrams per deciliter is low. I would compare this to a simultaneous serum glucose. If the CSF-to-serum glucose ratio is less than 0.4, that favors bacterial infection. Chemical meningitis from blood breakdown products typically does not depress the glucose to this degree. The protein of 180 is elevated, but this can be seen in both conditions. The neutrophilic predominance of eighty percent is seen in both early bacterial meningitis and chemical meningitis, so cell differential alone does not help me. The gram stain is negative, but gram stain sensitivity is only sixty to ninety percent, so a negative gram stain does not rule out bacterial infection. Given the combination of clinical signs including fever, headache, photophobia, and nuchal rigidity with a CSF glucose below 40, I will treat this as bacterial meningitis until cultures prove otherwise. I start empiric vancomycin plus cefepime or meropenem immediately. I do not wait for culture results. I will also obtain a CT head to rule out hydrocephalus, abscess, or other intracranial pathology. If cultures are negative at seventy-two hours and the clinical picture improves, I can reassess and potentially narrow or discontinue antibiotics. But I do not withhold antibiotics in this scenario.

Examiner: "Postoperative day one after clipping of a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm, the patient spikes to 38.5 degrees Celsius. Do you start antibiotics?"

Your answer: No. I do not reflexively start antibiotics for a fever of 38.5 on postoperative day one after SAH. The timing makes infection unlikely. This early fever is far more likely central fever from hypothalamic irritation by subarachnoid blood, a drug or transfusion reaction, or the systemic inflammatory response to surgery. I perform my standard fever workup: blood cultures, urinalysis, chest X-ray, wound inspection. If the workup is negative, I treat the fever with acetaminophen and surface cooling because fever is harmful to this brain regardless of cause. I do not start empiric antibiotics without an identified or strongly suspected infectious source. Unnecessary antibiotics expose the patient to drug reactions, C. difficile risk, and resistant organism selection. The examiner is testing whether you can resist the reflex to treat every fever with antibiotics.

Examiner: "Your patient is postoperative day six after a lumbar fusion with instrumentation. The wound looks clean externally, but the patient has had persistent low-grade fevers for three days. What are you missing?"

Your answer: A clean-appearing wound surface does not exclude a deep infection. After instrumented spine surgery, infection can track along hardware into the epidural space while the superficial layers remain intact. I am concerned about a deep surgical site infection or epidural abscess. My next step is an MRI of the lumbar spine with and without gadolinium, looking for enhancing epidural collections, paraspinal soft tissue enhancement, phlegmonous changes around the instrumentation, and signs of discitis or osteomyelitis. I also check inflammatory markers. CRP is more useful than ESR for trending because it rises and falls more quickly. A CRP that was declining postoperatively and then begins to rise again is highly suspicious for deep infection. If imaging confirms infection, this patient needs wound exploration, irrigation and debridement, deep tissue cultures, and a decision about hardware. If the hardware is stable and infection is caught early, I attempt hardware salvage with thorough debridement and prolonged IV antibiotics, typically six weeks of pathogen-directed therapy. If the infection is severe or hardware is loose, it may need removal.

Now let's discuss the trap questions and board pearls.

Trap question one: the examiner shows you a rising CSF white blood cell count and asks if this proves infection. It does not. In the post-hemorrhagic setting, CSF white blood cell counts can be elevated from the inflammatory response to blood. What matters more than the absolute count is the trend and the glucose. A rising WBC with a falling glucose is infection until proven otherwise. A stable or declining WBC with preserved glucose is likely chemical meningitis.

Trap question two: the examiner asks about prophylactic antibiotics for EVD placement. Current evidence supports a single dose of perioperative antibiotics, typically cefazolin, at the time of EVD insertion. Prolonged prophylactic antibiotics for the duration of EVD placement are not recommended because they increase the risk of resistant organism selection without clearly reducing ventriculitis rates. The exception is antibiotic-impregnated catheters, which have shown a reduction in catheter-related infections in some studies.

Trap question three: the examiner asks when you remove a bone flap for infection. An infected bone flap cannot be salvaged. The standard is removal, debridement, prolonged antibiotics, and delayed cranioplasty at three to six months after infection clearance. All associated hardware is removed. An infected cranioplasty implant is removed entirely.

Board pearl one: fever in a neurosurgical patient is never benign, even if noninfectious, because of its direct harmful effects on the injured brain. Treat the fever itself aggressively while you work up the cause.

Board pearl two: the classic mnemonic for postoperative fever is the five Ws. Wind is atelectasis and pneumonia. Water is UTI. Walking is DVT. Wound is surgical site infection. Wonder drugs is drug fever. The examiner knows this mnemonic. Use it as a framework and build your neurosurgery-specific differentials on top of it.

Board pearl three: when the examiner asks about central fever after SAH, always state it is a diagnosis of exclusion. Never diagnose central fever without completing a full infectious workup first. The examiner wants to hear that you are thorough and systematic, not that you jump to a convenient diagnosis that avoids further testing.

Board pearl four: know your CSF interpretation cold. Normal CSF has zero to five white blood cells per microliter, protein of 15 to 45 milligrams per deciliter, and glucose of 40 to 80 milligrams per deciliter or greater than sixty percent of serum glucose. In bacterial meningitis, expect WBC greater than 1,000 with neutrophilic predominance, protein above 200, and glucose below 40. In chemical meningitis, WBC may be elevated but glucose is typically preserved and gram stain and cultures are negative.

Board pearl five: C. difficile is the hidden killer on the boards. Any patient with fever and diarrhea who has received antibiotics must be tested. First-line treatment is oral vancomycin 125 milligrams four times daily for ten days, or fidaxomicin 200 milligrams twice daily for ten days. For fulminant C. difficile with toxic megacolon, add IV metronidazole 500 milligrams every eight hours and involve surgery for possible subtotal colectomy. Do not use oral metronidazole as first-line therapy. This changed in the 2017 IDSA guidelines and the examiner will expect you to know it.

The three things that will fail you on this topic. One: starting empiric antibiotics for every fever without a systematic workup and without differentiating infectious from noninfectious causes. Two: failing to recognize that fever is directly harmful in neurological patients and not mentioning aggressive normothermia targeting as a standard practice. Three: diagnosing central fever without completing a full infectious workup first, or conversely, missing a deep wound infection because the surface looks clean.

That covers postoperative fever workup in neurosurgery. You're ready for this one.
