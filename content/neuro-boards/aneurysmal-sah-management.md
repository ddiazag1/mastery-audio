Let's master aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage management for the oral boards.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage from a ruptured intracranial aneurysm is one of the highest-yield topics you will face. The examiner expects fluency in grading, acute management, definitive treatment, and vasospasm protocols. You will be tested on decision-making speed and your ability to navigate complications. Let's build this from the ground up.

Start with the epidemiology and the aneurysm itself. Approximately eighty percent of non-traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage is caused by ruptured saccular aneurysms. Incidence is roughly six to eight per one hundred thousand per year. Risk factors include smoking, hypertension, family history of SAH in a first-degree relative, and connective tissue disorders such as autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease and Ehlers-Danlos type four. Women are affected roughly one and a half times more than men, with peak incidence in the fifth to sixth decades.

Aneurysm location matters because it determines your surgical approach. Ninety percent arise from the anterior circulation. The most common location is the anterior communicating artery, accounting for roughly thirty percent. The posterior communicating artery origin of the internal carotid is next at twenty-five percent, followed by the middle cerebral artery bifurcation at twenty percent. Posterior circulation aneurysms comprise roughly ten percent, with the basilar tip being the most common posterior location. Remember that aneurysm rupture risk increases with size. Aneurysms less than seven millimeters in the anterior circulation have a very low annual rupture rate of roughly 0.1 percent per year based on ISUIA data, though a ruptured aneurysm of any size has already declared itself and must be treated.

Now the grading systems. You must know two clinical grading scales and two radiographic scales cold.

The Hunt and Hess scale grades clinical severity from one to five. Grade one is asymptomatic or minimal headache with slight nuchal rigidity. Grade two is moderate to severe headache, nuchal rigidity, and no neurological deficit other than a possible cranial nerve palsy. Grade three is drowsiness, confusion, or mild focal deficit. Grade four is stupor, moderate to severe hemiparesis, possible early decerebrate posturing, and vegetative disturbances. Grade five is deep coma, decerebrate posturing, and a moribund appearance.

The World Federation of Neurological Surgeons scale, the WFNS scale, correlates with the Glasgow Coma Scale. Grade one is GCS fifteen with no motor deficit. Grade two is GCS thirteen to fourteen without motor deficit. Grade three is GCS thirteen to fourteen with motor deficit. Grade four is GCS seven to twelve. Grade five is GCS three to six. The WFNS is slightly more objective and reproducible than Hunt and Hess because it relies on the GCS.

For radiographic grading, the Fisher scale describes the amount and distribution of blood on the initial CT. Fisher grade one is no blood detected. Grade two is diffuse or thin layers of blood less than one millimeter thick. Grade three is a localized clot or thick layer of blood greater than one millimeter. Grade four is diffuse or no subarachnoid blood but with intraventricular or intracerebral hemorrhage. The problem with the original Fisher scale is that grade four paradoxically suggests lower vasospasm risk than grade three. The modified Fisher scale corrects this. Modified Fisher grade zero is no SAH and no intraventricular hemorrhage. Grade one is thin SAH without IVH. Grade two is thin SAH with IVH. Grade three is thick SAH without IVH. Grade four is thick SAH with IVH. The modified Fisher scale is more linearly predictive of vasospasm, with grade three and four carrying the highest risk, roughly thirty to forty percent incidence of clinical vasospasm.

Moving to the clinical case.

You are consulted in the emergency department on a fifty-two-year-old woman who presented with sudden onset of the worst headache of her life while lifting groceries. She had a brief loss of consciousness at the scene, witnessed by her husband. On arrival her GCS is fourteen: eyes four, verbal four, motor six. She is confused but following commands. She has nuchal rigidity, photophobia, and a left third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil. Her blood pressure is one eighty-five over one hundred and five. Heart rate ninety-two. A non-contrast CT head shows diffuse, thick subarachnoid blood in the basal cisterns with extension into the Sylvian fissures bilaterally and blood layering in the occipital horns of the lateral ventricles. There is no hydrocephalus on this initial scan.

This patient has a Hunt and Hess grade three, WFNS grade two, and modified Fisher grade four subarachnoid hemorrhage. The left third nerve palsy with pupil involvement points toward a left posterior communicating artery aneurysm until proven otherwise.

Now let's go through the examiner interrogation.

Examiner: "What is your initial management of this patient?"

Your answer: I follow a systematic approach. First, secure the airway. This patient has a GCS of fourteen and is protecting her airway, so I do not need to intubate at this moment, but I will have a low threshold. I place her in a monitored setting, ideally a neurointensive care unit. For blood pressure, I target a systolic blood pressure below one sixty using a nicardipine drip or clevidipine, titrated to effect. The goal is to reduce transmural pressure across the aneurysm wall and lower rebleeding risk without compromising cerebral perfusion. I start nimodipine sixty milligrams orally every four hours, which is the only medication proven to improve outcomes after SAH, specifically reducing the incidence of poor outcome from delayed cerebral ischemia. I obtain basic labs including a coagulation panel, type and screen, and a troponin because neurogenic cardiac stunning is common. I place a Foley catheter to monitor urine output strictly because both cerebral salt wasting and SIADH can occur. I order a CT angiogram of the head immediately to identify the aneurysm.

Examiner: "The CTA shows a seven-millimeter left posterior communicating artery aneurysm with an irregular dome. What is your plan for definitive treatment?"

Your answer: This aneurysm needs to be secured as soon as possible, ideally within twenty-four hours and certainly within seventy-two hours of hemorrhage. The risk of rebleeding is four percent in the first twenty-four hours and then roughly one to two percent per day for the first two weeks if left untreated. The two options for securing the aneurysm are microsurgical clipping and endovascular coiling. For this specific aneurysm, a seven-millimeter posterior communicating artery aneurysm, both approaches are reasonable. The ISAT trial, published in Lancet in 2002 with long-term follow-up in 2015, randomized over two thousand patients with ruptured aneurysms amenable to either treatment and showed that endovascular coiling resulted in a seven percent absolute risk reduction in death or dependency at one year compared with clipping. However, ISAT had important caveats: it included mostly anterior circulation aneurysms with favorable anatomy for coiling, the rebleeding rate was slightly higher in the coiled group on long-term follow-up, and retreatment rates were higher with coiling at roughly seventeen percent versus four percent for clipping.

For a posterior communicating artery aneurysm specifically, I would discuss with my endovascular colleagues. If the anatomy is favorable for coiling, meaning an adequate dome-to-neck ratio of at least two to one and no critical branches arising from the aneurysm neck, coiling is a reasonable first-line approach. If the neck is wide or the anatomy is unfavorable, microsurgical clipping via a pterional or orbitozygomatic approach is my preference. In this patient with a third nerve palsy, there is a theoretical advantage to clipping because decompression of the third nerve by removing the aneurysm from it may improve the palsy, whereas coiling leaves the mass effect in place. I would communicate this to the patient and family as part of shared decision-making.

Examiner: "You decide to clip this aneurysm. Walk me through your approach."

Your answer: I perform a left-sided pterional craniotomy. I position the patient supine with the head turned approximately thirty degrees to the right, extended slightly, and secured in a Mayfield head holder. I make a curvilinear incision behind the hairline, elevate a myocutaneous flap, and perform a frontotemporal craniotomy centered on the pterion. I remove the sphenoid wing down to the anterior clinoid process with a high-speed drill. I open the dura in a curvilinear fashion based on the sphenoid wing. My microsurgical approach proceeds through the Sylvian fissure. I open the fissure widely to release CSF and gain relaxation. I identify the optic nerve, the internal carotid artery, and then follow the ICA to its posterior communicating artery origin. I identify the aneurysm neck, the posterior communicating artery itself, and the anterior choroidal artery, which is the structure I must preserve at all costs in this region. I obtain proximal control on the ICA before any manipulation of the aneurysm. I select an appropriately sized clip, typically a curved or angled clip, and apply it across the neck of the aneurysm, ensuring complete obliteration of the neck while preserving flow in the posterior communicating artery, the anterior choroidal artery, and the parent ICA. I confirm clip placement with micro-Doppler ultrasonography, indocyanine green videoangiography, and if available, intraoperative catheter angiography.

Examiner, and this is a trap question: "During dissection, the aneurysm ruptures prematurely. What do you do?"

Your answer: This is an intraoperative emergency but it is manageable if I am prepared. My first move is to apply temporary clips to the internal carotid artery proximally and distally to achieve proximal and distal vascular control. I have already exposed the ICA for exactly this reason. I use suction to clear the field and proceed with rapid clip application. If I cannot visualize the neck, I can place a temporary clip directly on the ruptured dome to slow hemorrhage while I continue dissection of the neck. I instruct anesthesia to transiently lower the blood pressure. I do not panic. The key principle is that premature rupture can almost always be controlled with temporary clipping, and the definitive clip can still be applied. The safe temporary occlusion time is roughly five to seven minutes at normothermia, extendable with burst suppression using propofol or etomidate. I avoid prolonged temporary clipping beyond ten minutes whenever possible.

Examiner: "Postoperatively, on day five, the patient becomes acutely confused and develops new right arm weakness. What is your differential and how do you manage this?"

Your answer: The timing is classic. Day five to fourteen is the window for cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia, or DCI, with peak incidence between days seven and ten. My differential includes vasospasm causing DCI, which is the most likely diagnosis. I also consider rebleeding from an incompletely clipped aneurysm, hydrocephalus, seizure, and metabolic derangement such as hyponatremia. My immediate workup includes a STAT non-contrast CT head to rule out rebleeding and evaluate for hydrocephalus. I check a basic metabolic panel for sodium, because both cerebral salt wasting and SIADH cause hyponatremia, though they require different treatments. I start continuous EEG monitoring to rule out nonconvulsive status epilepticus.

If the CT is negative for new hemorrhage and there is no hydrocephalus, I presume vasospasm until proven otherwise. I obtain a CT angiogram, which may show arterial narrowing, or a CT perfusion study, which may demonstrate an increase in mean transit time and a decrease in cerebral blood flow in the left middle cerebral artery territory. I initiate hemodynamic augmentation. The classic triple-H therapy of hypertension, hypervolemia, and hemodilution has evolved. Current evidence supports induced hypertension as the most effective component. I use a vasopressor, typically phenylephrine or norepinephrine, to raise the systolic blood pressure to two hundred or the mean arterial pressure twenty percent above baseline. Euvolemia is preferred over hypervolemia, which has not been shown to improve outcomes and increases the risk of pulmonary edema and cardiac complications. If the patient does not improve with hemodynamic augmentation within one to two hours, I escalate to endovascular intervention. This includes intra-arterial verapamil or nicardipine infusion directly into the spastic vessel, or balloon angioplasty of proximal vessel spasm. Balloon angioplasty is more durable but carries a two to five percent risk of vessel rupture.

Examiner, and this is a trap: "Would you start a statin for vasospasm prophylaxis?"

Your answer: No. This is a common question. Early studies such as the STASH trial in 2014 randomized over eight hundred patients and showed no benefit of simvastatin in preventing DCI or improving outcomes after aneurysmal SAH. Statins are not part of the current guidelines for vasospasm prophylaxis. The only medication with class one evidence for improving outcomes is nimodipine, which I have already started. I would not start a statin specifically for vasospasm.

Examiner: "On day seven, the patient develops polyuria with six liters of urine output and a serum sodium of one twenty-eight. Is this SIADH or cerebral salt wasting?"

Your answer: This distinction is critical because the treatments are opposite. With six liters of urine output, this is cerebral salt wasting, not SIADH. Both cause hyponatremia, but the key differentiator is volume status. Cerebral salt wasting presents with hyponatremia, high urine output, high urine sodium concentration, and a contracted intravascular volume. The patient is dry. SIADH presents with hyponatremia, concentrated urine, and a euvolemic or mildly hypervolemic state with low urine output. In the SAH population, cerebral salt wasting is more common than SIADH. My treatment for cerebral salt wasting is aggressive volume replacement with normal saline and addition of hypertonic saline if needed. I may add fludrocortisone 0.1 milligrams twice daily to promote sodium retention. I do NOT fluid-restrict this patient, which is what I would do for SIADH. Fluid restriction in a vasospasm-prone SAH patient with cerebral salt wasting would be dangerous because it would worsen the hypovolemia and increase the risk of DCI.

Examiner: "Her ventriculostomy has been draining an average of fifteen milliliters per hour and you've been unable to wean it. What do you do?"

Your answer: This patient likely needs permanent CSF diversion. Approximately twenty to thirty percent of aneurysmal SAH patients develop chronic hydrocephalus requiring a shunt. Risk factors include higher Fisher grade, intraventricular hemorrhage, older age, and posterior circulation hemorrhage. I would perform a gradual EVD weaning trial by raising the drain level progressively from ten to fifteen to twenty centimeters of water over forty-eight to seventy-two hours, monitoring the ICP and clinical exam at each level. If the ICP remains elevated above twenty or the patient becomes clinically worse when the drain is raised, the trial has failed. I would then proceed with placement of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. I prefer a programmable valve to allow noninvasive pressure adjustment postoperatively.

Now let's review the evidence and guidelines.

The landmark trials you must know for aneurysmal SAH are as follows.

The ISAT trial, the International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial, published in 2002, randomized 2,143 patients with ruptured aneurysms suitable for either clipping or coiling. At one year, coiling resulted in a 7.4 percent absolute reduction in death or dependency compared with clipping. Long-term follow-up at eighteen years showed that this advantage was maintained, though rebleeding and retreatment were more common in the coiling group. The key limitation is that ISAT only enrolled patients in whom equipoise existed between the two treatments, which was about one in five screened patients.

The BRAT trial, the Barrow Ruptured Aneurysm Trial, was a single-center trial that randomized all patients to coiling or clipping regardless of aneurysm anatomy, with crossover permitted. At six years, outcomes were similar between groups for anterior circulation aneurysms but significantly better with coiling for posterior circulation aneurysms. This trial is more pragmatic than ISAT but limited by its single-center design.

The STASH trial, published in 2014, randomized 803 patients to simvastatin forty milligrams versus placebo after aneurysmal SAH. There was no benefit in the primary outcome of modified Rankin scale at six months, no reduction in vasospasm, and no reduction in DCI. Statins are not recommended for SAH.

The CONSCIOUS trials tested clazosentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist, for vasospasm prevention. CONSCIOUS-1 showed a reduction in angiographic vasospasm but CONSCIOUS-2 and 3 showed no improvement in clinical outcomes. This drug is not standard of care.

The nimodipine evidence comes from the 1989 British aneurysm nimodipine trial showing a one-third reduction in poor outcomes. Nimodipine sixty milligrams orally every four hours for twenty-one days remains the standard. It does not prevent angiographic vasospasm but improves outcomes, likely through neuroprotection.

Current AHA/ASA guidelines from 2023 recommend securing the aneurysm within twenty-four hours when feasible, nimodipine for all patients, euvolemia rather than hypervolemia, and induced hypertension for symptomatic vasospasm.

Now for the board pearls.

Remember: the rebleeding rate in the first twenty-four hours is four percent, and rebleeding carries a mortality rate of seventy percent. This is why securing the aneurysm early is critical.

Never say "triple-H therapy" without qualification. The examiner will probe whether you understand that hypervolemia and hemodilution have fallen out of favor. Instead say "hemodynamic augmentation with induced hypertension and maintenance of euvolemia."

If they ask about a negative CT head in a patient with thunderclap headache, they are testing whether you know the sensitivity of CT falls with time. CT is roughly ninety-eight percent sensitive in the first six hours, dropping to ninety-three percent at twelve hours and roughly eighty percent at twenty-four hours. If the CT is negative and clinical suspicion is high, you must perform a lumbar puncture. Look for xanthochromia, which takes six to twelve hours to develop. If both CT and LP are negative, no further workup is needed for SAH, though you may consider CTA for an incidental aneurysm screen depending on the clinical scenario.

If they show you a CTA with multiple aneurysms and ask which one bled, remember: the ruptured aneurysm is usually the largest one, the one with an irregular or lobulated dome, the one located where the thickest clot is on CT, or the one with a focal nipple sign suggesting a recent bleed point.

The three things that will fail you on this topic. One: not knowing the difference between cerebral salt wasting and SIADH and treating them incorrectly. Two: recommending hypervolemia or triple-H therapy without acknowledging current evidence favoring induced hypertension alone with euvolemia. Three: failing to obtain proximal vascular control before aneurysm dissection, or panicking during an intraoperative rupture.

That covers aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. You are ready for this one.
