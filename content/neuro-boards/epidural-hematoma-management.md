Welcome to this oral board preparation session on epidural hematoma management. This is one of the most classic and high-yield neurosurgical emergencies you will encounter on the ABNS oral boards, and it is a topic where decisiveness and precision in your answers will distinguish a passing performance from a failing one. We are going to work through this systematically, starting with the foundational anatomy and pathophysiology, moving into a clinical case, and then drilling into the examiner-style interrogation that will test every edge of your knowledge.

Let us begin with the foundation. An epidural hematoma, abbreviated EDH, is a collection of blood between the inner table of the skull and the dura mater. It accounts for one to four percent of all traumatic brain injuries. There is a bimodal age distribution, with a peak incidence in the twenty to thirty year age range. EDH is notably rare at the extremes of age. In the elderly, the dura is tightly adherent to the inner table of the skull, making it difficult for blood to strip the dura away and create that epidural space. In infants under two years of age, the dura is similarly adherent, and the meningeal arteries are less developed, making arterial EDH uncommon in that population.

The most common etiology is a temporal bone fracture that crosses the middle meningeal artery. This accounts for sixty to ninety percent of all epidural hematomas. However, you must also know the other sources. Meningeal veins can be torn. Diploic veins within the bone itself can bleed into the epidural space. And critically, the dural venous sinuses can be the source, particularly in posterior fossa epidural hematomas where the transverse or sigmoid sinus is injured by an occipital bone fracture.

The pathophysiology is straightforward but lethal. Arterial bleeding under systemic pressure strips the dura away from the inner table of the skull. The blood accumulates as an expanding biconvex collection. When this occurs in the temporal region, the expanding mass pushes the medial temporal lobe, specifically the uncus, over the edge of the tentorium. This produces uncal herniation with compression of the ipsilateral third cranial nerve, followed by compression of the cerebral peduncle, and ultimately brainstem compression and death if untreated.

Now, the classic clinical presentation is something every medical student learns, but it is critical that you understand its limitations. The textbook sequence is a brief loss of consciousness at the time of impact, followed by a lucid interval where the patient appears to recover and is conversant, followed by rapid deterioration with contralateral hemiparesis from cerebral peduncle compression and an ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil from third nerve compression by the herniating uncus. However, this classic lucid interval is present in only ten to thirty percent of patients with epidural hematomas. On the oral boards, do not anchor yourself to this presentation. Many patients will present in coma from the outset. Others will have a more gradual decline. The examiner may try to steer you away from the diagnosis by presenting an atypical picture.

On CT imaging, the epidural hematoma has a characteristic biconvex or lenticular shape. It is hyperdense on non-contrast CT. Critically, it does not cross suture lines because the dura is adherent to the skull at the suture lines. This distinguishes it from a subdural hematoma, which freely crosses sutures. The most common location is temporal or temporoparietal. One exception to the suture line rule is the vertex epidural hematoma, which arises from injury to the superior sagittal sinus and can cross the midline at the vertex. One additional CT finding to know is the swirl sign, which refers to heterogeneous density within the hematoma with areas of hypodensity mixed into the hyperdense clot. This suggests active, ongoing bleeding and should increase your urgency.

With that foundation in place, let us move to the clinical case. A twenty-two-year-old male is struck by a baseball on the left temple during a recreational game. He had a brief loss of consciousness witnessed at the scene. He regained consciousness and was initially conversant, but by the time EMS arrives, his Glasgow Coma Scale is thirteen, broken down as E3 V4 M6, and he is complaining of progressively worsening headache. On examination, there is a left temporal scalp hematoma. His right pupil is three millimeters and reactive. His left pupil is five millimeters and sluggishly reactive. He has no motor deficit at this time. A CT head is obtained and demonstrates a left temporoparietal biconvex hyperdensity measuring eighteen millimeters in maximum thickness with seven millimeters of left-to-right midline shift. There is a linear temporal bone fracture underlying the collection.

Now, let us move into the examiner interrogation phase. This is where the oral boards are won or lost.

The examiner states, this patient needs emergent surgery. Your answer is yes, that is correct. This patient meets multiple criteria for emergent surgical evacuation. The hematoma thickness exceeds fifteen millimeters. The midline shift exceeds five millimeters. The patient has demonstrated GCS deterioration from a lucid interval, which is itself a surgical indication. And there is an ipsilateral pupil that is dilating and becoming sluggish, which represents early uncal herniation. Any one of these criteria alone would be sufficient to warrant surgery. This patient has all four.

The examiner then asks you to recite the surgical criteria. You should know these cold. According to the Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines, specifically the Bullock 2006 guidelines for surgical management of epidural hematomas, the indications for operative evacuation are as follows. First, an EDH greater than fifteen millimeters in maximum thickness. Second, midline shift greater than five millimeters. Third, a GCS of eight or less with focal neurological deficit. Fourth, neurological deterioration, meaning a declining GCS. Any single one of these criteria is sufficient to mandate surgery. You do not need to meet multiple criteria.

The examiner then flips the question and asks about non-operative management. When can you observe an epidural hematoma? The answer is that conservative management is appropriate when all of the following conditions are met simultaneously. The hematoma must be less than fifteen millimeters in maximum thickness. Midline shift must be less than five millimeters. The GCS must be greater than eight. And there must be no focal neurological deficit. If all of these conditions are satisfied, you may observe the patient, but observation has strict requirements. The patient must be admitted to a neurointensive care unit. Serial neurological examinations must be performed every one hour. A repeat CT scan is mandatory at six to eight hours after the initial scan and with any clinical change whatsoever. And the institution must have the capability to take the patient emergently to the operating room within thirty minutes of any deterioration. If your facility cannot provide this level of monitoring, the patient should be transferred or taken to surgery.

The examiner now asks you to describe your operative technique. You position the patient supine with the head turned to expose the ipsilateral temporal region. You perform a temporal craniotomy centered on the hematoma. For a large EDH, you use a question-mark or trauma flap, which provides broad exposure and the ability to convert to a decompressive craniectomy if needed. After the bone flap is elevated, you evacuate the clot. You identify and coagulate the middle meningeal artery. If the bleeding point is at the foramen spinosum and cannot be controlled with bipolar cautery, you can drill out the foramen spinosum and pack it with bone wax, or you can cauterize the artery directly at the skull base. You then inspect the dura carefully for any dural tears. After evacuation, you place tack-up sutures, also called dural hitching sutures. These are sutures that pass through drill holes in the bone flap and anchor the dura to the periosteum on the outer surface of the bone, obliterating the epidural space. This step is critical to prevent reaccumulation of the hematoma. You close in layers. Some surgeons will place a subdural drain at the end of the procedure. If the patient is actively herniating and there is no operating room immediately available, you can place an emergent burr hole at the pterion as a temporizing measure. This is not definitive treatment, but it can be life-saving by releasing enough pressure to interrupt the herniation cascade while the patient is being transported to definitive surgical care.

The examiner now pushes on timing and outcomes. You should state clearly that epidural hematoma is a time-critical surgical emergency. The outcome correlates directly with two variables: the preoperative GCS at the time of surgery and the elapsed time from onset of deterioration to surgical evacuation. Patients taken to surgery with a GCS greater than eight have a mortality of less than five percent. Patients with a GCS less than eight and pupillary dilation have a mortality of twenty to forty percent. Patients with bilateral fixed and dilated pupils have a mortality approaching ninety percent. However, and this is a key board point, bilateral fixed dilated pupils are not universally fatal. You should still operate urgently on these patients because a subset will survive with functional outcomes. Never declare a patient unsalvageable based on pupil exam alone.

The examiner now shifts to posterior fossa epidural hematomas. This is a critical subtopic. Posterior fossa EDH accounts for approximately three percent of all epidural hematomas. The typical source is injury to the transverse or sigmoid venous sinus, usually from an occipital bone fracture. The reason posterior fossa EDH is so dangerous is that the posterior fossa is a small, tightly enclosed compartment. Even a relatively small hematoma can produce rapid brainstem compression, obstructive hydrocephalus from compression of the fourth ventricle, and tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum. The surgical threshold is therefore much lower than for supratentorial EDH. The indications for surgery in posterior fossa EDH are any symptomatic posterior fossa EDH regardless of size, any posterior fossa EDH with thickness greater than fifteen millimeters, and any evidence of hydrocephalus or brainstem compression on imaging. Even a posterior fossa EDH that appears small on CT can be lethal, and you should have a very low threshold to operate. The surgical approach is a suboccipital craniotomy or craniectomy. If the venous sinus is the source of bleeding, repair can be challenging. You may use hemostatic agents, a muscle patch, or direct suture repair. The key point is that you should avoid ligating a patent transverse sinus, especially if it is the dominant side, because this can produce venous infarction of the cerebellum and catastrophic swelling. If you are unsure of dominance, assume it is dominant and preserve it.

The examiner then raises vertex epidural hematoma. This is a less common but highly testable variant. Vertex EDH arises from injury to the superior sagittal sinus. It can present with bilateral lower extremity weakness due to compression of the parasagittal motor cortex, which represents the leg area of the motor homunculus. Imaging shows a biconvex collection at the vertex that may cross the midline because the sagittal sinus straddles the midline and the falx does not prevent epidural blood from crossing at the vertex. Surgical treatment may require a bilateral parasagittal craniotomy to access the hematoma on both sides of the sinus. Because the source is venous, these hematomas can reaccumulate, and vigilant postoperative monitoring is essential.

Now let us address the trap questions. These are designed to catch you off guard, and they are exactly the kind of curveballs the oral board examiners will throw.

First trap question. A nine-month-old infant presents with a large left epidural hematoma. Does this change your approach? The answer is yes, significantly. Infants have a very small total blood volume, approximately eighty milliliters per kilogram. A large epidural hematoma in an infant can represent a hemodynamically significant proportion of their total circulating volume. You must type and crossmatch the patient, ensure adequate large-bore intravenous access, and be fully prepared for massive transfusion before and during surgery. Anemia and hemodynamic instability may actually be the presenting signs of the EDH in an infant, rather than the classic neurological deterioration you would expect in an adult. Additionally, whenever an infant presents with an epidural hematoma, you must consider non-accidental trauma. The mechanism should be carefully scrutinized, a skeletal survey should be obtained, retinal examination should be performed, and social work and child protective services should be involved early.

Second trap question. The patient has a small epidural hematoma, eight millimeters thick, no midline shift, and a GCS of fifteen. When do you repeat imaging? The answer is six to eight hours after the initial CT, and immediately with any clinical change. This is because the epidural hematoma you see on the first CT may not represent its final size. Ten to thirty percent of conservatively managed epidural hematomas will expand on subsequent imaging. An EDH can grow rapidly, particularly if the source is arterial, and a patient who appears stable with a small hematoma can deteriorate precipitously. Serial imaging is non-negotiable in the conservative management of EDH.

Third trap question. You perform a craniotomy and successfully evacuate the epidural hematoma, but the brain remains swollen and does not relax as expected. You recall that there was a contralateral thin subdural collection on the preoperative CT. What do you do? This scenario is a classic board trap. The lesson is twofold. First, you must always scrutinize the preoperative CT carefully for contralateral pathology before surgery. Do not become fixated on the obvious lesion and miss a second one. Second, intraoperatively, if the brain remains swollen after a technically adequate EDH evacuation, you must suspect a contralateral mass lesion as the cause. This may require a second craniotomy on the contralateral side to evacuate the subdural hematoma. This is why, when positioning the patient, you should position them to allow bilateral access when possible, typically supine with the head on a horseshoe or Mayfield and the ability to turn to either side or access both sides.

Let us now address the evidence base. The cornerstone reference is the Bullock 2006 Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines for the surgical management of acute epidural hematomas. These provide the thickness and midline shift criteria we discussed. The Cheung systematic review examined outcomes of conservative management and established the safety parameters for non-operative observation, confirming that careful patient selection with serial imaging yields good outcomes in appropriately selected patients. The Bricolo outcome data provide the mortality statistics stratified by preoperative GCS that you should know for the boards. For posterior fossa EDH specifically, the series by Otsuka and Bozbuga established the lower surgical threshold and outlined the unique dangers of this anatomic location.

Let us close with the board pearls you need to have at instant recall. Epidural hematoma is biconvex on CT and does not cross suture lines. Greater than fifteen millimeters thickness or greater than five millimeters of midline shift equals operate. Posterior fossa epidural hematoma has a low surgical threshold because the posterior fossa is a small compartment and even small hematomas can be lethal. The lucid interval is present in a minority of patients, only ten to thirty percent, so do not rely on its presence or absence to make the diagnosis. The swirl sign on CT indicates active bleeding. Infants can exsanguinate into an epidural hematoma. Always look for contralateral pathology before and during surgery. Time to surgery directly determines outcome. And bilateral fixed dilated pupils are not a contraindication to surgery.

That covers epidural hematoma management. You are ready for this one.
