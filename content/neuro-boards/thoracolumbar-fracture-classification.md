Welcome back. Today we are covering thoracolumbar fracture classification, a topic that appears on nearly every oral board examination. By the end of this session you will be able to classify any thoracolumbar fracture using the AO Spine system, score it with TLICS, determine operative versus non-operative management, select the correct surgical approach, and handle the examiner trap questions that separate a pass from a fail. Let us begin.

We start with the foundation, and that means understanding why the thoracolumbar junction is the most commonly fractured segment of the spine. The thoracolumbar junction spans from T11 to L2, and it represents the transition from the rigid, kyphotic thoracic spine, which is stabilized by the rib cage, to the mobile, lordotic lumbar spine. This transition creates a biomechanical stress riser. The thoracic spine is constrained by costovertebral articulations, the sternum, and the rib cage itself. Once you pass below the last rib-bearing vertebra, those constraints vanish. Axial loads, flexion moments, and rotational forces concentrate at this junction. That is why roughly 60 percent of all thoracolumbar fractures occur between T11 and L2.

Before we get to the modern AO Spine classification, you must understand the Denis three-column model because examiners still reference it and because it underpins the concept of spinal stability. Denis divided the spine into three columns. The anterior column consists of the anterior longitudinal ligament and the anterior half of the vertebral body including the anterior annulus. The middle column consists of the posterior half of the vertebral body, the posterior annulus, and the posterior longitudinal ligament. The posterior column consists of the pedicles, laminae, facet joints, facet capsules, spinous processes, and the posterior ligamentous complex, which includes the supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, and facet capsules. Denis proposed that disruption of two or more columns constitutes instability. The middle column is the key discriminator. A simple compression fracture involves only the anterior column and is stable. A burst fracture involves both the anterior and middle columns and may be unstable. A fracture-dislocation involves all three columns and is always unstable.

Now let us move to the current standard, the AO Spine Thoracolumbar Injury Classification System. This system classifies fractures into three main types based on injury morphology: Type A, compression injuries; Type B, distraction injuries; and Type C, translation or rotation injuries. Within each type there are subtypes, and you must know every single one.

Type A, compression injuries. A0 is a minor, non-structural fracture such as a transverse process fracture, spinous process fracture, or isolated lamina fracture. These are mechanically insignificant. A1 is a wedge compression or endplate impaction fracture involving a single endplate, without involvement of the posterior wall of the vertebral body. A2 is a split or coronal plane fracture, meaning a fracture through the vertebral body in the coronal plane, sometimes called a pincer fracture, involving both endplates but without posterior wall involvement. A3 is an incomplete burst fracture, where there is involvement of the posterior wall of the vertebral body, meaning the middle column is disrupted, but only one endplate is involved. A4 is a complete burst fracture with posterior wall involvement and both endplates are affected. The key distinction between A3 and A4 is whether one or both endplates are compromised.

Type B, distraction injuries. These involve failure under tension in either the posterior or anterior elements. B1 is a bony chance fracture, a transverse fracture extending through the posterior elements and vertebral body in a horizontal plane. This is a bony failure in distraction, classically from a lap-belt injury. B2 is a posterior ligamentous disruption, also called a posterior tension band injury. Here the posterior ligamentous complex has failed, and there is distraction through ligamentous structures posteriorly, often associated with an anterior compression or burst component. B3 is an anterior disruption through the disc or an anterior hyperextension injury. In B3 the anterior column fails in distraction, typically seen in ankylosing spondylitis or diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis where a hyperextension mechanism causes failure through an already fused anterior column.

Type C, translation or rotation injuries. These are the most severe. Type C indicates displacement or dislocation of one vertebral body relative to the adjacent one in any plane. Any fracture-dislocation is Type C. These injuries, by definition, involve disruption of all three columns and are always unstable.

The AO system also uses modifiers. M1 indicates an indeterminate posterior ligamentous complex status, meaning imaging is equivocal. M2 is a patient-specific modifier for conditions that may affect treatment, such as ankylosing spondylitis, diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis, or severe osteoporosis. The neurological modifier is critically important. N0 means neurologically intact. N1 indicates a transient neurological deficit that has resolved. N2 indicates radiculopathy, a nerve root deficit. N3 indicates an incomplete spinal cord injury or conus medullaris injury. N4 indicates a complete spinal cord injury. Nx means the neurological status is indeterminate, for example in an intubated, sedated patient.

Now we move to the Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score, known as TLICS. This is your decision-making algorithm. It assigns points in three categories, and the total score determines management.

The first category is injury morphology. A compression fracture receives 1 point. A burst fracture receives 2 points. Translation or rotation receives 3 points. Distraction receives 4 points. Note that distraction scores highest because posterior ligamentous complex disruption is the strongest predictor of progressive deformity.

The second category is posterior ligamentous complex integrity. If the PLC is intact, the score is 0. If the PLC is suspected or indeterminate, the score is 2. If the PLC is clearly injured, the score is 3. MRI is the best imaging modality for assessing PLC integrity. On MRI you look for high signal in the interspinous and supraspinous ligaments on T2 or STIR sequences, widening of the interspinous space, and facet joint disruption.

The third category is neurological status. If the patient is neurologically intact, the score is 0. If there is a nerve root injury or radiculopathy, the score is 2. If there is an incomplete spinal cord or conus medullaris injury, the score is 3. If there is a complete spinal cord or conus medullaris injury, the score is 2. If there is a cauda equina syndrome, the score is 3. Pay close attention here because this is a common point of confusion on boards. A complete cord injury scores only 2, not 3, because surgical decompression is unlikely to improve neurological outcome in a complete injury. An incomplete injury scores 3 because there is neural tissue at risk that may benefit from intervention.

Score interpretation is straightforward. A total TLICS score of 3 or less favors non-operative management. A score of exactly 4 is indeterminate and left to surgeon judgment. A score of 5 or greater indicates surgical management. Memorize these cutoffs.

Let us now apply everything to a clinical case, exactly as you would encounter it on the oral boards.

A 28-year-old male presents after a high-speed motor vehicle collision. He reports severe back pain. On examination he has bilateral lower extremity weakness graded at 3 out of 5 throughout, sensation is intact to light touch but proprioception is decreased in both feet. Rectal tone is present but diminished. CT scan of the thoracolumbar spine demonstrates an L1 burst fracture with approximately 50 percent canal compromise. There is widening of the interspinous distance at L1-L2. MRI of the lumbar spine confirms disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex at L1-L2, with high signal through the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments, and demonstrates compression of the conus medullaris by retropulsed bone from the L1 vertebral body. Full spine CT screening reveals no additional fractures. Take a moment to classify this fracture and calculate the TLICS score before I walk you through it.

Now let us move into the examiner interrogation phase, where I will walk through this case the way a board examiner would grill you.

The examiner asks you to calculate the TLICS score. Morphology is burst fracture, which is 2 points. Posterior ligamentous complex is injured, confirmed on MRI, which is 3 points. Neurological status is incomplete spinal cord injury, specifically a conus medullaris syndrome with bilateral weakness and proprioceptive loss, which is 3 points. Total TLICS score is 2 plus 3 plus 3, which equals 8. That is well above the surgical threshold of 5. This patient requires operative intervention.

The examiner then asks for the AO classification. The fracture is a complete burst with both endplates involved, so it is A4. There is posterior ligamentous disruption, so we add B2. The neurological status is incomplete cord or conus injury, so the modifier is N3. The complete AO classification is A4 B2 N3.

The examiner asks about your imaging workup. You should have obtained a CT scan of the full thoracolumbar spine with sagittal and coronal reformats, which is essential for characterizing fracture morphology and measuring canal compromise. MRI of the affected segment is critical for evaluating the posterior ligamentous complex and the neural elements. You must screen the entire spine because non-contiguous fractures are present in 5 to 20 percent of thoracolumbar trauma patients. CT angiography may be needed if there is concern for vertebral artery injury, though that is more relevant in cervical trauma.

The examiner asks about your surgical approach. For this patient, the primary approach is posterior short-segment pedicle screw fixation. The standard construct is one level above and one level below the fracture, meaning pedicle screws at T12 and L2. However, at the thoracolumbar junction, short-segment constructs have a higher failure rate due to the biomechanical stress at this transition zone. You can improve construct strength by adding index-level screws, meaning pedicle screws placed into the fractured L1 vertebral body itself. Mahar's biomechanical study demonstrated that adding index-level screws significantly increases stiffness, and Guven's clinical study showed lower failure rates with this technique. Alternatively, you can extend the construct to two levels above and two levels below, meaning T11 through L3, but this sacrifices more motion segments.

Direct decompression via laminectomy is indicated in this case because there is 50 percent canal compromise with neural compression on MRI and the patient has an incomplete neurological deficit. Indirect reduction through ligamentotaxis, where distraction across the segment pulls the retropulsed fragment anteriorly, can sometimes reduce canal compromise, but with 50 percent canal stenosis and a neurological deficit, you should plan for direct decompression.

When would you consider an anterior approach? An anterior approach or a combined anterior-posterior approach is considered when there is significant anterior column deficiency, such as a comminuted vertebral body that cannot support load even after posterior stabilization. It is also considered when posterior reduction fails to adequately reduce retropulsed bone out of the canal, or when there is a need for anterior column reconstruction with a cage or strut graft. For this patient, if posterior decompression and fixation achieve adequate canal clearance and alignment, an anterior approach is not necessary. But if postoperative imaging shows persistent anterior column deficiency with risk of progressive kyphosis, a staged anterior procedure with vertebral body replacement may be needed.

The examiner asks about timing of surgery. This is a critical point. For incomplete spinal cord injuries, early surgical decompression, defined as within 24 hours of injury, improves neurological outcomes. The landmark study here is STASCIS, the Surgical Timing in Acute Spinal Cord Injury Study, published by Fehlings and colleagues. STASCIS demonstrated that patients who underwent decompression within 24 hours had a significantly higher rate of at least a 2-grade improvement on the ASIA Impairment Scale at 6-month follow-up compared to those who underwent late surgery. The more recent SCI-POEM trial, a prospective multicenter study, further supported the benefit of early decompression, particularly for incomplete injuries. For this patient with an incomplete conus injury, you should proceed to surgery as soon as medically feasible, ideally within 24 hours.

The examiner asks about non-operative management. When is it appropriate? Non-operative treatment is appropriate when the TLICS score is 3 or less. The typical protocol is a thoracolumbosacral orthosis, a TLSO brace, worn for 8 to 12 weeks, with serial upright radiographs at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks to monitor for progressive kyphosis or loss of height. Weight-bearing is allowed as tolerated. If there is progressive deformity of more than 10 degrees on serial imaging, surgical intervention should be reconsidered.

The examiner asks about cement augmentation. Vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty are used for osteoporotic compression fractures. The evidence is mixed and this is a common board topic. The VERTOS II randomized controlled trial, published in the Lancet in 2010, compared vertebroplasty to conservative management and showed significant pain reduction at 1 month and at 1 year in the vertebroplasty group. However, two landmark sham-controlled trials published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009, the Buchbinder trial and the Kallmes trial, compared vertebroplasty to a sham procedure and found no significant difference in pain relief. This created significant controversy. The resolution, as far as board examiners are concerned, is that you should know both sides. Vertebroplasty may have a role in carefully selected patients with acute, painful osteoporotic compression fractures who have failed conservative management, but the level 1 evidence from sham-controlled trials questions its efficacy. Kyphoplasty adds the theoretical advantage of height restoration through balloon inflation before cement injection, but similar concerns about placebo effect apply.

Now here come the trap questions.

The examiner says, "A patient presents with a complete T6 spinal cord injury and an L1 burst fracture. Would you rush to surgery?" This is a trap. A complete spinal cord injury scores only 2 points for neurological status on TLICS, not 3. The reason is that complete injuries have an extremely low rate of neurological recovery regardless of intervention. The urgency for surgery in incomplete injuries is driven by the potential to salvage neural function. In a complete injury, there is no neural tissue to save. That said, surgery may still be indicated for mechanical stability. You calculate the full TLICS score. If the burst fracture has associated PLC disruption, the total may still reach 5 or above, and surgery is indicated for stabilization, but the timing is less critical than for an incomplete injury. You do not rush a complete injury to the operating room at 2 in the morning. You optimize the patient and take them in the next available elective slot.

The examiner asks, "Would you use a TLSO brace for an L1 burst fracture in a neurologically intact patient with intact PLC?" Calculate the TLICS. Burst fracture morphology is 2 points. PLC intact is 0 points. Neurologically intact is 0 points. Total TLICS is 2. That is clearly in the non-operative range. Non-operative management with a TLSO brace for 8 to 12 weeks is entirely appropriate. Multiple studies, including the Thomas Jefferson University series, have demonstrated acceptable outcomes with non-operative management of neurologically intact burst fractures with intact posterior ligamentous complex. The key is serial imaging to ensure no progressive deformity.

The examiner asks, "Short segment versus long segment fixation?" Short-segment fixation, one above and one below, preserves more motion segments and is biomechanically adequate for many fractures. However, at the thoracolumbar junction, short-segment posterior-only constructs have a reported failure rate of 9 to 54 percent, manifested as screw pullout, progressive kyphosis, or hardware failure. This is because the long moment arm at the thoracolumbar junction places significant stress on a short construct. Strategies to mitigate this include adding index-level pedicle screws at the fractured vertebra, as demonstrated by Mahar's biomechanical study showing increased construct stiffness by approximately 70 percent, and confirmed clinically by Guven and colleagues who showed significantly lower failure rates with index-level screws. Another strategy is extending to a two-above, two-below construct, which sacrifices motion segments but provides greater stability. The choice depends on fracture severity, bone quality, and the degree of comminution. In general, for a young patient with good bone quality and a straightforward burst fracture, short-segment fixation with index-level screws is a sound strategy.

Let us now review the key evidence you need to cite on boards. TLICS was developed by the Spine Trauma Study Group and validated in multiple studies showing good inter-observer reliability and correlation with surgical decision-making. The AO Spine classification system has been validated in reliability studies demonstrating moderate to substantial inter-observer agreement, superior to the older Denis classification and the Magerl system. STASCIS, published by Fehlings and colleagues in 2012 in PLoS One, is the definitive study supporting early decompression for acute spinal cord injury within 24 hours. SCI-POEM, a prospective multicenter observational study, provided additional evidence supporting early surgery for incomplete SCI. The vertebroplasty controversy centers on VERTOS II showing benefit versus the two 2009 New England Journal of Medicine sham-controlled trials by Buchbinder and Kallmes showing no benefit over placebo. Know these studies by name and their conclusions.

Let us close with the board pearls you must have at your fingertips. TLICS cutoffs: 3 or less is non-operative, 4 is indeterminate, 5 or greater is operative. AO Spine types: A is compression, B is distraction, C is translation or rotation. Within Type A, remember A0 through A4 progress from minor non-structural fractures to complete burst fractures. Within Type B, B1 is bony chance, B2 is posterior ligamentous disruption, B3 is anterior disruption. TLICS neurological scoring: incomplete cord injury scores 3, complete cord injury scores only 2, and that is a classic board trap. PLC integrity is best assessed on MRI. Non-contiguous fractures occur in 5 to 20 percent of cases, so screen the entire spine. Early surgery within 24 hours for incomplete SCI, citing STASCIS and SCI-POEM. Short-segment constructs at the thoracolumbar junction benefit from index-level screws. Cement augmentation for osteoporotic fractures remains controversial given conflicting level 1 evidence.

That covers thoracolumbar fracture classification. You are ready for this one.
