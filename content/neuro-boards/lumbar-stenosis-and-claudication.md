questions:
  - q: Neurogenic or vascular claudication? Separate them.
    a: Neurogenic claudication is relieved by flexion and positional change rather than simply by stopping — the shopping cart sign — and walking uphill or cycling is better tolerated than walking downhill. Vascular claudication is relieved by stopping regardless of posture, with absent pulses and trophic changes.
    probe: What is your screening test for the vascular cause?
    probeA: The ankle-brachial index, with a value below zero point nine indicating peripheral arterial disease. It is cheap, quick and non-invasive, and it is the single test that stops me decompressing a spine in a patient whose legs hurt because of their arteries. I would combine it with examining the peripheral pulses and looking for trophic changes, hair loss and delayed capillary refill, and I would obtain formal arterial duplex if the index is abnormal or if it is falsely elevated by calcified incompressible vessels in a diabetic, where toe-brachial index is more reliable.
  - q: What ankle-brachial index confirms peripheral vascular disease?
    a: Less than zero point nine. It is the screening test, it is cheap, and it prevents you from decompressing a spine in a patient whose legs hurt because of their arteries.
    probe: What if both are present?
    probeA: Then I treat the one that is dominant and reversible first, and I say so explicitly rather than pretending the question has a single answer. Coexistence is common, because both are diseases of the same elderly, smoking, diabetic population. I would obtain vascular surgical input, and if there is critical limb ischaemia or a short-distance vascular claudication, revascularisation usually comes first, because it is lower risk and because improving perfusion may relieve enough of the symptom burden to change the calculus. If the history is clearly postural with a shopping cart sign and the vascular disease is mild, I would decompress. I would also counsel the patient that treating one may unmask the other, so neither of us should expect a complete resolution from a single operation.
  - q: Describe the shopping cart sign as a patient would.
    a: He can walk the whole supermarket leaning on the trolley without symptoms, but walking across the car park without it is limited to fifty yards. The flexed posture increases canal diameter; extension narrows it. It is close to pathognomonic.
    probe: Which activity would you expect him to tolerate well?
    probeA: Cycling, particularly on a bicycle where he is leaning forward over the handlebars, which puts the lumbar spine in flexion and opens the canal. Walking uphill is also better tolerated than walking downhill, for the same reason: going uphill flexes the trunk, whereas going downhill extends it. Pushing a trolley or a walking frame, leaning on a supermarket counter, and sitting all relieve symptoms quickly. That constellation is what makes the history positional rather than exertional, which is the distinction from vascular claudication, where the patient is limited by a fixed walking distance regardless of posture and relieved simply by standing still.
  - q: What do epidural injections actually achieve here?
    a: Modest short-term benefit. The LESS trial, Friedly, NEJM 2014, found epidural glucocorticoid plus lidocaine gave minimal or no benefit over lidocaine alone at six weeks, and the wider literature supports only modest short-term relief. They are reasonable for symptom control and can have diagnostic value, but they are not a durable solution and I would say so honestly.
    probe: Would a good response predict a good surgical outcome?
    probeA: Not reliably, no, and I would be careful here because it is an intuitive belief that the evidence does not support well. A good response to an injection tells me the pain is probably arising from the level injected, which has some diagnostic value, particularly in a multilevel degenerate spine where I am trying to identify the symptomatic level. But there is no strong evidence that the magnitude or duration of response to an epidural steroid injection predicts the outcome of decompression, and some data suggest prior injections are associated with slightly worse surgical outcomes and higher infection risk if given close to surgery. So I use it for symptom control and, cautiously, for level localisation, not as a surgical prognostic test.
  - q: State your surgical indications.
    a: Failure of adequate conservative management for three to six months, progressive neurological deficit at any time, and cauda equina syndrome, which is an emergency. Pain severity alone without a trial is not an indication.
    probe: What does SPORT say about the benefit of surgery?
    probeA: That surgery is better than continued non-operative care for symptomatic lumbar stenosis, but with an important caveat about how you read it. The intention-to-treat analysis was heavily diluted by crossover in both directions, so it understated the treatment effect. The as-treated analysis at four years showed significantly greater improvement in bodily pain, physical function and Oswestry Disability Index in the surgical group, and that advantage was maintained out to eight years in the longer follow-up. The honest framing is that surgery gives faster and greater improvement, that non-operative patients also improve to a degree, and that the difference narrows over time but does not disappear.
  - q: Summarise the SPORT stenosis data.
    a: The as-treated analysis at four years showed patients who underwent surgery had significantly greater improvement in bodily pain, physical function and Oswestry Disability Index than those treated conservatively. The intention-to-treat analysis was muddied by substantial crossover, which is the caveat to state.
    probe: Why does crossover matter so much in that trial?
    probeA: Because an intention-to-treat analysis compares the groups as randomised, so if a large proportion of patients assigned to non-operative care cross over and have surgery, and some assigned to surgery decline it, the two groups become contaminated with each other's treatment and the measured difference between them shrinks toward zero. In SPORT the crossover was substantial in both directions, so the intention-to-treat result underestimates the true effect of the operation. The counter-argument is that as-treated analysis breaks randomisation and reintroduces selection bias, since patients who chose to cross over to surgery may differ systematically. So neither analysis is clean, and the correct answer is to quote both and explain why they differ rather than cite whichever one suits.
  - q: Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis. Decompress alone?
    a: No — add fusion. The SLIP trial, Ghogawala, NEJM 2016, randomised exactly this population to decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion. The fusion group had significantly better SF-36 physical component scores — the primary outcome, measured at two years — with the benefit maintained at four. That is level one evidence.
    probe: What is the commonest error candidates make here?
    probeA: Recommending decompression alone. It is the classic error because it looks conservative and reasonable, and it ignores level one evidence from a trial that randomised precisely this population. The second error, which is subtler, is to quote the SLIP trial as if it licences fusion for every degenerative lumbar spine: it applies to Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis, and it should not be extended to stenosis without spondylolisthesis, where decompression alone remains correct and adding fusion adds morbidity for no benefit. I would also mention the Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study, which found no added benefit from fusion in its population, and explain that the discrepancy turns on whether a true spondylolisthesis with instability is present.
  - q: What criteria make you add fusion?
    a: Spondylolisthesis greater than Meyerding grade one; any grade with dynamic instability, meaning more than three millimetres of translation on flexion-extension films; the need for a wide decompression removing more than half the facets bilaterally; recurrent stenosis after prior decompression; and significant deformity or scoliosis.
    probe: How much facet can you take before the segment is unstable?
    probeA: The conventional biomechanical teaching is that removing more than fifty percent of both facet joints, or performing a complete unilateral facetectomy, renders the segment unstable and mandates fusion. In practice I would preserve at least half of each facet and the pars, and stay away from the pars interarticularis, which is the structure that fails if it is thinned. Where a wide decompression is needed, I would achieve it through a unilateral approach with over-the-top contralateral decompression, or with bilateral laminotomies, which preserves the midline structures and the facets far better than a wide laminectomy. And I would say that the fifty percent figure is a guide derived from cadaveric work rather than a precise threshold, and that preoperative instability and alignment matter as much as how much bone I remove.

    stem: A 68-year-old smoker with type 2 diabetes describes buttock and calf pain on walking for six months. He can push a supermarket trolley the length of the store but manages only 50 yards across the car park. He has had physiotherapy and two epidural injections with a few weeks of relief each. MRI shows severe central stenosis at L4-5 with a Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis.
    q1: What single investigation would you not skip, and why?
    a1: Ankle-brachial index. He is a diabetic smoker, so peripheral vascular disease is a real competing diagnosis, and an index below zero point nine confirms it. The shopping cart sign strongly favours neurogenic claudication, but the two can coexist and decompressing a spine in a patient whose legs hurt from their arteries is a preventable failure.
    p1: He is diabetic. Could the ABI be falsely normal?
    pa1: Yes, and that is an important caveat rather than a technicality. Long-standing diabetes causes medial arterial calcification, which makes the vessels incompressible, so the cuff cannot occlude them and the measured ankle pressure is falsely elevated. An index above about one point three is diagnostic of incompressible vessels, and a value like his of one point zero five in a diabetic smoker should be interpreted with caution rather than taken as definitive. The way round it is a toe-brachial index, since the digital vessels are usually spared from calcification, with a value below zero point seven abnormal. I would also use pulse volume waveforms or arterial duplex if the clinical suspicion remained, and I would examine for trophic changes and neuropathy rather than relying on a single number.
    q2: ABI is 1.05 and pulses are intact. Does he meet surgical indications?
    a2: Yes. He has failed adequate conservative management well beyond three to six months, including physiotherapy and injections which gave only the expected two to six weeks of benefit that the LESS trial data predict. SPORT's as-treated analysis at four years supports surgery over continued conservative care for exactly this picture.
    p2: Does he need more conservative treatment first?
    pa2: No. He has had six months of symptoms, structured physiotherapy and two epidural injections, each giving only the few weeks of benefit that the LESS trial and the wider literature would predict. That constitutes an adequate trial, and repeating it is not going to change anything except to consume more time. The guidance is failure of three to six months of conservative management, and he is beyond that. The only reason I would delay is to optimise him rather than to treat him conservatively again, meaning smoking cessation and glycaemic control before an instrumented fusion, and that is a modifiable-risk conversation rather than another therapeutic trial.
    q3: Decompression alone or decompression plus fusion? Commit and justify.
    a3: Decompression plus fusion. He has a Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis, which is precisely the population randomised in the SLIP trial, where the fusion group had significantly better SF-36 physical component scores at two years, maintained at four. Recommending decompression alone here is the classic error, because it ignores level one evidence.
    p3: What if there were no spondylolisthesis, just the stenosis?
    pa3: Then decompression alone, and adding fusion would be the error in that direction. Stenosis without instability or deformity does not benefit from fusion, and the evidence is reasonably consistent: the Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study found no added benefit from fusion in patients with or without a degenerative spondylolisthesis, and adding fusion in a patient who does not need it adds operative time, blood loss, hardware complications, adjacent segment disease and infection risk for no gain. So the distinction turns on whether there is a genuine spondylolisthesis with instability, not on the severity of the stenosis. I would perform a decompression preserving the facets and the pars, ideally through a unilateral approach with over-the-top contralateral decompression.
    q4: He is 68 with diabetes. How do you weigh the added morbidity of fusion?
    a4: Honestly, and as a genuine trade-off. Fusion adds operative time, blood loss, hardware complications, adjacent segment disease and a higher infection risk, all amplified by diabetes. I would optimise his glycaemic control and smoking preoperatively, because smoking substantially raises pseudarthrosis rates. But the evidence favours fusion for this specific pathology, and I would not withhold it on age alone at sixty-eight.
    p4: What is his actual risk of a wound infection, and can you lower it?
    pa4: In an instrumented lumbar fusion the baseline surgical site infection rate is roughly two to five percent, and diabetes and smoking each raise it independently, so he sits meaningfully above that. I can lower it: optimise the HbA1c preoperatively, with figures around eight percent commonly used as a threshold above which elective instrumented surgery is deferred; insist on smoking cessation for at least four weeks, which also substantially reduces his pseudarthrosis risk; treat any active infection; and manage perioperative glucose tightly, since intraoperative and postoperative hyperglycaemia predicts infection independently of the baseline HbA1c. Add standard measures: appropriate timing of prophylactic antibiotics, skin preparation, minimising operative time and tissue trauma, and consideration of intrawound vancomycin powder, which has supportive though not unanimous evidence.

Let's master lumbar stenosis and claudication for the oral boards.

Lumbar spinal stenosis is one of the most common conditions you will manage as a neurosurgeon and one of the most frequently tested spine topics on the oral boards. The examiner expects you to clearly distinguish neurogenic from vascular claudication, classify stenosis by anatomic zone, articulate surgical decision-making, and know when decompression alone is sufficient versus when fusion is required. You must know the SPORT trial data and be prepared for trap questions about degenerative spondylolisthesis. Let's build this from the pathophysiology up.

Start with the mechanism of canal narrowing. Lumbar stenosis is a degenerative process driven by three converging pathologies. First, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. The ligamentum flavum thickens with age due to loss of elastic fibers and replacement with collagen and fibrosis. Normal ligamentum flavum thickness is two to four millimeters. In stenosis, it can exceed six to eight millimeters, causing significant posterior compression of the thecal sac. Second, facet joint hypertrophy. The facet joints undergo osteoarthritic degeneration with capsular thickening, osteophyte formation, and synovial cyst development, all of which encroach on the canal from the posterolateral direction. Third, disc bulging and osteophyte formation anteriorly. As the disc desiccates and loses height, the annulus bulges posteriorly and endplate osteophytes form, compressing the thecal sac and nerve roots from the front. The combined effect of these three processes is circumferential narrowing of the spinal canal, lateral recesses, and neural foramina.

Now the three anatomic types of stenosis. You must classify these precisely.

Central stenosis is narrowing of the central spinal canal. The normal anteroposterior diameter of the lumbar spinal canal is fifteen to twenty-five millimeters. Relative stenosis is defined as an AP diameter less than twelve millimeters. Absolute stenosis is an AP diameter less than ten millimeters. Central stenosis compresses the cauda equina and produces the classic bilateral neurogenic claudication pattern. The cross-sectional area is also important: a dural sac cross-sectional area less than one hundred square millimeters on axial MRI is considered severe stenosis and correlates with symptom severity.

Lateral recess stenosis is narrowing of the lateral recess, the zone between the medial border of the pedicle and the posterolateral margin of the vertebral body. The lateral recess is bordered anteriorly by the posterior vertebral body and disc, laterally by the pedicle, and posteriorly by the superior articular facet. The Lee classification defines lateral recess height. Normal is greater than five millimeters. Three to five millimeters is suggestive of stenosis. Less than three millimeters is definitive stenosis. Lateral recess stenosis compresses the traversing nerve root, so at L4-5, the L5 root is affected.

Foraminal stenosis is narrowing of the neural foramen. Normal foraminal height is greater than fifteen millimeters. Foraminal stenosis is identified by a foraminal height less than fifteen millimeters, loss of perineural fat around the exiting nerve root on sagittal MRI, and disc-osteophyte contact with the dorsal root ganglion. Foraminal stenosis compresses the exiting nerve root, so at L4-5, the L4 root is affected. This distinction between traversing and exiting root compression is critical for the boards.

Now the essential clinical distinction: neurogenic versus vascular claudication. This is a board favorite and you will be asked to differentiate them.

Neurogenic claudication is caused by compression of the cauda equina. Symptoms include bilateral or unilateral leg pain, heaviness, numbness, and weakness that worsen with lumbar extension, prolonged walking, and standing. Symptoms improve with lumbar flexion, sitting, or leaning forward. This is the shopping cart sign: patients feel better leaning over a shopping cart because flexion opens the spinal canal by increasing the cross-sectional area and reducing ligamentum flavum infolding. Patients often report they can ride a stationary bicycle without symptoms because biking is done in flexion. The pain follows a dermatomal distribution, though it can be diffuse. Pedal pulses are present and normal.

Vascular claudication is caused by peripheral arterial disease. Symptoms include cramping calf pain that occurs with walking and is relieved by rest, regardless of position. Patients do not need to sit down; simply stopping walking relieves the pain. Flexion does not help. The pain is in a non-dermatomal, muscular distribution, typically in the calves. Pedal pulses are diminished or absent. The ankle-brachial index is the screening test: an ABI less than 0.9 confirms peripheral vascular disease. Skin changes such as hair loss, thinning skin, and poor capillary refill may be present.

For the boards, remember these five differentiators. Neurogenic claudication: provoked by extension, relieved by flexion, dermatomal distribution, normal pulses, positive shopping cart sign. Vascular claudication: provoked by walking distance, relieved by rest in any position, muscular distribution in the calves, diminished pulses, abnormal ABI.

The natural history of lumbar stenosis is important context. Studies show that over four years, fifteen to fifty percent of patients worsen, roughly thirty to fifty percent remain stable, and some improve spontaneously. This informs the rationale for initial conservative management.

Moving to the clinical case.

You are consulted on a seventy-two-year-old man who presents with a six-month history of bilateral leg pain and heaviness when walking more than one block. The symptoms are relieved by sitting down or leaning over his walker. He reports the shopping cart sign: he can walk through the entire grocery store leaning on the cart without symptoms, but walking across the parking lot without the cart is limited to fifty yards. His neurological examination is intact. Motor strength is full in all groups. Sensation is intact. Reflexes are symmetric. Pulses are palpable and normal bilaterally. MRI of the lumbar spine demonstrates severe central stenosis at L4-5 with marked ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, facet joint hypertrophy, and a broad-based disc bulge. The AP diameter of the canal at L4-5 measures six millimeters. The levels above and below are normal. Standing flexion-extension lateral radiographs show no translation at L4-5. There is no spondylolisthesis.

This is a straightforward presentation of neurogenic claudication from single-level severe central stenosis at L4-5 without instability.

Now the examiner interrogation.

Examiner: "Walk me through your workup for this patient."

Your answer: My workup begins with the MRI, which is the gold standard imaging modality for lumbar stenosis. It provides direct visualization of the thecal sac, nerve roots, ligamentum flavum, facet joints, and discs without radiation. If the patient cannot undergo MRI due to a pacemaker or claustrophobia, the alternative is a CT myelogram, which provides excellent bony detail and demonstrates the degree of thecal sac compression with intrathecal contrast. I obtain standing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs along with dynamic flexion-extension lateral views to assess alignment, disc height, and the presence of spondylolisthesis or dynamic instability. I define dynamic instability as greater than three millimeters of translation on flexion-extension views. Given this patient's age and the need to rule out vascular claudication, I would check an ankle-brachial index. His pulses are normal, which is reassuring, but an ABI less than 0.9 would prompt a vascular surgery referral. I do not routinely order electromyography for typical stenosis presentations, though it can be useful if the diagnosis is unclear or if I need to differentiate stenosis from a peripheral neuropathy.

Examiner: "What conservative options do you recommend before surgery?"

Your answer: I recommend a structured course of conservative management for three to six months before offering surgery, assuming there is no progressive neurological deficit. First, physical therapy focused on flexion-based exercises, also called Williams flexion exercises, which open the canal and strengthen the core musculature. I avoid extension-based protocols. Second, activity modification, including use of a rolling walker or shopping cart posture for prolonged walking. Third, I consider epidural steroid injections. The LESS trial, Friedly, NEJM 2014, found epidural glucocorticoid plus lidocaine gave minimal or no benefit over lidocaine alone at six weeks, and other data show at best modest short-term benefit lasting a few weeks. Do not cite MILD here: MILD is a percutaneous decompression device, not an injection trial. Injections can be interlaminar or transforaminal depending on the stenosis pattern. They are most useful as a bridge or diagnostic tool, not a definitive treatment. I also optimize medical comorbidities including weight loss, smoking cessation, and diabetic neuropathy management if applicable. Oral medications such as gabapentin or pregabalin may provide symptomatic relief for the neuropathic component.

Examiner: "He returns after four months of conservative care with no improvement. What are your surgical indications?"

Your answer: Surgical indications for lumbar stenosis include failure of adequate conservative management for three to six months, progressive neurological deficit at any time, and cauda equina syndrome, which is an emergency. This patient meets the first criterion. He has failed four months of conservative care with persistent functional limitation. The SPORT trial demonstrated that surgical treatment is superior to continued conservative management at four years for lumbar stenosis in the as-treated analysis.

Examiner: "What procedure do you recommend for this patient?"

Your answer: For this patient with single-level severe central stenosis at L4-5 without spondylolisthesis and without instability on dynamic films, I recommend a decompressive laminectomy at L4-5. This is the gold standard procedure for central lumbar stenosis. I perform a midline posterior approach, remove the L4 inferior lamina and L5 superior lamina, resect the hypertrophied ligamentum flavum bilaterally, and perform medial facetectomy as needed to decompress the lateral recesses. The key technical principle is adequate bilateral decompression while preserving at least fifty percent of each facet joint to maintain stability. I undercut the medial facets rather than resecting them entirely.

An alternative approach is a unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression, which is a minimally invasive option. Through a unilateral paramedian approach, I tilt the microscope or tubular retractor to decompress the ipsilateral side, then angle across the midline to decompress the contralateral lateral recess and central canal by undercutting the contralateral lamina and ligamentum flavum. This approach preserves the midline posterior tension band, including the spinous process and interspinous ligament, theoretically reducing the risk of postoperative instability. It is particularly well-suited for single-level stenosis in a patient without instability.

Interspinous process spacers such as the Superion device are an FDA-approved option for moderate lumbar stenosis at one to two levels. They work by maintaining the interspinous space in a slightly flexed position, preventing extension and indirectly opening the canal. However, they are contraindicated in the presence of spondylolisthesis, severe stenosis with AP diameter less than six millimeters, significant instability, or stenosis at more than two levels. For this patient with an AP diameter of six millimeters, the stenosis is at the borderline for interspinous spacer candidacy, and I would favor a standard decompression.

Examiner: "Now change the scenario. Suppose preoperative imaging reveals a Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis. Would you decompress alone or add fusion?"

Your answer: This is a critical decision point. When a Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis is present at L4-5 with stenosis, the evidence favors decompression with fusion over decompression alone. The SPORT trial spondylolisthesis arm demonstrated that surgery was superior to conservative care for degenerative spondylolisthesis with stenosis, and fusion was the procedure performed. The Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study showed better outcomes with fusion and decompression compared to decompression alone in patients with spondylolisthesis. Most importantly, the Ghogawala SLIP trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016, directly randomized patients with Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 to decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion. The primary outcome, SF-36 physical component score at two years, favored fusion, and the advantage in physical function and reoperation rate persisted at four years. This is the definitive trial on this question. My procedure in this scenario would be an L4-5 laminectomy with posterior lumbar interbody fusion or transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion using pedicle screw fixation.

Examiner: "What criteria define instability that mandates adding fusion to your decompression?"

Your answer: I use five criteria to determine the need for fusion. First, spondylolisthesis greater than Grade 1 on the Meyerding classification. Second, any grade of spondylolisthesis with dynamic instability, which I define as greater than three millimeters of translation on flexion-extension radiographs. Third, scoliosis greater than ten degrees associated with the stenotic segment. Fourth, the need for extensive facetectomy during decompression, meaning greater than fifty percent bilateral facet resection, which destabilizes the motion segment. Fifth, recurrent stenosis after prior decompression at the same level, where the structural integrity of the posterior elements is already compromised and further bone removal would create iatrogenic instability.

Examiner: "Would you decompress an asymptomatic severe stenosis found incidentally on MRI?"

Your answer: No. I treat the patient, not the MRI. Asymptomatic spinal stenosis, even if radiographically severe, does not require surgical intervention. There is a well-documented disconnect between radiographic severity and clinical symptoms. Many patients with severe stenosis on imaging are completely asymptomatic, and operating on them exposes them to surgical risk without clinical benefit. I would document the finding, counsel the patient on symptoms to watch for, and follow clinically.

Examiner: "Your patient presents with acute onset of urinary retention, bilateral leg weakness, and saddle anesthesia. MRI shows cauda equina compression from severe L4-5 stenosis. How urgent is surgery?"

Your answer: This is cauda equina syndrome and it is a surgical emergency. I take this patient to the operating room emergently, ideally within twenty-four hours and as soon as possible. Progressive deficits and delayed decompression worsen prognosis. The longer the cauda equina is compressed, the less likely the patient is to recover bladder function. Studies show that decompression within forty-eight hours is associated with better outcomes than delayed surgery, and decompression within twenty-four hours is associated with the best recovery. I would not delay for any additional workup beyond the MRI that has already established the diagnosis. This is an L4-5 laminectomy performed emergently.

Examiner: "You perform the laminectomy and encounter a dural tear during decompression. How do you manage it?"

Your answer: Incidental durotomy occurs in five to fifteen percent of lumbar decompression cases. It is more common in revision surgery and in patients with severe stenosis where the dura is adherent to the ligamentum flavum. When I recognize a dural tear, I first control the field with gentle patties and avoid suctioning directly on the dural defect, which can injure nerve roots. I perform a primary repair using 5-0 or 6-0 non-absorbable suture, typically Prolene or Nurolon, in a running or interrupted watertight fashion. If the defect is in a location that makes primary suture difficult, I reinforce with a fascial graft, a collagen dural substitute, or fibrin glue. After repair, I perform a Valsalva maneuver by having anesthesia deliver sustained positive pressure to forty centimeters of water to confirm a watertight closure. If the repair is solid, I do not routinely place a lumbar drain. I keep the patient flat for twenty-four to forty-eight hours postoperatively as a precaution. If the repair is tenuous or a significant leak persists despite repair, I place a lumbar drain for two to three days. The key teaching point is that a well-repaired incidental durotomy does not worsen patient outcomes.

Examiner: "What other complications should you counsel the patient about?"

Your answer: Beyond dural tear, I counsel about recurrent stenosis, which occurs in five to twenty percent of patients at long-term follow-up due to continued degenerative progression or scar formation. I discuss postoperative instability, which is the rationale for preserving the facet joints during decompression: resecting more than fifty percent of the bilateral facets creates iatrogenic instability and may require subsequent fusion. Epidural hematoma is a rare but serious complication requiring emergent evacuation if it causes progressive neurological deficit. Infection occurs in one to three percent of cases, higher in diabetics and obese patients. Nerve root injury is possible but rare with careful technique. I also discuss the possibility of inadequate relief if the primary pain generator was not the stenosis, which underscores the importance of correlating symptoms with imaging findings preoperatively.

Now let's consolidate the evidence.

The SPORT trial is the landmark study for lumbar stenosis. The Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial enrolled patients with lumbar stenosis across thirteen centers in the United States. It had both a randomized cohort and an observational cohort. There was significant crossover between groups, which compromised the intent-to-treat analysis. However, the as-treated analysis at four years showed that patients who underwent surgery had significantly greater improvement in bodily pain, physical function, and the Oswestry Disability Index compared to those treated conservatively. The treatment effect was durable at eight-year follow-up.

The SPORT spondylolisthesis arm separately enrolled patients with degenerative spondylolisthesis and stenosis and showed that surgery, which in this arm was decompression with fusion, was significantly superior to conservative care at four years.

The Ghogawala SLIP trial, published in 2016 in the New England Journal of Medicine, is the definitive randomized trial comparing decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion for Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5. At four years, the fusion group had significantly better SF-36 physical component scores and a reoperation rate of fourteen percent compared to thirty-four percent in the decompression-alone group. This trial settled the debate: when spondylolisthesis is present, add fusion.

The Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study corroborated these findings, showing that decompression with fusion produced better outcomes than decompression alone in patients with spondylolisthesis, though the benefit was less clear in patients without spondylolisthesis.

For epidural steroid injections, the LESS trial and subsequent meta-analyses demonstrate at best modest short-term symptom improvement lasting a few weeks. Epidural injections do not alter the natural history of stenosis and should not be considered definitive treatment.

Now for the board pearls.

The single most important distinction on this topic is when to add fusion. Remember the rule: stenosis alone without spondylolisthesis or instability gets decompression alone. Stenosis with spondylolisthesis, dynamic instability, scoliosis greater than ten degrees, or the need for extensive facetectomy gets decompression plus fusion. The Ghogawala SLIP trial is your citation for this. Know the trial name, the journal, and the result.

Never confuse neurogenic and vascular claudication. If the examiner presents a patient with claudication, confirm the mechanism before recommending spine surgery. Check pulses. Check an ABI. The shopping cart sign is pathognomonic for neurogenic claudication. Vascular claudication does not improve with flexion.

Know the anatomy of root compression by zone. Central stenosis compresses the cauda equina. Lateral recess stenosis compresses the traversing root: at L4-5, that is L5. Foraminal stenosis compresses the exiting root: at L4-5, that is L4. If the examiner asks which root is affected and you get the zone wrong, it signals a fundamental gap in your anatomic understanding.

Remember the numbers. Absolute central stenosis is an AP diameter less than ten millimeters. Lateral recess stenosis is less than three millimeters by the Lee classification. Foraminal stenosis is a foraminal height less than fifteen millimeters. Dynamic instability is greater than three millimeters of translation on flexion-extension films. More than fifty percent bilateral facetectomy mandates fusion. Incidental durotomy rate is five to fifteen percent.

The three things that will fail you on this topic. One: recommending decompression alone for a patient with degenerative spondylolisthesis, which ignores the SLIP trial evidence. Two: confusing neurogenic and vascular claudication or failing to check an ABI when vascular disease is in the differential. Three: operating on an asymptomatic patient based solely on severe radiographic stenosis rather than treating the patient's symptoms and functional limitations.

That covers lumbar stenosis and claudication. You're ready for this one.
