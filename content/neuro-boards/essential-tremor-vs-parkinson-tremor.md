Welcome to this oral board review session on essential tremor versus Parkinson tremor. These are the two most common movement disorders you will encounter in clinical practice, and the examiner expects you to differentiate them cleanly at the bedside, select the right workup, manage them medically, and know when and how to refer for surgery. The surgical targets are different, the patient selection criteria are different, and the pitfalls are real. Let's build this systematically.

Starting with essential tremor. Essential tremor is the single most common movement disorder in the adult population, with a prevalence of 1 to 5 percent, increasing with age. It is characterized by a 4 to 12 Hz action and postural tremor, predominantly affecting the hands bilaterally. The tremor is kinetic, meaning it worsens during voluntary movement, and postural, meaning it appears when the hands are held against gravity. It may be asymmetric in amplitude, but it is bilateral in distribution. Head tremor, known as titubation, occurs in 30 to 50 percent of patients and is a hallmark that favors essential tremor over Parkinson disease. Voice tremor affects 15 to 20 percent of patients. Family history is positive in 50 to 60 percent of cases, and the inheritance pattern is autosomal dominant with variable penetrance. One of the classic clinical features the examiner may probe is alcohol responsiveness. Essential tremor improves transiently with alcohol intake, which is both diagnostically useful and clinically relevant because it helps differentiate from Parkinson tremor, which does not respond to alcohol. The tremor worsens with stress, caffeine, fatigue, and adrenergic stimulation. Critically, essential tremor is a monosymptomatic condition in its pure form. There is no bradykinesia, no rigidity, and no postural instability. If you find those features, you are no longer dealing with isolated essential tremor, and you need to reconsider your diagnosis.

Now Parkinson disease. Parkinson disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer disease, with a prevalence of 1 to 2 per 1,000 in the general population, rising to 1 percent in individuals over age 60. The cardinal motor features are captured by the mnemonic TRAP. T for tremor, which is a rest tremor at 4 to 6 Hz, classically pill-rolling in character, and characteristically asymmetric at onset. R for rigidity, which is cogwheel rigidity, a ratcheting resistance to passive movement that reflects the superimposition of tremor on increased tone. A for akinesia and bradykinesia, which is slowness and reduction in amplitude of repetitive movements, and this is the most functionally disabling motor feature. P for postural instability, which is a late feature and generally does not appear until Hoehn and Yahr stage III or beyond. The pathophysiology is the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, with intracellular accumulation of Lewy bodies containing misfolded alpha-synuclein protein. Dopamine depletion in the nigrostriatal pathway results in an imbalance of the basal ganglia motor circuit, with overactivity of the indirect pathway and underactivity of the direct pathway, producing the hypokinetic movement disorder.

You must know the Hoehn and Yahr staging system. Stage I is unilateral involvement only. Stage II is bilateral involvement without impairment of balance. Stage III is bilateral disease with postural instability, but the patient is still physically independent. Stage IV is severe disability, but the patient can still stand and walk unassisted. Stage V is wheelchair-bound or bedridden without assistance. The examiner will also expect you to know the non-motor features of Parkinson disease because they are increasingly recognized as part of the prodromal syndrome. Anosmia often precedes motor symptoms by years. Constipation is common and reflects autonomic dysfunction. REM sleep behavior disorder, in which patients act out their dreams due to loss of normal REM atonia, is strongly associated with future development of synucleinopathies including Parkinson disease. Depression and anxiety are present in 30 to 40 percent of patients. Cognitive decline progresses to Parkinson disease dementia in a substantial proportion of patients over time.

Let me now walk through the key differentiators verbally, the way you would present them to an examiner. Rest tremor points to Parkinson disease. Action and postural tremor points to essential tremor. Pill-rolling quality points to Parkinson disease. Head tremor or titubation points to essential tremor. Alcohol responsiveness points to essential tremor. Bradykinesia points to Parkinson disease. Strong family history with autosomal dominant pattern points to essential tremor. Asymmetric onset with unilateral predominance points to Parkinson disease. These are the anchors. If you can articulate these distinctions clearly and quickly, you demonstrate command of the topic before the examiner even starts pressing you.

Now let me present you with two patients, the way the examiner would. Patient A is a 65-year-old right-handed man who presents with bilateral hand tremor that has been slowly progressive for 10 years. He reports that the tremor is worst when holding a cup of coffee or writing, and it has caused him significant embarrassment at dinner. His mother had a similar tremor, and his brother has also been diagnosed with tremor. He notes that the tremor reliably improves after one glass of wine. On examination, he has bilateral upper extremity postural and kinetic tremor, worse on the right than the left. There is no tremor at rest. Muscle tone is normal. Finger tapping is full speed and amplitude without decrement. Gait is normal with intact arm swing bilaterally. There is a fine head tremor that he does not notice himself but his wife confirms. This is essential tremor. Patient B is a 68-year-old right-handed man who presents with a right hand tremor noticed over the past 2 years. His wife observes that the hand trembles when it is resting on his lap while watching television, and it stops when he reaches for the remote. He reports difficulty buttoning his shirt, and his handwriting has become progressively smaller. On examination, there is a 4 to 5 Hz rest tremor in the right hand with a pill-rolling quality. Cogwheel rigidity is present at the right wrist and elbow. Finger tapping on the right shows progressive decrement in amplitude. His facial expression is diminished with a masked facies. His gait shows decreased arm swing on the right, shortened stride length, and a slight shuffle. This is Parkinson disease. The two presentations are textbook, but the examiner will now push you into the gray zones.

Let me take you through the examiner interrogation, starting with clinical differentiation at the bedside. When you evaluate a tremor patient, you must observe the hands in three conditions: at rest with the hands relaxed in the lap, with the arms outstretched in a postural position, and during goal-directed movement such as finger-to-nose testing or pouring water between cups. A tremor that is maximal at rest and suppresses with action is a rest tremor pointing to Parkinson disease. A tremor that emerges with posture and worsens with action is a kinetic and postural tremor pointing to essential tremor. Spiral drawing is a powerful bedside tool. In essential tremor, the spiral will show large-amplitude oscillations that may cause the patient to go outside the lines. In Parkinson disease, the spiral will be micrographic, small, and cramped, reflecting the bradykinesia. Finger tapping is the key test for bradykinesia. Ask the patient to tap the index finger against the thumb as quickly and as widely as possible. In Parkinson disease, you will see progressive decrement in both speed and amplitude with repetition. This fatiguing pattern is specific to bradykinesia and is not present in essential tremor.

Now the workup. When the clinical picture is clear, no imaging is necessary. But when the diagnosis is uncertain, and the examiner will specifically ask about this scenario, the DaTscan is your tool. The DaTscan uses iodine-123 ioflupane, a radiotracer that binds to the dopamine transporter on presynaptic dopaminergic nerve terminals in the striatum. It is performed as a SPECT scan. In a normal study and in essential tremor, the dopamine transporter uptake is preserved, and the striatum shows a symmetric comma-shaped pattern of uptake on both sides. In Parkinson disease, dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra leads to reduced dopamine transporter density in the striatum, and the uptake pattern changes from a comma to a period. This is described as the comma-to-period sign, reflecting loss of the caudate tail or putaminal uptake. The DaTscan is FDA-approved specifically for differentiating essential tremor from parkinsonian syndromes. It does not differentiate Parkinson disease from other causes of parkinsonism such as multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, or corticobasal degeneration, because all of these also involve dopaminergic neuron loss. An MRI of the brain should be obtained to rule out structural causes of tremor, including Wilson disease with basal ganglia changes, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and cerebellar lesions.

Let's move to medical management, starting with essential tremor. First-line therapy is propranolol or primidone. Propranolol is the most extensively studied beta-blocker for essential tremor, dosed at 60 to 320 milligrams per day, typically starting at 60 milligrams daily in divided doses or as long-acting formulation. It reduces tremor amplitude by approximately 50 percent in responders. Avoid propranolol in patients with asthma, decompensated heart failure, or significant bradycardia. Primidone is equally effective as first-line therapy, dosed at 25 to 750 milligrams per day. You must start at a very low dose, 25 milligrams at bedtime, because patients experience significant initial sedation and dizziness that improves with slow titration. Second-line agents include topiramate at 50 to 400 milligrams per day and gabapentin at 900 to 3600 milligrams per day. For head and voice tremor specifically, botulinum toxin injection is the preferred approach because systemic medications are less effective for axial and laryngeal tremor.

For Parkinson disease, the pharmacotherapy is more complex. Levodopa combined with carbidopa is the most effective medication and remains the gold standard. Carbidopa inhibits peripheral decarboxylation of levodopa, reducing nausea and increasing central bioavailability. Typical dosing starts at 25/100, meaning 25 milligrams of carbidopa with 100 milligrams of levodopa, three times daily, and is titrated upward to a typical range of 300 to 600 milligrams of levodopa per day based on clinical response. The examiner may ask about motor complications of long-term levodopa use. After 5 to 10 years, approximately 50 percent of patients develop motor fluctuations including wearing-off phenomena, where the medication effect shortens between doses, and dyskinesias, which are involuntary choreiform movements that occur at peak levodopa levels. Dopamine agonists, specifically pramipexole and ropinirole, can be used as initial monotherapy in younger patients to delay the introduction of levodopa and thereby postpone motor complications. However, dopamine agonists carry significant risk of impulse control disorders including pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping, and binge eating, occurring in approximately 15 to 20 percent of patients. You must warn patients about this and monitor for it. MAO-B inhibitors, rasagiline at 0.5 to 1 milligram daily and selegiline at 5 milligrams twice daily, provide mild symptomatic benefit as monotherapy in early disease or as adjuncts to levodopa in moderate disease. Amantadine at 100 to 300 milligrams per day is the only medication with proven efficacy for levodopa-induced dyskinesias and is an important tool in managing motor complications.

Now we arrive at the surgical component, which is the core of what you need to own as a neurosurgeon on the oral boards. The general indication for surgical intervention in both essential tremor and Parkinson disease is medication-refractory tremor or intolerable medication side effects that prevent adequate dosing. For Parkinson disease specifically, you must establish levodopa responsiveness before offering deep brain stimulation. The patient must demonstrate at least 30 percent improvement in the Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale motor score on a levodopa challenge, comparing the off-medication state to the on-medication state. This threshold is not arbitrary. Levodopa responsiveness is the single best predictor of response to subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation. If the patient does not respond to levodopa, you must suspect atypical parkinsonism. Multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration do not respond to DBS, and implanting a patient with one of these conditions is a board-level error. Additional contraindications to DBS include significant cognitive impairment, as formal neuropsychological testing should be performed preoperatively. Dementia is a contraindication. Severe untreated psychiatric disease is also a contraindication, particularly active psychosis, severe depression, or active suicidality.

The surgical targets differ by disease. For essential tremor, the target is the VIM, the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus. The VIM is a cerebellar relay nucleus that sits in the ventrolateral thalamus and functions as a critical node in the cerebello-thalamo-cortical tremor circuit. DBS of the VIM effectively suppresses tremor in 70 to 90 percent of essential tremor patients. For Parkinson disease, the two primary DBS targets are the subthalamic nucleus, the STN, and the globus pallidus internus, the GPi. Both have Level I evidence supporting their efficacy. The COMPARE trial directly compared STN and GPi DBS for Parkinson disease and found similar motor outcomes at the primary endpoint. However, there are important differences that determine target selection. STN stimulation allows greater levodopa dose reduction, typically 30 to 50 percent, which is advantageous in patients with significant dyskinesias related to medication load. GPi stimulation directly suppresses dyskinesia without requiring medication reduction. GPi may be preferred in patients with mild cognitive concerns because STN DBS has a higher rate of cognitive and mood side effects, including worsening verbal fluency, depression, and impulsivity. In most centers, STN is preferred for tremor-dominant Parkinson disease and for patients who would benefit from medication reduction, while GPi is preferred in patients with prominent dyskinesia, cognitive vulnerability, or mood instability.

The examiner will very likely ask you about MRI-guided focused ultrasound, MRgFUS. This is FDA-approved for medication-refractory essential tremor, performing a unilateral VIM thalamotomy, and also for tremor-dominant Parkinson disease. The procedure uses focused ultrasound energy delivered through the intact skull under real-time MRI thermometry guidance, creating a precise thermal lesion in the VIM without an incision, without a burr hole, and without an implant. The Elias 2016 New England Journal of Medicine randomized controlled trial demonstrated significant tremor reduction in the treated hand compared to sham at 3 months, with sustained benefit at 1 and 2 year follow-up. The advantages are obvious: no hardware means no infection risk, no lead migration, no battery replacement. But the disadvantages are equally important. The lesion is not adjustable. Unlike DBS where you can reprogram stimulation parameters to optimize benefit and minimize side effects, a thalamotomy lesion is permanent. If you get a suboptimal result or stimulation-equivalent side effects, you cannot dial it back. More critically, MRgFUS thalamotomy is unilateral only. Bilateral thalamotomy carries an unacceptable risk of permanent dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive decline. If the examiner asks whether you would perform bilateral MRgFUS thalamotomy for bilateral essential tremor, the answer is no. For bilateral tremor control, bilateral VIM DBS is the preferred approach because stimulation is adjustable and reversible. Additionally, MRgFUS requires an adequate skull density ratio for ultrasound penetration, typically above 0.4 to 0.45, and patients with low skull density ratios cannot be treated effectively.

Now let me hit you with the trap questions the examiner may deploy. First trap: the patient has tremor and bradykinesia, so it must be Parkinson disease, right? Not necessarily. You must consider the differential diagnosis. Essential tremor plus is a recognized entity in which essential tremor is accompanied by soft neurological signs such as mild gait impairment, questionable rest tremor, or subtle tandem gait difficulty, but without meeting criteria for Parkinson disease. Drug-induced parkinsonism is extremely common and is caused by dopamine receptor blocking agents including antipsychotics such as haloperidol and quetiapine, and antiemetics such as metoclopramide and prochlorperazine. The tremor and bradykinesia resolve with discontinuation of the offending agent, and a DaTscan will be normal because the presynaptic neurons are intact. Wilson disease must be considered in any young patient under age 40 with a movement disorder. Wilson disease is an autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism. Get a ceruloplasmin level. If low, follow up with 24-hour urine copper and slit-lamp examination for Kayser-Fleischer rings. Normal pressure hydrocephalus presents with the classic Hakim triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and dementia, and the gait may include parkinsonian features. Vascular parkinsonism from small vessel ischemic disease affecting the basal ganglia can mimic Parkinson disease but typically presents with lower body predominance and poor levodopa response.

Second trap: would you offer DBS to a patient with Parkinson disease who does not respond to levodopa? The answer is no. Levodopa responsiveness is the best predictor of STN DBS response. If the patient does not improve with levodopa, the most likely explanation is that they have an atypical parkinsonian syndrome such as multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, or corticobasal degeneration. These conditions do not respond to DBS. The levodopa challenge with at least 30 percent improvement on the UPDRS motor score is a mandatory prerequisite. This is a hard and fast rule.

Third trap: would you do bilateral MRgFUS thalamotomy for essential tremor? No. As discussed, bilateral thalamotomy carries unacceptable morbidity including permanent dysarthria, dysphagia, and cognitive decline. For patients requiring bilateral tremor control, bilateral VIM DBS is the standard because stimulation parameters can be individually optimized on each side and can be reduced or turned off if side effects emerge.

Let me now discuss complications of DBS that you must be prepared to address. Infection occurs in 3 to 5 percent of cases and may involve the generator pocket, the extension wire, or the intracranial lead. Superficial infections may respond to antibiotics, but deep infections or infections involving the hardware typically require explantation. Intracranial hemorrhage occurs in 1 to 2 percent of cases and can range from an asymptomatic tract hemorrhage to a symptomatic hematoma requiring evacuation. Lead migration or fracture can occur and presents with loss of therapeutic benefit, requiring revision surgery. Hardware malfunction including lead fracture, extension cable break, and generator failure must be considered when a previously well-controlled patient loses benefit. Stimulation-related side effects are common but programmable. They include dysarthria, paresthesias, muscle contractions, gait worsening, and mood changes. These are managed by adjusting stimulation parameters including amplitude, pulse width, frequency, and contact selection. Battery life is 3 to 5 years for non-rechargeable generators and 9 to 15 years for rechargeable generators, and patients must be counseled about the need for future generator replacement surgery.

Let me cover the key evidence. The COMPARE trial directly compared STN versus GPi DBS for Parkinson disease and found equivalent motor outcomes, establishing both targets as valid options and shifting the decision to secondary considerations such as medication reduction and cognitive profile. The VA Cooperative Study compared DBS to best medical therapy for advanced Parkinson disease and demonstrated superiority of DBS for motor function, quality of life, and on-time without troublesome dyskinesia. The EARLYSTIM trial randomized Parkinson disease patients with early motor complications, within 7.5 years of disease onset and 3 years of motor fluctuations, to STN DBS plus medical therapy versus medical therapy alone and showed that earlier DBS intervention improved quality of life, motor disability, motor complications, and time with good mobility and no dyskinesia. This trial pushed the window for DBS consideration earlier in the disease course. The Elias 2016 New England Journal of Medicine trial established MRgFUS thalamotomy as an effective treatment for medication-refractory essential tremor. Key pharmacotherapy references include the Fasano and Bhidayasiri reviews on essential tremor treatment algorithms.

Let me close with the board pearls that you should have at the tip of your tongue. The single most important differentiating feature is rest versus action tremor. Rest tremor is Parkinson disease. Action and postural tremor is essential tremor. DaTscan resolves diagnostic uncertainty by demonstrating reduced dopamine transporter uptake in the striatum in Parkinson disease, with the comma-to-period sign, versus normal uptake in essential tremor. Levodopa responsiveness with at least 30 percent improvement on the UPDRS motor score is the best predictor of DBS success in Parkinson disease and is a mandatory prerequisite. The surgical target for essential tremor is the VIM. The surgical targets for Parkinson disease are the STN and GPi. Never perform bilateral thalamotomy, whether open, radiofrequency, or focused ultrasound. For bilateral tremor, DBS is preferred because it is adjustable and reversible. Atypical parkinsonism, including multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration, does not respond to DBS, and these patients should not be implanted. MRgFUS is a valid unilateral option for medication-refractory essential tremor and tremor-dominant Parkinson disease, but the lesion is permanent and not adjustable, and bilateral lesioning is contraindicated. Drug-induced parkinsonism is the most common secondary cause of parkinsonism and will have a normal DaTscan. Always check the medication list. Wilson disease must be excluded in any young patient with a movement disorder by checking ceruloplasmin.

That covers essential tremor versus Parkinson tremor. You're ready for this one.
