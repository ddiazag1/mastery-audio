Welcome to this oral board teaching session on subdural hematoma management. This is one of the highest-yield trauma topics you will face on the ABNS oral boards, and you need to be completely fluent in the classification, surgical decision-making, anticoagulation reversal protocols, and the landmark evidence that drives modern practice. Let's build this from the ground up and then work through two clinical cases the way an examiner would interrogate you.

Let's start with the foundational anatomy and pathophysiology. A subdural hematoma is a collection of blood between the dura mater and the arachnoid membrane. It is the most common traumatic intracranial mass lesion. The bleeding source, and this is critical, is the bridging veins. These are the cortical veins that traverse the subdural space from the brain surface to the dural venous sinuses. They cross this space unsupported, and that is exactly what makes them vulnerable. When the brain moves relative to the dura, whether from acceleration-deceleration injury, a direct blow, or even trivial trauma, these bridging veins tear and bleed into the subdural space.

Now, classification by timing. Acute subdural hematoma is defined as less than 3 days, or 72 hours, from injury. On non-contrast CT, acute blood is hyperdense, measuring 50 to 60 Hounsfield units. It appears as a bright white crescent along the convexity. Subacute subdural hematoma spans 3 to 21 days. This is the dangerous diagnostic window because the collection becomes isodense to brain parenchyma, meaning it can be nearly invisible on CT. This is a classic missed diagnosis, and we will come back to that. Chronic subdural hematoma is greater than 21 days. On CT it is hypodense, similar to CSF density, and you will often see enhancing neomembranes on the inner and outer surfaces of the collection. Mixed density within a subdural collection, areas of hyperdensity within a hypodense collection, tells you this is an acute-on-chronic subdural hematoma, meaning there has been a rebleed into a pre-existing chronic collection.

The CT appearance is characteristic and you must describe it precisely. Subdural hematomas are crescent-shaped, also described as concavo-convex. They conform to the brain surface. They cross suture lines, which distinguishes them from epidural hematomas. They do not cross the midline because the falx cerebri blocks their spread. When you see bilateral subdural hematomas, those are two separate collections, one on each side of the falx.

Why does the patient population matter so much? In elderly patients, brain atrophy creates a larger subdural space, and the bridging veins are stretched across a greater distance. This makes them vulnerable to tearing with even trivial trauma, a minor fall, a stumble, sometimes no remembered event at all. Anticoagulated patients bleed more readily and their collections expand. Alcoholic patients have brain atrophy and coagulopathy, a double vulnerability. In young patients, acute subdural hematoma results from high-energy trauma: motor vehicle collisions, falls from height, assaults. Here is the critical prognostic distinction. Acute subdural hematoma in young patients from high-energy trauma is almost always accompanied by underlying brain injury, cortical contusions, diffuse axonal injury, brain swelling. This is why acute subdural hematoma carries a far worse prognosis than epidural hematoma despite successful surgical evacuation. The clot is only part of the problem. The mortality for acute subdural hematoma requiring surgery is 40 to 60 percent.

Chronic subdural hematoma has a completely different pathophysiology. After the initial hemorrhage, neomembranes form on the inner and outer surfaces of the collection. These membranes contain fragile capillaries that undergo recurrent microhemorrhages. There is an inflammatory cascade with fibrinolytic activity within the collection, and an osmotic gradient that draws additional fluid into the hematoma cavity. The collection slowly expands over weeks to months. This is why chronic subdural hematomas grow and become symptomatic long after the initial injury.

Now let's move into the clinical cases. I am going to present two scenarios, and then we will work through each one the way an examiner would.

Case A. A 35-year-old male involved in a high-speed motor vehicle collision. He arrives to the trauma bay with a GCS of 7, intubated in the field. His left pupil is fixed and dilated at 6 millimeters. The right pupil is 3 millimeters and reactive. CT head shows a right hemispheric acute subdural hematoma measuring 15 millimeters in maximum thickness with 12 millimeters of midline shift from right to left. The basal cisterns are effaced.

Case B. A 78-year-old woman on warfarin for atrial fibrillation. Her INR on arrival is 2.8. Her family reports progressive confusion over the past 3 weeks with new right-sided weakness that developed over the last several days. CT head shows a left chronic subdural hematoma measuring 22 millimeters in maximum thickness with 8 millimeters of midline shift. The collection is hypodense with a thin rim of increased density along the inner membrane.

Let's take Case A first, the acute subdural hematoma. The examiner is going to want you to state the surgical indications immediately and confidently. The Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines, often referenced as the Bullock surgical guidelines, give clear indications for operative evacuation of acute subdural hematoma. Surgery is indicated when the hematoma thickness exceeds 10 millimeters, when midline shift exceeds 5 millimeters, when the GCS has dropped by 2 or more points from the time of injury to hospital admission, or when the intracranial pressure exceeds 20 millimeters of mercury. This patient meets multiple criteria. He has 15 millimeters of thickness, 12 millimeters of shift, and a GCS of 7 with a blown pupil. This is an emergent craniotomy, no question.

The surgical technique is a large frontotemporal-parietal trauma craniotomy. You need a large bone flap, and you need to state this explicitly. The reason is adequate exposure. You must be able to visualize the entire convexity to evacuate the clot completely, inspect the brain surface for cortical lacerations and contusions, achieve meticulous hemostasis, and identify and coagulate the bleeding bridging veins. After clot evacuation, you assess the brain. If the brain is swollen and does not re-expand to fill the cranial vault after the hematoma has been removed, you must leave the bone flap off. This converts the procedure to a decompressive craniectomy with expansion duraplasty. You open the dura widely, place a dural substitute to expand the intradural volume, and store the bone flap in a subcutaneous abdominal pocket or a freezer for later cranioplasty. You place a subdural drain before closure.

Timing is everything in acute subdural hematoma, and the evidence is unequivocal. The Seelig study from 1981 is a landmark paper that showed mortality was 30 percent when operative evacuation occurred within 4 hours of injury versus 90 percent when surgery was delayed beyond 4 hours. You must know this study and these numbers. When the examiner asks about timing, the answer is operate within 4 hours. Time from injury to operating room is the single most modifiable factor affecting outcome.

The examiner will then ask about prognostic factors. The most important prognostic factor is preoperative GCS. After that, pupillary reactivity, patient age, the presence and severity of associated brain injury such as contusions and diffuse axonal injury, and time to surgery. Now here is a nuance they may test you on. A patient who is GCS 3 with bilateral fixed and dilated pupils has greater than 90 percent mortality. But this is not uniformly fatal in young patients. If the patient is young, you still operate. You cannot prognosticate based on the initial exam alone in a young patient with a surgically evacuable mass lesion. The examiner wants to hear that you will not give up on a young patient.

What about non-operative management of acute subdural hematoma? If the thickness is less than 10 millimeters, the midline shift is less than 5 millimeters, the GCS is stable, and there is no neurological deterioration, the patient can be managed non-operatively. This means ICU admission, serial neurological examinations every 1 hour, repeat CT in 6 to 8 hours, and immediate neurosurgical availability for emergent surgery if the patient deteriorates. Non-operative does not mean non-monitored.

Now let's turn to Case B, the chronic subdural hematoma in the anticoagulated elderly patient. Before you even think about the operating room, you must reverse the anticoagulation. The examiner is absolutely going to test you on reversal protocols, and you must give specific agents and doses.

For warfarin with an INR of 2.8, the protocol is intravenous vitamin K 10 milligrams plus 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate, which is KCentra, dosed at 25 to 50 units per kilogram. Check the INR 30 minutes after administration. Your target INR is less than 1.5 before proceeding to surgery. Here is a critical point. Do not use fresh frozen plasma as your primary reversal agent. FFP is too slow to administer, requires large volumes causing volume overload especially in elderly patients with cardiac comorbidities, and does not reliably normalize the INR in a timely fashion. PCC is faster, more effective, and lower volume. FFP is a backup, not a first-line agent.

For direct oral anticoagulants, you must know the specific reversal agents. Idarucizumab, brand name Praxbind, reverses dabigatran. The dose is 5 grams intravenously given as two 2.5-gram boluses. Andexanet alfa, brand name Andexxa, reverses the factor Xa inhibitors apixaban and rivarelbaan. If specific antidotes are not available, 4-factor PCC at 50 units per kilogram is used as an empiric reversal agent. For antiplatelet agents such as aspirin or clopidogrel, the role of platelet transfusion is controversial. The PATCH trial demonstrated that platelet transfusion actually worsened outcomes in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. However, the evidence specific to surgical subdural hematoma evacuation is limited. On the boards, acknowledge the PATCH trial, state that platelet transfusion is not routinely recommended for spontaneous ICH, and note that for a planned surgical procedure you may consider platelet transfusion to optimize surgical hemostasis, but this is a nuanced decision.

Now, the surgical technique for chronic subdural hematoma. The standard procedure is burr hole drainage. You place 1 to 2 burr holes, one frontal and one parietal is typical. Open the dura at each burr hole site. Irrigate the subdural space with warm saline until the irrigant runs clear. Place a subdural drain directed frontally and bring it out through a separate stab incision. Close the burr holes. The subdural drain is left in place for 24 to 48 hours. The patient is kept flat or in slight Trendelenburg to promote brain re-expansion. This brings us to one of the most important pieces of evidence in chronic subdural hematoma management. The Santarius trial, published in 2009, was a randomized controlled trial that demonstrated that placement of a subdural drain after burr hole drainage reduced recurrence from 24 percent to 9.3 percent. This is a massive reduction in recurrence, and subdural drain placement after burr hole evacuation is now standard of care. You must know this study.

What about alternatives to burr hole drainage? Mini-craniotomy provides a slightly larger exposure and allows better visualization of the membranes, which can be advantageous for organized or partially solid collections. Twist-drill craniostomy is a bedside option using a smaller caliber hole, suitable for patients who are too unstable for the operating room. Full craniotomy is reserved for organized or solid hematomas that cannot be evacuated through burr holes, multiloculated collections with thick septations, or recurrence after previous burr hole drainage.

The overall recurrence rate for chronic subdural hematoma is 10 to 20 percent. Risk factors for recurrence include ongoing anticoagulation, bilateral subdural hematomas, low brain re-expansion after drainage which can be due to trapped brain or severe atrophy, and inadequate initial drainage.

This brings us to an important emerging treatment: middle meningeal artery embolization. This is a procedure performed by neurointerventional colleagues where the branches of the middle meningeal artery feeding the neomembranes are embolized, cutting off the blood supply to the fragile capillaries within the membranes that are responsible for recurrent microhemorrhages. The embolic agents used include Onyx, which is an ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer, and polyvinyl alcohol particles. The EMBOLISH trial and multiple retrospective studies have demonstrated that middle meningeal artery embolization reduces recurrence rates to 5 to 10 percent compared with 25 to 30 percent in control groups. Embolization can be used in three ways. First, as an adjunct to surgery, where you embolize and then perform burr hole drainage. Second, as a standalone treatment for minimally symptomatic or small chronic subdural hematomas that do not require urgent surgical evacuation. Third, as a rescue treatment for recurrent chronic subdural hematoma after failed surgical drainage. The evidence is growing rapidly, and middle meningeal artery embolization is likely to become standard of care for chronic and recurrent subdural hematomas. On the boards, demonstrate that you are aware of this technique and can discuss its role.

Now, the examiner is going to set traps, and you need to be ready. Here is a classic one. The examiner says the 78-year-old has a chronic subdural hematoma that is isodense with brain on CT, and asks you, is it there? The answer is yes. Subacute and isodense chronic subdural hematomas can be extremely difficult to see on standard non-contrast CT because the density of the collection matches the density of the adjacent brain. But there are indirect signs you must look for. Sulcal effacement along the convexity. Loss of gray-white matter differentiation at the cortical surface. Midline shift that is disproportionate to any visible intracranial pathology, which is a red flag that you are missing a mass lesion. A compressed lateral ventricle on one side with a dilated ventricle on the other. If you suspect an isodense subdural, order a contrast-enhanced CT, which will show enhancing neomembranes outlining the collection, or obtain an MRI, which is far more sensitive for subdural collections regardless of their age. This is one of the classic missed diagnoses in neurosurgery.

Second trap. The examiner tells you that after burr hole drainage, the brain does not re-expand. There is a large subdural air collection and a persistent fluid space. What is this? This is trapped brain. The brain has lost its compliance and cannot re-expand to fill the cranial vault. The causes include cortical adhesions tethering the brain surface, thick neomembranes encasing the brain, and chronic compression that has altered brain elasticity. Pneumocephalus fills the space where the brain cannot expand. The key management concern is avoiding tension pneumocephalus. Keep the drain clamped when the patient is upright to prevent air entry. Keep the patient flat. If the collection persists and the patient remains symptomatic, consider placement of a subdural-peritoneal shunt to provide ongoing drainage of the fluid that will continue to accumulate because the brain cannot fill the space. Craniotomy with membrane stripping is a last resort because it carries significant risk of cortical injury and hemorrhage.

Third trap. The examiner asks, would you give dexamethasone for chronic subdural hematoma? The answer is no. The Dex-CSDH trial, published in 2020, was a multicenter randomized controlled trial that showed dexamethasone did not improve outcomes in chronic subdural hematoma and was associated with more adverse events including hyperglycemia, infections, and other steroid-related complications. Steroids are not recommended for chronic subdural hematoma. This is a definitive trial and you should cite it by name.

Let's consolidate the evidence you need to have at your fingertips. Seelig 1981 demonstrated that operating on acute subdural hematoma within 4 hours reduced mortality from 90 percent to 30 percent. The Brain Trauma Foundation Bullock guidelines define surgical indications for acute subdural hematoma: thickness greater than 10 millimeters, midline shift greater than 5 millimeters, GCS drop of 2 or more, or ICP greater than 20. Santarius 2009 is the landmark randomized trial proving that subdural drain placement after burr hole drainage reduces chronic subdural hematoma recurrence from 24 percent to 9.3 percent. The Dex-CSDH trial from 2020 showed that dexamethasone provides no benefit and causes harm in chronic subdural hematoma. The EMBOLISH trial and work from Ban and others established middle meningeal artery embolization as an effective treatment reducing recurrence to 5 to 10 percent. The PATCH trial demonstrated that platelet transfusion worsened outcomes in spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage.

Let me close with the board pearls, the things you absolutely must have loaded and ready to fire. Subdural hematomas are crescent-shaped and cross suture lines. That is your distinguishing feature from epidural hematomas. Acute subdural hematoma demands surgery within 4 hours, and you cite Seelig. For chronic subdural hematoma, the standard operation is burr holes plus a subdural drain, and you cite Santarius. Reverse warfarin with PCC, not FFP. Know your DOAC reversal agents by name and dose. Middle meningeal artery embolization is the emerging treatment for recurrent chronic subdural hematoma, and you should be conversant with its indications. Never give steroids for chronic subdural hematoma, and cite Dex-CSDH. If the brain does not re-expand after drainage, you are dealing with trapped brain. An isodense subdural on CT is a classic miss, and you look for indirect signs of mass effect.

That covers subdural hematoma management. You're ready for this one.
