Welcome to this oral board preparation session on pediatric brain tumors. This is one of the highest-yield topics you will encounter on the ABNS oral examination because it tests your knowledge of tumor biology, surgical decision-making, molecular classification, and adjuvant therapy all in a single case. Let's build your framework from the ground up.

We begin with a foundational principle that the examiner expects you to know cold. Brain tumors are the most common solid tumor in children and the leading cause of cancer-related death in the pediatric population. Unlike adults, where supratentorial tumors dominate, children have a posterior fossa predominance. Roughly sixty percent of pediatric brain tumors arise in the posterior fossa. This single anatomic fact shapes nearly every clinical scenario you will face on the boards.

You need to know the big four posterior fossa tumors in children. First, pilocytic astrocytoma, which is the most common overall pediatric brain tumor. Second, medulloblastoma, which is the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor. Third, ependymoma. Fourth, brainstem glioma, now classified as diffuse midline glioma. On the supratentorial side, your differential includes craniopharyngioma, low-grade glioma, high-grade glioma, atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor or ATRT, and choroid plexus tumors.

Here is the key conceptual shift you must demonstrate to the examiner. The 2021 WHO classification of central nervous system tumors integrated molecular markers into the diagnostic criteria. This fundamentally changed pediatric neuro-oncology. It is no longer sufficient to classify tumors by histology alone. Molecular characterization now drives diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection. The examiner will probe whether you understand this paradigm shift.

Let's start with pilocytic astrocytoma because it is the most common pediatric brain tumor and the one with the most favorable biology. Pilocytic astrocytoma is WHO Grade 1. In the cerebellum, it presents classically as a well-circumscribed cystic mass with an enhancing mural nodule. In the optic pathway, it tends to be solid and infiltrative. The molecular driver in the majority of cerebellar pilocytic astrocytomas is the BRAF-KIAA1549 fusion. This is distinct from the BRAF V600E point mutation, which occurs in supratentorial pilocytic and other low-grade gliomas and carries a more aggressive biology. This distinction matters because BRAF V600E-mutant tumors can be targeted with BRAF inhibitors such as dabrafenib, and increasingly, combination BRAF plus MEK inhibition.

Optic pathway gliomas deserve special mention. They are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1. Approximately fifteen percent of NF1 patients develop optic pathway gliomas. The critical management principle is that many of these are observed rather than treated. You intervene when there is progressive visual loss, proptosis, or hypothalamic dysfunction. Chemotherapy, typically carboplatin and vincristine, is the first-line treatment when intervention is required. Surgery is reserved for exophytic components causing mass effect, and radiation is generally avoided in young children with NF1 due to the risk of secondary malignancies and moyamoya vasculopathy.

Now let's move into a clinical case, and this is the kind of scenario the examiner will hand you.

A six-year-old boy presents with a three-week history of morning headaches, nausea and vomiting, and progressive gait unsteadiness. His parents report that he has become increasingly clumsy and has fallen several times. On examination, you find papilledema bilaterally, truncal ataxia, and inability to tandem walk. His MRI demonstrates a homogeneously enhancing solid mass filling the fourth ventricle with resultant obstructive hydrocephalus. The lateral and third ventricles are dilated. You obtain a spine MRI, which reveals two small enhancing nodules along the dorsal surface of the thoracic thecal sac.

The examiner now begins the interrogation. Expect it to be rapid and unforgiving.

The first question will be, what is your differential for this fourth ventricle mass? Your answer must be organized and specific. The most likely diagnosis is medulloblastoma. This is a solid, homogeneously enhancing mass filling the fourth ventricle in a six-year-old male with leptomeningeal drop metastases. That constellation is classic. Ependymoma is on your differential but is less likely because ependymomas characteristically squeeze through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie, giving them a "plastic" appearance that extends into the cerebellopontine angle or through the foramen of Magendie into the cisterna magna. Ependymomas are also less likely to present with disseminated disease at diagnosis. Pilocytic astrocytoma is possible but typically arises in the cerebellar hemisphere as a cystic mass with an enhancing mural nodule, not as a solid fourth-ventricle-filling lesion. ATRT should be considered but is far more common in children younger than three years.

The examiner will immediately challenge your management. This child has obstructive hydrocephalus. How do you manage it? You have two options: placement of an external ventricular drain or endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Either is performed prior to or at the time of definitive tumor surgery. You also start dexamethasone preoperatively to reduce peritumoral edema. An important number to know is that sixty to seventy percent of medulloblastoma patients will ultimately require permanent cerebrospinal fluid diversion with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. However, you do not place a shunt at the initial operation. You wait one to two weeks after tumor resection to determine whether the hydrocephalus resolves with removal of the fourth ventricular obstruction. If it does not, then you proceed with shunt placement.

Now the examiner asks about your surgical approach. You position the patient prone or in the park bench position. You perform a midline suboccipital craniotomy. The approach to the fourth ventricle is the telovelar approach. This involves dissecting the tela choroidea and the inferior medullary velum to access the floor of the fourth ventricle. The critical advantage of the telovelar approach is that it avoids splitting the cerebellar vermis. Vermis splitting has been associated with a higher incidence of cerebellar mutism syndrome. Your surgical goal is maximal safe resection. The definition of subtotal resection that changes risk stratification is greater than 1.5 square centimeters of residual tumor on postoperative MRI, which should be obtained within 48 hours of surgery.

The examiner will now ask about cerebellar mutism syndrome, also called posterior fossa syndrome. This is a critical complication that occurs in twenty to twenty-five percent of children after posterior fossa surgery, and it is most common after medulloblastoma resection. The clinical features include mutism, emotional lability, hypotonia, and worsened ataxia. Onset is typically one to two days after surgery, not immediately. Recovery is gradual over weeks to months, but many children are left with permanent dysarthria. The risk factors you must cite are midline tumor location, vermis splitting during the approach, and brainstem manipulation during resection.

Here comes the core molecular oncology portion, and this is where you differentiate yourself. Medulloblastoma has four molecular subgroups, and you must know all four with their key features and prognosis.

The WNT subgroup accounts for approximately ten percent of medulloblastomas. It is characterized by monosomy 6 and nuclear accumulation of beta-catenin on immunohistochemistry. The five-year overall survival exceeds ninety percent, making this the best-prognosis subgroup by a wide margin. Because of this excellent prognosis, current clinical trials are investigating de-escalated therapy, specifically reduced-dose craniospinal irradiation, to minimize long-term neurocognitive toxicity while maintaining cure rates.

The SHH subgroup, or Sonic Hedgehog, accounts for approximately thirty percent. It is driven by mutations in PTCH1, SMO, or SUFU along the hedgehog signaling pathway. Histologically, it often shows desmoplastic or nodular features, particularly in infants. There is a characteristic bimodal age distribution, occurring in infants and in adolescents or young adults. The prognosis is intermediate. In recurrent SHH medulloblastoma, hedgehog pathway inhibitors such as vismodegib have shown activity and represent a targeted therapeutic option.

Group 3 accounts for approximately twenty-five percent. It is defined by MYC amplification and frequently demonstrates large cell or anaplastic histology. Group 3 has the highest rate of leptomeningeal dissemination at diagnosis and carries the worst prognosis of all subgroups, with a five-year overall survival of approximately fifty percent. When you see the combination of MYC amplification, anaplastic histology, and metastatic disease, you are looking at the highest-risk medulloblastoma.

Group 4 is the most common subgroup, accounting for approximately thirty-five percent. It is associated with isochromosome 17q. The prognosis is intermediate. Group 4 is somewhat of a heterogeneous catch-all category, but it remains clinically important as the single most common molecular subgroup.

The examiner will now test your knowledge of risk stratification. Standard-risk medulloblastoma is defined by all of the following criteria: patient age greater than three years, gross total resection or near-total resection with less than 1.5 square centimeters of residual tumor, no metastatic disease which is designated M0, and absence of high-risk histologic or molecular features. High-risk medulloblastoma is defined by any of the following: age less than three years, subtotal resection with greater than 1.5 square centimeters of residual, metastatic disease classified M1 through M4, large cell or anaplastic histology, or MYC amplification.

The adjuvant therapy discussion is where numbers matter enormously. For standard-risk medulloblastoma, the craniospinal irradiation dose is 23.4 Gray. This was reduced from the historical dose of 36 Gray based on the landmark work by Packer, who demonstrated equivalent outcomes with lower-dose craniospinal irradiation combined with adjuvant chemotherapy. For high-risk medulloblastoma, the craniospinal irradiation dose remains at 36 Gray. Regardless of risk category, the posterior fossa receives a boost to 54 to 55.8 Gray. The standard chemotherapy regimen, known as the Packer regimen, consists of cisplatin, vincristine, and either lomustine or cyclophosphamide.

Now a critical nuance that the examiner will absolutely test. For infants younger than three years with medulloblastoma, you avoid or delay craniospinal irradiation because radiation to the developing brain in this age group causes devastating and irreversible neurocognitive injury. Instead, you use chemotherapy-only protocols such as Head Start or the HIT-SKK regimen. Craniospinal irradiation is delayed until age three to five if it can be deferred. If the examiner asks whether you would give craniospinal irradiation to a two-year-old with medulloblastoma, the answer is no.

Let's transition to ependymoma. The examiner may pivot to this as a separate case or as a comparison question. Posterior fossa ependymoma is classified as WHO Grade 2 or Grade 3. The molecular classification now recognizes two posterior fossa groups: PFA and PFB. PFA occurs in younger children, is associated with 1q gain, and carries a poorer prognosis. PFB occurs in older children and adults and carries a better prognosis.

The single most important prognostic factor in ependymoma is extent of resection. Gross total resection achieves a five-year progression-free survival of seventy to eighty percent, compared to only thirty to forty percent with subtotal resection. If you achieve only a subtotal resection, you should seriously consider re-resection before proceeding to radiation. This is a point the examiner expects you to make proactively. Adjuvant treatment for ependymoma is conformal radiation therapy directed at the tumor bed. Craniospinal irradiation is not used unless there is documented leptomeningeal dissemination. There is no proven role for chemotherapy in ependymoma. These are critical distinctions from medulloblastoma management that you must articulate clearly.

If the examiner asks whether a patient is cured after gross total resection of an ependymoma, the answer is no. Adjuvant focal radiation is standard even after complete resection. The recurrence rate remains thirty to forty percent even with gross total resection followed by radiation therapy. Serial surveillance MRI is essential for long-term follow-up.

Now we turn to diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered. This entity was formerly known as diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma, or DIPG, when it arises in the pons. The 2021 WHO classification unified these tumors under the molecular designation of diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered, regardless of anatomic location. These tumors can also arise in the thalamus and spinal cord, but the pontine location remains the most common and the most devastating.

The clinical presentation of a pontine diffuse midline glioma is a triad of cranial nerve palsies, long tract signs, and ataxia, developing over a short period of weeks. MRI shows a diffusely expanding lesion centered in the pons, typically T2 hyperintense with variable and patchy enhancement. The tumor engulfs the basilar artery rather than displacing it. The molecular hallmark is the H3 K27M mutation, a histone mutation that drives epigenetic reprogramming.

The prognosis is uniformly fatal. Median survival is nine to eleven months from diagnosis. Stereotactic biopsy is increasingly performed to confirm the molecular diagnosis and determine clinical trial eligibility. Treatment consists of radiation therapy to a dose of 54 Gray, which is the only intervention that provides temporary clinical improvement, typically lasting a few months. There is no effective chemotherapy for this disease.

The examiner will set a trap here. If asked whether you would attempt resection of a diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma, the answer is absolutely not. This tumor is diffusely infiltrative throughout the brainstem. Resection is impossible and would cause devastating neurological deficits. Surgery has no role beyond stereotactic biopsy for tissue diagnosis and molecular characterization. This is a question designed to see whether you will make a catastrophic surgical decision under pressure.

Let's review the key evidence you should be able to cite. Packer's work established reduced-dose craniospinal irradiation at 23.4 Gray combined with chemotherapy as the standard of care for standard-risk medulloblastoma, demonstrating equivalent survival with reduced neurocognitive toxicity compared to the prior 36 Gray dose. Taylor's 2012 publication defined the four molecular subgroups of medulloblastoma and demonstrated that molecular classification is a better predictor of outcome than histology alone. Merchant's St. Jude series established conformal radiation therapy as the standard adjuvant treatment for ependymoma after gross total resection, documenting excellent local control rates. Mackay's work characterized the H3 K27M mutation as the defining molecular feature of diffuse midline gliomas, leading to the reclassification in the WHO system. The cooperative group frameworks you should reference are SIOP, which is the International Society of Paediatric Oncology, and COG, the Children's Oncology Group, which coordinate the multicenter trials that define treatment standards.

Let's close with the board pearls you must carry into the examination room.

Medulloblastoma has four molecular subgroups. WNT has the best prognosis at greater than ninety percent five-year survival. Group 3 with MYC amplification has the worst at approximately fifty percent. SHH and Group 4 are intermediate.

For ependymoma, gross total resection is the single most important prognostic factor. If you get a subtotal resection, consider taking the patient back for re-resection before radiation. Adjuvant treatment is focal radiation, not craniospinal irradiation, and chemotherapy has no proven role.

Never resect a diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma. Biopsy for molecular characterization, then radiation to 54 Gray. Median survival is nine to eleven months regardless of treatment.

Cerebellar mutism syndrome occurs in twenty to twenty-five percent of children after posterior fossa surgery. Use the telovelar approach to avoid vermis splitting. Onset is typically one to two days postoperatively.

Craniospinal irradiation dosing is 23.4 Gray for standard-risk medulloblastoma and 36 Gray for high-risk, with a posterior fossa boost to 54 to 55.8 Gray. In children younger than three, avoid craniospinal irradiation entirely and use chemotherapy-only protocols.

Risk stratification hinges on four factors: age, extent of resection, metastatic status, and molecular features including histologic subtype and MYC amplification. Greater than 1.5 square centimeters of residual tumor defines subtotal resection and elevates the patient to high risk.

That covers pediatric brain tumors. You're ready for this one.
