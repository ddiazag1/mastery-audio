questions:
  - q: Give me the Knosp classification.
    a: Grades cavernous sinus invasion against three lines on the cavernous carotid: the medial tangent, the intercarotid line through the vessel centres, and the lateral tangent. Grade 0: tumour medial to the medial tangent, no invasion. Grade 1: past the medial tangent but not beyond the intercarotid line. Grade 2: past the intercarotid line but not beyond the lateral tangent. Grade 3: lateral to the lateral tangent. Grade 4: total encasement of the cavernous segment of the internal carotid.
    probe: Which grades are effectively unresectable for cure?
    probeA: Grades 3 and 4. Grade 4, with total encasement of the cavernous carotid, is essentially never cured surgically, with gross total resection and endocrine remission rates approaching zero. Grade 3 is close behind, and the 2016 subdivision is worth knowing: grade 3A, invading the superior compartment of the cavernous sinus, has a substantially lower true invasion rate at around twenty-six percent than grade 3B, invading the inferior compartment, at around seventy percent, so 3A is meaningfully more favourable than 3B. Grades 0 to 2 have high rates of gross total resection and remission.
  - q: Which Knosp grades change your operative goal?
    a: Three and four are essentially unresectable for cure via surgery. That does not mean you do not operate — you may still debulk for mass effect or chiasmal compression — but the conversation about biochemical cure in a functioning adenoma changes entirely, and adjuvant medical or radiation therapy becomes central.
    probe: How would you counsel a Cushing's patient with a Knosp 4 tumour?
    probeA: Honestly, and by reframing the goal before the operation rather than after it. I would explain that with total carotid encasement, surgery is very unlikely to achieve biochemical remission, so the aim is maximal safe debulking to reduce the tumour burden and relieve any mass effect, not cure. I would then set out the multimodal plan in advance: debulking improves the response to subsequent radiotherapy or radiosurgery and to medical therapy, and medical options include ketoconazole, metyrapone, osilodrostat or mifepristone for cortisol control, with pasireotide acting on the tumour. I would raise bilateral adrenalectomy as a definitive option for uncontrolled hypercortisolism, with the caveat of Nelson syndrome. And I would emphasise that uncontrolled Cushing's is itself lethal, so doing nothing is not a neutral option.
  - q: Which functioning adenoma is not primarily surgical?
    a: Prolactinoma. First-line treatment is a dopamine agonist — cabergoline — which achieves biochemical control and tumour shrinkage in the large majority, even in giant tumours with chiasmal compression. Operating first on a prolactinoma is a classic error.
    probe: When would you operate on a prolactinoma?
    probeA: When medical therapy fails or cannot be tolerated: genuine dopamine agonist resistance, which is uncommon but real, or intolerable side effects including nausea, postural symptoms, or impulse control disorders such as pathological gambling and hypersexuality, which must be asked about specifically. Also apoplexy with visual compromise, a cerebrospinal fluid leak arising as the tumour shrinks away from an eroded skull base, which is a recognised complication of successful medical therapy in a large invasive tumour, a cystic tumour that will not shrink, and a patient with progressive visual loss who is not responding quickly enough. Patient preference for surgery over lifelong medication is a legitimate reason in a surgically favourable microadenoma with high expected remission rates.
  - q: What must be checked before any pituitary operation?
    a: Full anterior pituitary axis assessment and formal visual fields with acuity. Endocrine because unrecognised hypocortisolism is lethal perioperatively and must be replaced before surgery; visual fields because they are both an indication and the baseline against which you judge success.
    probe: Which hormone do you replace before anything else?
    probeA: Cortisol, meaning hydrocortisone, and before thyroxine. The reason for the order is that giving thyroxine first to a patient with unrecognised adrenal insufficiency increases the metabolic rate and accelerates cortisol clearance, which can precipitate an adrenal crisis. So I establish and treat the adrenal axis first, then replace thyroid hormone. The same principle applies in any panhypopituitary patient, and it is the sort of detail that separates a safe answer from a dangerous one. Sex steroids and growth hormone are deferred until after the operation.
  - q: Describe pituitary apoplexy and its management.
    a: Sudden headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia and altered consciousness from haemorrhage or infarction into an adenoma. Immediate priority is steroid replacement, because acute hypoadrenalism kills. Urgent surgical decompression is indicated for visual compromise or deteriorating consciousness; selected patients without visual loss can be managed conservatively.
    probe: Which cranial nerve is most often affected and why?
    probeA: The third nerve, because it lies in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus most medially and is therefore the closest to an expanding sellar mass. Clinically that gives ptosis, a dilated pupil and a down-and-out eye. The fourth and sixth nerves and the first two divisions of the trigeminal can also be affected, and the sixth nerve is worth noting as an anatomical curiosity because it runs freely within the sinus adjacent to the carotid rather than in the wall, yet the third is still the one most commonly involved in apoplexy.
  - q: Endoscopic endonasal or transcranial?
    a: Endonasal for the great majority — sellar and suprasellar extension with a favourable midline configuration. Transcranial is reserved for significant lateral extension beyond the carotid, a dumbbell configuration with a narrow waist at the diaphragma, or fibrous tumours that will not descend, where pulling blindly risks the optic apparatus.
    probe: What anatomical variant makes the endonasal route hazardous?
    probeA: A pair of medially deviated or kissing carotid arteries, where the intercarotid distance at the sella is narrowed and the vessels lie in or close to the midline corridor. That makes the standard sellar opening hazardous and can make a safe exposure impossible without navigation and Doppler. Other important variants are a conchal or presellar sphenoid sinus, which lacks the usual pneumatisation and landmarks and requires far more drilling; sphenoid septations inserting directly onto the carotid canal, where breaking a septum can tear the artery; a dehiscent bony carotid canal; and an anteriorly placed or prefixed chiasm. I would review a fine-cut CT alongside the MRI specifically to look for these before every case.
  - q: What are the specific complications you consent for?
    a: Cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea and the associated meningitis risk; diabetes insipidus, usually transient; hypopituitarism requiring replacement; visual deterioration; carotid injury, which is rare and catastrophic; epistaxis; and sinonasal morbidity including altered smell.
    probe: How do you manage a postoperative CSF leak?
    probeA: By grading and escalating. A low-flow leak recognised intraoperatively is repaired at the time with a multilayer closure using fat, fascia or a collagen matrix, and for a high-flow leak or a wide arachnoid defect, a vascularised nasoseptal flap, which has transformed leak rates. Postoperatively, a suspected leak is confirmed with beta-2 transferrin rather than a bedside glucose test, and localised with thin-slice CT and heavy T2 MRI or CT cisternography. Initial management is bed rest, head elevation and avoidance of straining, nose blowing and Valsalva, with a lumbar drain for two to five days in selected cases. A leak that persists beyond a few days, or any leak with meningitis, goes back to theatre for endoscopic re-exploration and definitive repair. I would not manage a persistent leak conservatively for weeks, because the meningitis risk is cumulative.
  - q: Sodium is 126 on day eight after transsphenoidal surgery. What is it?
    a: Delayed hyponatraemia, most often the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis, which characteristically peaks around day seven to ten and is the commonest cause of readmission after transsphenoidal surgery. Distinguish from cerebral salt wasting by volume status; treat SIADH with fluid restriction and correct slowly.
    probe: How fast may you correct it?
    probeA: By no more than about eight to ten millimoles per litre in the first twenty-four hours, and I would aim lower, around six to eight, in a patient at high risk of osmotic demyelination, which means malnutrition, alcoholism, liver disease, hypokalaemia or a profoundly low starting sodium. The practical management of the common case, which is SIADH, is fluid restriction to around one litre a day with daily or twice-daily sodium monitoring, since over-rapid correction is more often caused by a spontaneous water diuresis as the SIADH resolves than by anything I give. If the sodium rises too fast I would stop the correction and consider giving hypotonic fluid or desmopressin to relower it, which is an accepted rescue. Hypertonic saline is reserved for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia with seizures or a reduced conscious level, given as a small controlled bolus. And I would recheck the cortisol, because hypoadrenalism causes hyponatraemia and is the reversible cause that must not be missed in this specific population.

Most often the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis; the alternative is cerebral salt wasting, distinguished by volume status — SIADH is euvolaemic or mildly hypervolaemic, salt wasting is hypovolaemic. SIADH is treated with fluid restriction, salt wasting with salt and volume. Either way I would correct by no more than ten to twelve millimoles per litre in twenty-four hours to avoid osmotic demyelination, and recheck his cortisol, because hypoadrenalism also causes hyponatraemia.

Let's talk about pituitary adenoma approaches. This is a high-yield oral board topic because it tests your endocrine knowledge, your surgical anatomy, your judgment about when to operate and when not to operate, and your ability to handle complications. There are several classic traps the examiners love, and we are going to walk through every one of them.

Starting with the foundation. Pituitary adenomas are the most common sellar mass and account for ten to fifteen percent of all intracranial tumors. On incidental MRI, the prevalence is roughly ten percent, meaning these lesions are extremely common in the general population, and most of them are clinically silent. Keep that in mind, because the prevalence of incidentalomas is a key factor in how you interpret imaging findings when working up endocrine disorders. Size classification matters. A microadenoma is less than ten millimeters. A macroadenoma is ten millimeters or greater. A giant adenoma is forty millimeters or greater. These size cutoffs are not arbitrary. They directly influence surgical planning, expected outcomes, and recurrence risk. Microadenomas, particularly functional ones, have the highest surgical cure rates, often exceeding ninety percent for experienced surgeons. Macroadenomas have lower cure rates, and giant adenomas present significant challenges for complete resection, often requiring staged procedures or multimodal treatment.

Now, the most important classification is functional versus non-functional. About sixty-five percent of pituitary adenomas are functional, meaning they actively secrete hormones. The remaining thirty-five percent are non-functional, sometimes called null cell adenomas or clinically non-functioning adenomas. Among the functional types, here is the frequency breakdown you need to know. Prolactinomas are the most common, representing forty to forty-five percent of all pituitary adenomas and the most common functional subtype by a wide margin. Growth hormone-secreting adenomas causing acromegaly account for about twenty percent. ACTH-secreting adenomas causing Cushing disease represent ten to fifteen percent. TSH-secreting adenomas, also called thyrotropinomas, are rare, less than one percent. Gonadotropinomas are rarely clinically functional, though they are common among non-functional adenomas on immunohistochemistry.

Non-functional adenomas present with mass effect. The classic visual field deficit is bitemporal hemianopsia from chiasmal compression. The anatomy here matters. The tumor grows superiorly out of the sella and compresses the optic chiasm from below. The crossing fibers from the inferonasal retina, which correspond to the superior temporal visual fields, are affected first. So the earliest deficit is in the superior temporal quadrants bilaterally, progressing to full bitemporal hemianopsia. Other mass effect symptoms include headache, hypopituitarism from either the stalk effect or direct gland compression, and cavernous sinus invasion producing cranial nerve palsies involving cranial nerves three, four, the first and second divisions of five, and six.

Two classifications you must know cold. The Hardy classification grades sellar anatomy. Grade zero is a normal sella with a microadenoma inside. Grade one is an intact sella with slight sellar enlargement. Grade two is an enlarged sella with intact floor. Grade three shows localized sellar floor erosion. Grade four is diffuse sellar floor destruction. The Knosp classification grades cavernous sinus invasion and is arguably more important for surgical planning. Knosp Grade zero means no cavernous sinus invasion. Grade one means the tumor extends past the medial tangent line, drawn along the medial wall of the cavernous carotid, but not beyond the intercarotid line joining the centres of the two carotids. Grade two means the tumor extends past the intercarotid line but not beyond the lateral tangent line. Grade three means the tumor extends lateral to the lateral tangent line. Grade four means total encasement of the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery. This is critical. Knosp three and four tumors are essentially unresectable for cure via surgery. You may debulk them, but you are not going to achieve gross total resection without unacceptable risk to the carotid artery.

Now let's move into the clinical case. A thirty-eight-year-old woman presents with amenorrhea, galactorrhea, and headaches for one year. Visual field testing with Humphrey perimetry reveals bitemporal hemianopsia. MRI shows a two-point-eight centimeter sellar and suprasellar mass with heterogeneous enhancement. The optic chiasm is compressed and displaced superiorly. Laboratory evaluation reveals a prolactin level of three hundred eighty nanograms per milliliter, with the normal being less than twenty-five. The remaining pituitary hormones show low FSH and LH, low free T4, and a normal cortisol. Knosp grading is Grade two. So you are looking at a macroprolactinoma with visual compromise and partial hypopituitarism from mass effect.

Here is where the examiner interrogation begins, and the first question will test whether you make the most common mistake candidates make with this exact scenario.

The examiner asks, "What is your endocrine workup for a sellar mass?" This is required for all sellar masses regardless of how obvious the diagnosis seems. You need prolactin, IGF-1 to screen for growth hormone excess, a morning cortisol or alternatively a twenty-four-hour urine free cortisol or a one milligram overnight dexamethasone suppression test to screen for Cushing syndrome, TSH and free T4 to assess both thyroid function and to screen for a rare TSH-secreting adenoma, FSH and LH, and either testosterone in men or estradiol in women. You also need formal visual field testing with Humphrey perimetry. Skip none of these. Even if you think you know the diagnosis, the examiner expects a complete workup.

Then comes the key question. "This is a macroprolactinoma with a prolactin of three hundred eighty. What is your first-line treatment?" The answer is medical therapy with a dopamine agonist. Not surgery. This is the single most important point in the entire pituitary adenoma topic. Prolactinomas are the only pituitary adenoma subtype where medication is first-line, even in the setting of visual field deficits. The drug is cabergoline, started at zero-point-two-five milligrams twice weekly and titrated upward based on prolactin levels and tumor response. Cabergoline is preferred over bromocriptine because of superior efficacy, better tolerability, and a more convenient dosing schedule. Even with bitemporal hemianopsia, dopamine agonists can shrink prolactinomas rapidly. Visual improvement often begins within days to weeks of starting treatment. Surgery is reserved for specific scenarios: medication intolerance, medication failure or resistance defined as inability to normalize prolactin or achieve adequate tumor shrinkage, CSF leak that develops as the tumor shrinks away from the skull base, patient preference after a thorough informed consent discussion, or pituitary apoplexy with acute neurological decline.

Now, the examiner will almost certainly probe the distinction between stalk effect and prolactinoma, because this is a classic oral board trap. Any sellar mass, whether it is a non-functional adenoma, a craniopharyngioma, a meningioma, or a Rathke cleft cyst, can elevate prolactin by compressing the pituitary stalk and blocking the normal dopamine inhibition of prolactin secretion from the anterior pituitary. However, stalk effect typically produces prolactin levels less than one hundred fifty to two hundred nanograms per milliliter. A prolactin level greater than two hundred almost always indicates a true prolactinoma. In this case, the patient has a prolactin of three hundred eighty with a two-point-eight centimeter mass, and that is entirely consistent with a macroprolactinoma, not stalk effect. The prolactin level correlates roughly with tumor volume in true prolactinomas.

The examiner then shifts. "When is surgery first-line for a pituitary adenoma?" You need to rattle these off cleanly. Surgery is first-line for non-functional macroadenomas causing visual compromise or other significant mass effect. Surgery is first-line for growth hormone-secreting adenomas causing acromegaly, where even a successful debulking can improve response rates to adjuvant medical therapy with somatostatin analogs like octreotide or lanreotide. Surgery is first-line for ACTH-secreting adenomas causing Cushing disease, where drug therapy with agents like ketoconazole, metyrapone, or pasireotide is considered second-line or bridging therapy, not a substitute for definitive surgical treatment. Surgery is first-line for TSH-secreting adenomas. And surgery is indicated urgently in pituitary apoplexy with progressive visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, or altered consciousness.

Let's discuss the surgical approach. The transsphenoidal approach is the workhorse, and the modern standard at most high-volume centers is the endoscopic endonasal technique using a binostril approach. Compared to the traditional microscopic sublabial or transnasal technique, the endoscopic approach provides superior visualization, a wider panoramic angle, and the ability to use angled endoscopes to inspect around corners for residual tumor. Here are the key steps. You begin with a posterior septectomy to create a common nasal corridor for binostril access. Then a wide sphenoidotomy is performed to expose the anterior wall of the sella. Critical landmarks must be identified at this stage: the bilateral carotid prominences, the opticocarotid recesses which mark the junction of the optic canal and carotid artery and serve as the superolateral boundary of your safe working corridor, and the clival recess inferiorly. Once landmarks are confirmed, the sellar floor is opened widely. The dura is opened in a cruciate fashion. Tumor removal proceeds with extracapsular dissection when possible, using ring curettes and angled suctions. After the bulk of tumor is removed, you use a thirty-degree and forty-five-degree angled endoscope to inspect for residual tumor in the suprasellar space, the lateral recesses, and the cavernous sinus margins. Reconstruction depends on whether a CSF leak is present. If there is an intraoperative CSF leak, you reconstruct with an inlay of fat graft harvested from the abdomen, a fascial graft, and a vascularized nasoseptal flap, known as the Hadad-Bassagasteguy flap, which is based on the posterior septal artery, a branch of the sphenopalatine artery. This flap should be raised at the beginning of the case in any operation where a CSF leak is anticipated.

When do you choose a transcranial approach instead? There are specific indications. Significant lateral extension beyond the cavernous sinus into the middle fossa. A large suprasellar component with a narrow waist at the diaphragma sellae, creating an hourglass configuration where the suprasellar portion will not deliver inferiorly through the diaphragmatic opening. Or a firm, fibrous tumor that cannot be delivered through the transsphenoidal corridor despite adequate exposure. The most common transcranial approaches for these situations are the pterional or orbitozygomatic craniotomy, which provide excellent access to the suprasellar cistern and allow direct visualization of the optic nerves and chiasm during dissection. Some surgeons also use a subfrontal approach for tumors with predominantly anterior suprasellar extension. In practice, the vast majority of pituitary adenomas, probably greater than ninety-five percent at high-volume centers, are managed transsphenoidally. The decision to go transcranial should be a deliberate one based on imaging characteristics, not a fallback because of limited endonasal experience.

The examiner now asks about remission criteria, and these are specific to each tumor type. For prolactinomas, remission means a normal prolactin level while off dopamine agonist therapy. For acromegaly, remission requires a random growth hormone level less than one nanogram per milliliter, a nadir growth hormone on oral glucose tolerance test less than zero-point-four nanograms per milliliter, and a normal age- and sex-matched IGF-1 level. For Cushing disease, the postoperative nadir cortisol must be less than two micrograms per deciliter, and the lower the nadir, the more durable the remission. Patients who achieve a nadir cortisol of less than two will need hydrocortisone replacement therapy because the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis will be suppressed, and recovery takes months to as long as a year or more.

Now let's cover pituitary apoplexy, because the examiner will bring it up. Apoplexy is hemorrhage or infarction within a pituitary adenoma. Risk factors include anticoagulation, pregnancy, dopamine agonist therapy in large prolactinomas, dynamic endocrine testing, and major surgery. The presentation is dramatic: sudden severe headache, often described as thunderclap, acute visual loss, ophthalmoplegia from cavernous sinus compression, altered level of consciousness, and potentially hemodynamic instability from acute adrenal crisis due to destruction of ACTH-secreting tissue. The differential diagnosis includes subarachnoid hemorrhage, and a CT head may show hemorrhage in the sella, but MRI is the imaging modality of choice if the patient is stable. On MRI, acute hemorrhage appears hyperintense on T1-weighted sequences. Management begins immediately with intravenous stress-dose hydrocortisone, one hundred milligrams IV bolus followed by fifty milligrams IV every eight hours. You do not wait for lab confirmation of adrenal insufficiency in this setting. Draw the labs, but treat empirically. After hemodynamic stabilization, decision-making depends on neurological status. Urgent surgical decompression via the transsphenoidal approach is indicated if there is progressive visual deterioration or altered consciousness. The Pituitary Apoplexy Score, described by Rajasekaran, can help guide this decision, incorporating visual acuity, visual field deficits, and cranial nerve palsies. Medical management with high-dose steroids and close neurological and endocrine monitoring is appropriate if the visual deficits are mild and stable. Patients managed conservatively often recover cranial nerve function over weeks, though they will likely still need elective surgery later for the underlying adenoma.

Here come the trap questions. The examiner says, "The prolactin is eighty-five nanograms per milliliter and the tumor is three centimeters. Is this a prolactinoma?" The answer is almost certainly no. A prolactin of only eighty-five for a three-centimeter sellar mass is a red flag for stalk effect, not a true prolactinoma. A genuine prolactinoma of that size should produce a prolactin level well above two hundred, often above one thousand. This discordance between a relatively low prolactin and a large tumor strongly suggests a non-functional adenoma causing stalk effect. This patient needs surgery, not cabergoline. If you put this patient on a dopamine agonist, the prolactin might normalize, but the tumor will not shrink because it is not a prolactinoma, and the patient will lose time while the optic chiasm remains compressed.

The second trap. "The ACTH is elevated, cortisol is elevated, and MRI shows a four-millimeter pituitary lesion. Is this Cushing disease?" Do not fall into the trap of assuming correlation. Incidental pituitary microadenomas are found in up to ten percent of the population. You must first confirm Cushing syndrome with proper screening tests: twenty-four-hour urine free cortisol on at least two occasions, late-night salivary cortisol on at least two occasions, and the one-milligram overnight dexamethasone suppression test. If Cushing syndrome is confirmed biochemically, you then need to establish the source. A high-dose dexamethasone suppression test can help differentiate pituitary from ectopic ACTH production. The definitive test to confirm a pituitary source is inferior petrosal sinus sampling with CRH stimulation. A central-to-peripheral ACTH ratio greater than two at baseline or greater than three after CRH administration confirms a pituitary source.

The third trap. "Postoperatively, the patient develops diabetes insipidus. How do you manage it?" Transient diabetes insipidus occurs in ten to twenty percent of transsphenoidal cases. You diagnose it by urine output exceeding three hundred milliliters per hour, urine specific gravity less than one-point-zero-zero-five, and a rising serum sodium. Treatment is DDAVP, either one microgram intravenously or ten micrograms intranasally. You must monitor sodium closely because of the triphasic response pattern. The first phase is diabetes insipidus from direct injury to the posterior pituitary or stalk, occurring in the first one to two days. The second phase is SIADH, occurring around days four to nine, caused by uncontrolled release of stored vasopressin from dying posterior pituitary neurons. If you continue DDAVP through this phase, you will cause dangerous hyponatremia. The third phase is permanent diabetes insipidus if the injury is severe enough, requiring long-term DDAVP replacement.

Rounding out the complications discussion. CSF leak occurs in three to five percent of standard transsphenoidal cases and is higher for extended approaches. If a postoperative CSF leak is identified, initial management includes lumbar drainage for forty-eight to seventy-two hours. If that fails, return to the operating room for re-exploration and repair with a nasoseptal flap if one was not placed initially. Postoperative hypopituitarism with new hormonal deficits occurs in five to ten percent, and patients require long-term endocrine follow-up with hormone replacement as needed, including thyroid hormone, cortisol, sex hormones, and growth hormone. Epistaxis is usually minor but occasionally requires nasal packing. Carotid artery injury is rare, occurring in zero-point-five to one percent, but it is catastrophic when it happens. If you encounter brisk arterial bleeding during a transsphenoidal approach, you pack the sella immediately with hemostatic material, do not attempt to clip or cauterize the carotid, and obtain emergent angiography for possible endovascular sacrifice or covered stent placement. A preoperative CT angiogram is wise for revision cases or tumors with significant cavernous sinus invasion to identify a dehiscent or ectatic carotid. Meningitis risk is low but present, particularly with CSF leaks, and perioperative antibiotics are routinely administered though the evidence base is debated. Visual worsening can occur from direct surgical injury, hematoma in the tumor bed, or vasospasm of the superior hypophyseal arteries supplying the chiasm.

Let's now cover the key evidence you should be able to reference. The Endocrine Society clinical practice guidelines authored by Melmed and Casanueva provide the framework for prolactinoma management, emphasizing dopamine agonists as first-line therapy and defining the criteria for medication resistance. The Endocrine Society guidelines for acromegaly and Cushing disease outline diagnostic algorithms and remission criteria. For acromegaly specifically, the guidelines emphasize that surgical cure rates for microadenomas exceed eighty-five percent at experienced centers, while macroadenoma cure rates drop to forty to sixty percent, making adjuvant therapy with somatostatin analogs, GH receptor antagonists like pegvisomant, or radiation frequently necessary. The Congress of Neurological Surgeons published a systematic review on the transsphenoidal approach that supports endoscopic techniques as equivalent or superior to microscopic approaches in terms of extent of resection and complication rates for most adenoma subtypes. Importantly, surgeon volume and institutional experience are among the strongest predictors of outcomes, with high-volume pituitary surgeons defined as those performing more than fifty cases per year consistently achieving superior results. The Knosp classification has been validated in multiple large surgical series as a reliable predictor of gross total resection feasibility, with Knosp three and four grades predicting significantly lower rates of complete tumor removal. For adjuvant radiation, stereotactic radiosurgery with Gamma Knife or CyberKnife is the preferred modality for residual or recurrent adenomas, with the key constraint being adequate distance from the optic apparatus, typically requiring at least three to five millimeters of separation to deliver a safe and effective dose of twelve to sixteen Gray in a single fraction.

Closing with the board pearls you need to walk in with. A prolactin level above two hundred nanograms per milliliter means prolactinoma. Do not operate first. Start cabergoline. A prolactin below one hundred fifty to two hundred in the setting of a large sellar mass is stalk effect from a non-functional adenoma, and that patient needs surgery, not medication. Remission criteria are specific to each tumor type: normalize prolactin off medication for prolactinomas, achieve a GH nadir below zero-point-four on OGTT with a normal IGF-1 for acromegaly, and achieve a cortisol nadir below two postoperatively for Cushing disease. Inferior petrosal sinus sampling with CRH stimulation is the gold standard to confirm a pituitary source in Cushing syndrome, and you should never skip it just because there is a visible lesion on MRI. The Knosp classification predicts surgical resectability, with grades three and four essentially precluding gross total resection. Pituitary apoplexy demands immediate stress-dose steroids before anything else. Always remember the triphasic response of diabetes insipidus after transsphenoidal surgery so you do not overcorrect with DDAVP during the SIADH phase. And one more pearl: for postoperative surveillance, MRI should be obtained at three months after surgery for all tumor types, with subsequent imaging intervals dictated by hormonal status and presence of residual tumor. Patients with residual non-functional adenoma should be followed with annual MRI for at least five years, then every two years if stable.
