  - q: Before any numbers: what is spinal stability?

It is a judgement about whether the spine can bear physiological load without progressive deformity, neurological injury or incapacitating pain, drawn from bony integrity, ligamentous integrity, and neurological status. Every classification is a structured way of combining those three.

    a: Pelvic incidence equals pelvic tilt plus sacral slope. The targets are sagittal vertical axis under five centimetres, pelvic tilt under twenty degrees, and pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis under ten degrees.
    probe: Which of those three is fixed and cannot be changed by surgery?
  - q: A patient has a high pelvic tilt. What does that tell you clinically?
    a: He is compensating: retroverting the pelvis to keep the trunk over the hips. Other compensatory mechanisms are knee flexion, ankle dorsiflexion, thoracic hypokyphosis and cervical hyperlordosis. Once exhausted, he presents stooped and unable to stand for long.

Let's master spinal imaging, alignment, and instability parameters for the oral boards.

Spine cases in the oral examination almost always begin with imaging, and the examiner is measuring something specific: can you look at a radiograph or a reconstruction and decide whether this spine is stable. That decision drives everything downstream. Candidates who can quote a measurement and then justify a decision do well. Candidates who describe the pathology without ever addressing stability get taken apart. Let's build the measurements and then the judgment.

Start with the craniocervical junction, because it has the most measurements and the highest consequence of error.

The basion dental interval is the distance from the basion, the anterior margin of the foramen magnum, to the tip of the dens. In adults it should be less than roughly twelve millimeters, and in children less than roughly ten to twelve millimeters. An increased basion dental interval indicates craniocervical distraction, which is a highly unstable and frequently fatal injury, and it is the single most important measurement in the pediatric trauma patient because children dislocate at the craniocervical junction far more readily than adults.

The Powers ratio is the distance from the basion to the posterior arch of the atlas divided by the distance from the opisthion to the anterior arch of the atlas. A ratio greater than one suggests anterior atlanto-occipital dislocation. It is less reliable than the basion dental interval and misses posterior and distraction injuries, so mention it but lead with the basion dental interval.

The atlantodental interval is the distance between the anterior arch of the atlas and the anterior surface of the dens. In adults it should be less than approximately three millimeters, and in children up to approximately five millimeters because of ligamentous laxity. Widening indicates transverse ligament insufficiency, which you see in trauma, in rheumatoid arthritis with pannus, and in Down syndrome. The transverse ligament is the primary stabilizer of the atlantoaxial joint, and its incompetence makes the joint unstable regardless of bony integrity. In the setting of a Jefferson burst fracture of the atlas, the rule of Spence states that if the combined lateral overhang of the lateral masses of the atlas on the axis exceeds roughly seven millimeters on an open mouth view, the transverse ligament is likely disrupted, which historically pushed toward halo immobilization or fusion rather than a collar. Modern practice weights magnetic resonance evidence of ligamentous integrity heavily, and you should say that.

Know the odontoid fracture classification by Anderson and D'Alonzo. Type one is an avulsion of the tip, generally stable but raises the question of associated craniocervical distraction. Type two is a fracture through the base of the dens, the most common and the most problematic because of a high nonunion rate, particularly with displacement greater than roughly five millimeters, angulation greater than roughly ten degrees, age over fifty, and posterior displacement. Type three extends into the body of the axis and has a better healing rate because of the broad cancellous surface. The examiner will push you on the elderly type two fracture, where the decision between surgical fixation and external immobilization is genuinely contested and where halo immobilization in the elderly carries significant morbidity and mortality. Have a reasoned position.

Now the subaxial cervical spine. On a lateral radiograph, trace four lines: the anterior vertebral line, the posterior vertebral line, the spinolaminar line, and the tips of the spinous processes. Any step off indicates subluxation. The prevertebral soft tissue should measure less than roughly six millimeters at the level of the second cervical vertebra and less than roughly twenty two millimeters at the sixth, a rule sometimes remembered as six at two and two at six centimeters. Widening suggests prevertebral hematoma and therefore significant injury.

The historical White and Panjabi criteria for subaxial instability assign points for anterior and posterior element destruction, sagittal plane translation greater than three point five millimeters, sagittal plane rotation greater than eleven degrees, positive stretch test, spinal cord or root damage, abnormal disc narrowing, and anticipated dangerous loading, with a total of five or more indicating instability. Know the translation and angulation numbers specifically, because those are the ones asked.

Modern practice uses the Subaxial Cervical Spine Injury Classification, which scores morphology as compression, burst, distraction, or translation and rotation, scores discoligamentous complex integrity as intact, indeterminate, or disrupted, and scores neurologic status. A total of four or more generally favors surgery, three or less favors nonoperative management, and four is the discretionary zone. Be able to state the three components and the threshold.

For the thoracolumbar spine, the Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score works analogously, scoring morphology, posterior ligamentous complex integrity, and neurologic status, with four or more favoring surgery. The AO Spine classification divides injuries into type A compression, type B distraction or tension band failure, and type C translation or displacement, with type C being the most unstable. The key conceptual point across all of these systems is that the posterior ligamentous complex, comprising the supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, and facet capsules, is the structure whose integrity determines stability, and that magnetic resonance with fat suppressed T2 or short tau inversion recovery sequences is how you assess it. Interspinous widening on plain imaging, facet joint diastasis, and a naked facet sign on axial CT are all surrogates for posterior ligamentous complex failure.

Now sagittal alignment, which dominates the deformity portion of any spine discussion. You must know these parameters and their relationships.

Pelvic incidence is a fixed anatomic parameter, the angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and a line from that midpoint to the center of the femoral heads. It does not change with position and it does not change after skeletal maturity. Normal is roughly fifty degrees plus or minus ten.

Pelvic tilt is the angle between the vertical and a line from the midpoint of the sacral endplate to the femoral heads. It is positional and represents pelvic retroversion, the body's compensation for sagittal imbalance. Normal is roughly less than twenty degrees, and an elevated pelvic tilt means the patient is working to stay upright.

Sacral slope is the angle between the sacral endplate and the horizontal. The fundamental relationship is that pelvic incidence equals pelvic tilt plus sacral slope. Memorize that equation, because examiners ask it directly.

Lumbar lordosis should approximately match the pelvic incidence. The mismatch, expressed as pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis, should ideally be less than roughly ten degrees. A large mismatch indicates the patient does not have enough lordosis for their pelvic anatomy and will compensate by retroverting the pelvis and flexing the knees.

The sagittal vertical axis is the horizontal offset between a plumb line dropped from the center of the seventh cervical vertebral body and the posterosuperior corner of the sacrum. Normal is roughly less than five centimeters. Greater values correlate with pain and disability.

These three targets, sagittal vertical axis less than five centimeters, pelvic tilt less than twenty degrees, and pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis less than ten degrees, are the surgical goals of adult deformity correction and you should be able to recite them.

For coronal alignment and scoliosis, measure the Cobb angle between the superior endplate of the most tilted upper vertebra and the inferior endplate of the most tilted lower vertebra. Know that a curve greater than roughly ten degrees defines scoliosis, that bracing is generally considered in the skeletally immature patient with curves in the twenty five to forty degree range, and that surgical correction is generally considered above roughly fifty degrees in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. Use the Risser sign, grading iliac apophyseal ossification from zero to five, to estimate remaining skeletal growth and therefore progression risk.

For spondylolisthesis, grade by the Meyerding classification based on the percentage of anterior translation of the vertebral body relative to the one below: grade one is up to twenty five percent, grade two up to fifty, grade three up to seventy five, grade four up to one hundred, and grade five is spondyloptosis, complete translation. Assess the slip angle and the pelvic incidence, because high grade slips are associated with elevated pelvic incidence.

Now the crucial concept of dynamic instability. A static study can be entirely normal in a spine that is unstable. Flexion and extension radiographs assess for motion, and the commonly cited threshold for lumbar instability is translation greater than roughly four millimeters or angular change greater than roughly ten degrees between adjacent levels. Be careful, however: flexion extension imaging in the acute trauma setting is unreliable because muscle spasm splints the spine and produces false negatives, and it is dangerous in a patient with a suspected unstable injury. Say that. In the acute setting, magnetic resonance assessment of the ligaments is the appropriate study.

Understand what each modality contributes. Plain radiographs give you alignment under physiologic load, which is why standing films are essential for deformity work and why a supine magnetic resonance study underestimates listhesis. Computed tomography gives you bone detail, fracture morphology, facet anatomy, fusion assessment, and pedicle dimensions for instrumentation planning. Magnetic resonance gives you the neural elements, the disc, the ligaments, marrow signal, and soft tissue pathology. For suspected infection or tumor you need contrast enhanced magnetic resonance. For a patient who cannot undergo magnetic resonance, computed tomographic myelography is the alternative for assessing neural compression. Nuclear medicine bone scanning or single photon emission computed tomography can localize an active pars defect or a painful pseudarthrosis.

Before the measurements, fix the mental model. Stability is not a property you can read off a single image; it is a judgement about whether the spine can bear physiological load without progressive deformity, neurological injury or incapacitating pain. That judgement draws on three things: the integrity of the bony column, the integrity of the ligamentous restraints, and the neurological status of the patient. Every classification you are about to hear is simply a structured way of combining those three. If you say that sentence before you quote a single number, the examiner knows you understand what you are measuring.

Add the craniocervical measurements you have not yet covered, because this junction generates the most examination questions per centimetre of anatomy. The Wackenheim clival line, drawn along the posterior surface of the clivus, should fall tangential to the posterior aspect of the odontoid tip; if the dens crosses it, there is basilar invagination. McRae line spans the foramen magnum from basion to opisthion, and the odontoid tip should lie below it. Chamberlain line runs from the hard palate to the opisthion, and a dens tip more than roughly five millimetres above it indicates basilar invagination. The clivo-axial angle, between the clivus and the posterior surface of the odontoid and axis, is normally roughly one hundred fifty to one hundred eighty degrees, and values below about one hundred thirty five suggest pathological ventral brainstem compression and correlate with symptoms. Know that basilar invagination is the developmental upward migration of the odontoid, whereas basilar impression is the acquired form seen in Paget disease, osteomalacia, rickets and osteogenesis imperfecta.

For the subaxial spine, add the parameters that drive cervical deformity work. The C two to C seven sagittal vertical axis is a plumb line from the centroid of C two to the posterosuperior corner of C seven and should be under roughly four centimetres. The T one slope describes the tilt of the first thoracic endplate and behaves like a cervical analogue of pelvic incidence: a high T one slope requires more cervical lordosis to keep the head over the pelvis, and the T one slope minus cervical lordosis mismatch should ideally be under about fifteen to twenty degrees. The chin brow vertical angle measures horizontal gaze and is the functional parameter that matters most to a patient with a fixed chin on chest deformity. And the K line, drawn from the midpoints of the canal at C two and C seven, predicts whether posterior decompression alone will allow adequate cord drift back in ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament: if the ossified mass crosses the line, the patient is K line negative and an anterior or combined approach is generally required.

Quantify canal and cord compromise. The Torg Pavlov ratio is the sagittal canal diameter divided by the vertebral body diameter on a lateral radiograph, with a value below zero point eight suggesting developmental stenosis; it is sensitive but poorly specific and should be quoted with that caveat. On magnetic resonance, describe the degree of cord compression, the presence of cerebrospinal fluid effacement, and any intramedullary T2 hyperintensity, because cord signal change indicates myelomalacia or oedema and is associated with more severe deficit and less complete recovery after decompression. In thoracolumbar burst fractures, quantify canal compromise as a percentage and loss of anterior vertebral body height as a percentage, and note the segmental kyphosis, since those numbers feed directly into the classification scores.

Add the measurements specific to the sacrum and pelvis for deformity planning, and know that pelvic incidence is fixed and anatomic while pelvic tilt and sacral slope are positional, so a patient with high pelvic tilt is actively compensating and will fatigue. Compensatory mechanisms to look for on a standing full length film include pelvic retroversion, knee flexion, ankle dorsiflexion, thoracic hypokyphosis and cervical hyperlordosis; a patient who has exhausted these will present with a forward stooped posture and inability to stand for long. Always obtain standing full length thirty six inch films for deformity, because supine imaging systematically underestimates both deformity and listhesis.

Finally, know the imaging decision rules for clearing a cervical spine, because an examiner may open with a trauma case. The National Emergency X-Radiography Utilization Study low risk criteria permit clinical clearance without imaging if there is no midline cervical tenderness, no focal neurological deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, and no painful distracting injury. The Canadian C-spine rule is an alternative with slightly different structure. For the obtunded patient with a negative high quality computed tomography, current practice in most centres is to remove the collar on the basis of the computed tomography alone, though some still obtain magnetic resonance to assess the ligaments; state that this remains debated rather than asserting one answer. And never obtain flexion extension films in the acute setting, because muscle spasm splints the spine and produces false negatives while risking displacement of an unstable injury.

Finally, the examination behavior that earns credit. When you are handed a spine image, state the modality and the projection, identify the level precisely by counting from a fixed landmark, describe the alignment before the pathology, describe the pathology, then explicitly address stability and say what additional imaging you need to complete the assessment. The sentence that wins is something like: there is a burst fracture of the first lumbar vertebra with approximately forty percent loss of anterior vertebral body height, retropulsion of bone into the canal with roughly thirty percent canal compromise, and interspinous widening suggesting posterior ligamentous complex injury. I would obtain a magnetic resonance with short tau inversion recovery sequences to assess the posterior ligamentous complex, and if it is disrupted this becomes an unstable injury requiring stabilization. Alignment, pathology, stability, next study. Every time.
