Let's master carpal tunnel syndrome for the oral boards.

This is the most common entrapment neuropathy in the world and the most common peripheral nerve operation performed by neurosurgeons, which makes it a near certainty as an examination topic. The examiner is rarely testing whether you can release a carpal tunnel. They are testing whether you can confirm the diagnosis, exclude the mimics, justify the indication, and handle the patient who does not get better. That last scenario is the one that separates candidates. Let's build it properly.

Start with the anatomy, because every physical finding and every complication derives from it. The carpal tunnel is a fibro-osseous canal at the wrist. Its floor and walls are formed by the carpal bones arranged in an arch. Its roof is the transverse carpal ligament, also called the flexor retinaculum, which attaches radially to the scaphoid tubercle and the trapezium and ulnarly to the pisiform and the hook of the hamate. Through the tunnel pass ten structures: the four tendons of flexor digitorum superficialis, the four tendons of flexor digitorum profundus, the tendon of flexor pollicis longus, and the median nerve. The median nerve is the most superficial and most radial structure, lying immediately deep to the transverse carpal ligament, which is why it is the structure injured during release.

Two branches matter enormously. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve arises roughly five to seven centimeters proximal to the wrist crease and passes superficial to the transverse carpal ligament, not through the tunnel. Therefore sensation over the thenar eminence is spared in carpal tunnel syndrome. This is a crucial discriminator: if the patient has numbness over the thenar eminence, the lesion is proximal to the wrist, and you should be thinking about a more proximal median neuropathy, a cervical radiculopathy, or a brachial plexopathy. Say this out loud, because it is the classic examination pearl. It is also the branch injured by a poorly placed incision, producing a painful neuroma, which is why the incision is placed in line with the radial border of the ring finger, ulnar to the thenar crease.

The recurrent motor branch supplies the thenar muscles, specifically abductor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis, and the superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis. Its anatomy is variable: extraligamentous in roughly half, subligamentous in about a third, and transligamentous, meaning it pierces the transverse carpal ligament, in a minority. That transligamentous variant is precisely the one at risk during release, and naming it demonstrates real anatomic knowledge. The remaining thenar muscle, adductor pollicis, is ulnar innervated, which is why thumb adduction is preserved.

Now the clinical presentation. The classic history is nocturnal pain and paresthesia in the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring finger, relieved by shaking the hand, a behavior called the flick sign. Patients often report dropping objects and difficulty with fine tasks such as buttoning. Symptoms are provoked by sustained wrist flexion or extension, such as driving or holding a phone. The nocturnal predominance is explained by the tendency to sleep with the wrist flexed and by the nocturnal redistribution of fluid.

On examination, test sensation in the median distribution with attention to sparing of the thenar eminence. Assess thenar bulk and specifically test abductor pollicis brevis by having the patient abduct the thumb perpendicular to the plane of the palm against resistance. Thenar atrophy is a late finding and indicates significant axonal loss.

Know the provocative tests and their performance characteristics. The Phalen maneuver holds the wrists in flexion for sixty seconds and is positive if symptoms reproduce. The Tinel sign is percussion over the carpal tunnel producing paresthesia in the median distribution. The Durkan carpal compression test applies direct pressure over the tunnel for roughly thirty seconds and is generally the most sensitive and specific of the three. Be honest with the examiner that all three have only moderate accuracy and that the diagnosis is clinical, supported by electrodiagnostics.

Now the differential, which is what the examiner is really probing. Cervical radiculopathy, particularly at the sixth cervical root, produces sensory symptoms in the thumb and index finger but should also produce neck pain, proximal symptoms, a dermatomal rather than peripheral nerve pattern, and reflex changes. Proximal median neuropathy, such as pronator syndrome or anterior interosseous syndrome, involves the thenar eminence sensation and may weaken the long finger flexors. Thoracic outlet syndrome produces lower trunk symptoms in an ulnar distribution and is aggravated by overhead activity. Peripheral polyneuropathy from diabetes or other causes produces a stocking glove pattern. Brachial plexopathy and cord pathology must be considered with atypical findings. And critically, consider the double crush phenomenon, in which a proximal compression such as cervical radiculopathy renders the distal nerve more vulnerable, meaning a patient can genuinely have both, and releasing one may yield only partial improvement. Mentioning double crush is a strong answer.

Look also for secondary causes, because they change management. Pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, amyloidosis, acromegaly, obesity, and dialysis related amyloid are the classic associations. A distal radius fracture or a ganglion within the tunnel can produce acute compression. Acute carpal tunnel syndrome after trauma with severe unremitting pain is a surgical emergency requiring urgent release, and you should distinguish it clearly from the chronic elective condition.

Electrodiagnostic studies are the confirmatory test. The earliest and most sensitive abnormality is prolongation of the distal sensory latency, because sensory fibers are affected first. Motor findings, specifically prolonged distal motor latency, appear later. In severe disease, electromyography of abductor pollicis brevis shows fibrillations and positive sharp waves indicating denervation. Comparative studies across the palm are more sensitive than standard studies. Know that roughly ten to fifteen percent of clinically genuine carpal tunnel syndrome has normal electrodiagnostics, so a negative study does not exclude the diagnosis in a convincing clinical picture. Know also that electrodiagnostics serve two additional functions: excluding alternative or superimposed diagnoses, and establishing a documented baseline, which is medicolegally important. Ultrasound and magnetic resonance can be used adjunctively, with an increased cross sectional area of the median nerve at the tunnel inlet being the ultrasound criterion, and they are particularly useful for identifying a mass lesion.

Now management. Start with nonoperative treatment for mild to moderate disease without denervation. The cornerstone is nocturnal neutral wrist splinting, which keeps the wrist out of flexion during sleep, and it has genuine evidence behind it. Add activity modification and ergonomic adjustment. Corticosteroid injection into the carpal tunnel provides meaningful short term relief in a majority of patients, though the benefit frequently wanes within months; it also has diagnostic value, since a good response predicts a good surgical outcome. Oral steroids provide short term benefit. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, diuretics, and vitamin B six lack convincing evidence and you should say so rather than list them approvingly. Treat the underlying condition where one exists.

Surgical indications are failure of nonoperative management, the presence of thenar atrophy or electrodiagnostic evidence of denervation, and severe or progressive symptoms. Acute compression is an emergency. The key principle to state is that you do not wait in the presence of axonal loss, because atrophy may not reverse.

The operation is division of the transverse carpal ligament. Open release uses a short longitudinal incision in the palm aligned with the radial border of the ring finger, staying ulnar to the thenar crease and generally not crossing the wrist crease, to protect the palmar cutaneous branch. Divide the ligament along its ulnar aspect to keep away from the recurrent motor branch, and confirm complete release both distally into the palmar fat and proximally into the distal forearm fascia, because incomplete release is the most common cause of persistent symptoms. Inspect the nerve. Do not routinely perform internal neurolysis, epineurotomy, tenosynovectomy, or flexor retinaculum reconstruction, as none has demonstrated benefit and each adds risk.

Endoscopic release, either single or two portal, produces faster return to work and less scar tenderness in the early postoperative period, with equivalent outcomes at one year. Its tradeoffs are a higher rate of transient nerve injury in some series, a steeper learning curve, and the inability to address a mass lesion. State that you would convert to open if visualization is inadequate, which is the answer the examiner wants.

Know the complications and be ready to discuss them unprompted. Incomplete release, the most common cause of failure. Injury to the palmar cutaneous branch producing a painful neuroma. Injury to the recurrent motor branch producing thenar weakness. Injury to the median nerve proper or to the superficial palmar arch, which lies just distal to the distal edge of the ligament. Pillar pain, which is tenderness at the thenar and hypothenar eminences, common and usually self limited over several months. Bowstringing of the flexor tendons from over-release combined with loss of the pulley effect. Complex regional pain syndrome. Infection and stiffness.

Now the scenario the examiner is driving toward: the patient who fails to improve. Approach it systematically and out loud. First, was the original diagnosis correct? Revisit the differential, particularly cervical radiculopathy, proximal median neuropathy, and polyneuropathy, and re-examine and reimage as needed. Second, was the release complete? Incomplete division, particularly of the distal forearm fascia, is the leading technical cause, and this can be assessed with repeat electrodiagnostics and imaging. Third, is this recurrence from perineural scarring, typically after an initial period of genuine improvement? Fourth, is there irreversible axonal loss, meaning the nerve was released too late and the symptoms are permanent deficits rather than ongoing compression? Fifth, is there a new or missed lesion, such as a ganglion or a tumor? Revision surgery is most successful when incomplete release is documented; it is least successful when the original diagnosis was wrong or when the deficit is fixed. For recurrent scarring, revision with a vascularized flap such as a hypothenar fat pad flap is an option worth naming.

Know the anatomical variants, because they explain confusing examination findings and they are exactly the detail an examiner uses to separate candidates. A Martin Gruber anastomosis is a crossover of motor fibres from the median or anterior interosseous nerve to the ulnar nerve in the forearm, present in roughly fifteen to twenty percent of people; it can produce unexpectedly preserved thenar function in a median lesion and it produces characteristic electrodiagnostic artefacts. The Riche Cannieu anastomosis is the reciprocal connection in the palm between the recurrent median branch and the deep ulnar branch, and in its complete form produces an all ulnar hand in which the thenar muscles are ulnar innervated. A persistent median artery and a bifid median nerve both occupy space within the tunnel, predispose to compression, and must be recognised before an endoscopic release. Remember also that the recurrent motor branch is transligamentous in a minority, which is precisely the variant that gets divided during a blind release.

Grade severity, because it drives both treatment and prognosis. Clinically, mild disease is intermittent sensory symptoms alone, moderate is persistent sensory symptoms with mild weakness, and severe is fixed sensory loss with thenar atrophy. Electrodiagnostically, the earliest change is prolonged distal sensory latency with preserved amplitude; then sensory amplitude falls; then distal motor latency prolongs; and finally needle examination of abductor pollicis brevis shows fibrillations and reduced recruitment, which signals axonal loss. The management rule follows directly: sensory only disease is a candidate for a genuine non-operative trial, whereas any motor axonal loss argues for prompt release because atrophy may not reverse.

Add the situations that change the algorithm. In pregnancy, carpal tunnel syndrome is common, usually bilateral, and most cases resolve spontaneously after delivery, so treat with night splinting and reassurance and reserve surgery for severe or progressive deficit. In a dialysis patient, consider beta two microglobulin amyloid deposition, which thickens the flexor tenosynovium and may warrant tenosynovectomy at the time of release. In an older patient with bilateral disease, spontaneous biceps tendon rupture, lumbar canal stenosis or cardiac failure, consider transthyretin amyloidosis, for which carpal tunnel syndrome is often the earliest manifestation by several years; sending tenosynovium for Congo red staining at the time of release is a genuinely modern and high scoring answer, because disease modifying therapy now exists. In diabetes, expect a superimposed polyneuropathy, a less complete response to release, and the need to counsel accordingly.

Be precise about the operative conduct. The incision runs in line with the radial border of the ring finger, ulnar to the thenar crease, from the distal wrist crease distally toward Kaplan cardinal line, which is drawn from the first web space toward the hook of the hamate and marks the approximate level of the superficial palmar arch. Staying ulnar protects the palmar cutaneous branch and the recurrent motor branch; staying proximal to Kaplan line protects the arch. Divide the transverse carpal ligament under direct vision along its ulnar margin, then confirm the release proximally by passing an instrument into the distal forearm fascia, because incomplete proximal release is a leading cause of persistent symptoms, and distally into the palmar fat. Inspect the nerve, note any bifid anatomy or persistent median artery, and close skin only. Most of this can be done under wide awake local anaesthesia with no tourniquet, which allows intraoperative confirmation of thumb abduction and avoids general anaesthesia entirely in a frail patient.

Quote outcomes honestly. Open and endoscopic release both relieve symptoms in roughly ninety percent of appropriately selected patients. Endoscopic release confers faster return to work and less scar tenderness in the first several weeks, with equivalent results by one year and a slightly higher rate of transient nerve injury in some series. Ultrasound guided percutaneous release is emerging with promising early data and is worth naming as a developing option rather than asserting as standard. Nocturnal symptoms typically resolve within days; fixed numbness and thenar atrophy improve slowly over many months, if at all. Grip strength commonly dips for six to twelve weeks because of pillar pain and loss of the pulley effect before recovering.

Finally, think about the whole patient, not just the wrist. Bilateral disease is common, so examine and test both sides. Consider the double crush phenomenon and examine the neck. Ask about occupational exposure and consider ergonomic modification and, where relevant, occupational health reporting. And counsel that carpal tunnel release is one of the most reliably successful operations in surgery when the diagnosis is right, which is precisely why the diagnosis deserves the care described above.

To summarize. The median nerve is superficial and radial within the tunnel. Thenar sensation is spared because the palmar cutaneous branch passes over the ligament. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by distal sensory latency prolongation, and a normal study does not exclude it. Splint and inject for mild disease, operate for failure or for any denervation, and operate urgently for acute compression. Release the ligament completely along its ulnar edge, both proximally and distally. And when the patient fails, work the list: wrong diagnosis, incomplete release, recurrent scar, irreversible axonal loss, or a missed second lesion.
