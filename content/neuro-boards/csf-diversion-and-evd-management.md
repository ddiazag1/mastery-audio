Let's talk about CSF diversion and EVD management. This is a bread-and-butter neurosurgical topic, one you will absolutely face on your oral boards, and one where the examiners expect crisp, confident answers. We are going to walk through CSF physiology, hydrocephalus classification, EVD placement technique, drainage management, weaning protocols, VP shunt fundamentals, alternative diversion procedures, and normal pressure hydrocephalus. Then we will work through a clinical case with examiner-style questions, including the traps they like to set.

Starting with CSF physiology. Cerebrospinal fluid is produced primarily by the choroid plexus at a rate of approximately 20 milliliters per hour, which translates to roughly 500 milliliters per day. The total CSF volume in the adult is about 150 milliliters at any given moment, meaning the entire volume turns over three to four times per day. CSF is produced in the lateral ventricles, flows through the foramina of Monro into the third ventricle, traverses the cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius into the fourth ventricle, and exits through the foramina of Luschka laterally and the foramen of Magendie midline into the subarachnoid space. Absorption occurs via the arachnoid granulations, also called Pacchionian granulations, which protrude into the dural venous sinuses, predominantly the superior sagittal sinus. CSF is absorbed passively along a pressure gradient from the subarachnoid space into the venous system. This production-absorption balance is the foundation of everything we are about to discuss.

Now, hydrocephalus classification. The first major division is communicating versus non-communicating, also called obstructive. In communicating hydrocephalus, there is no mechanical blockage within the ventricular system itself. The obstruction is at the level of the arachnoid granulations or within the subarachnoid space, preventing normal absorption. In non-communicating or obstructive hydrocephalus, there is a discrete blockage within the ventricular system, most commonly at the cerebral aqueduct, the foramen of Monro, or the fourth ventricular outlets. The second classification axis is acute versus chronic. Acute hydrocephalus presents with rapid clinical deterioration, often over hours, with headache, altered consciousness, and signs of elevated intracranial pressure. Chronic hydrocephalus develops over weeks to months, with gradual symptom onset and often markedly dilated ventricles with relatively preserved neurological function.

The causes of hydrocephalus organize neatly by category. Hemorrhagic causes include subarachnoid hemorrhage and intraventricular hemorrhage, where blood products obstruct arachnoid granulations or ventricular pathways. Infectious causes include meningitis and ventriculitis, where inflammatory debris and adhesions impair CSF flow and absorption. Neoplastic causes include any tumor that obstructs the ventricular system, classically posterior fossa tumors compressing the fourth ventricle or aqueduct, colloid cysts at the foramen of Monro, and pineal region tumors compressing the aqueduct. Congenital causes include aqueductal stenosis, Dandy-Walker malformation, Chiari malformations, and myelomeningocele. And then idiopathic causes, most notably normal pressure hydrocephalus, which we will address in detail later.

Let's move to EVD placement technique, which you need to describe with precision on your oral boards. The standard entry point is Kocher's point. The landmarks are 1 centimeter anterior to the coronal suture and 2 to 3 centimeters lateral to the midline, which typically falls at the mid-pupillary line. You want to be on the non-dominant side when possible, so typically the right side. If there is a question about which side, you choose the side with more blood if placing for intraventricular hemorrhage, or the side with larger ventricles.

For trajectory, you aim the catheter perpendicular to the skull surface in two planes. In the coronal plane, the target is the medial canthus of the ipsilateral eye. In the sagittal plane, the target is the external auditory meatus, which is essentially the tragus. This trajectory directs the catheter toward the ipsilateral frontal horn of the lateral ventricle, specifically toward the foramen of Monro. The typical depth to reach the frontal horn is 5 to 7 centimeters from the outer table of the skull. When you enter the ventricle, you will feel a give or pop, and CSF should flow from the catheter. If you reach 7 centimeters without CSF return, withdraw the stylet and check, but do not advance beyond 8 centimeters without reassessing your trajectory. Limit yourself to three passes maximum to minimize hemorrhagic complications.

After confirming CSF flow, you tunnel the catheter subcutaneously at least 5 centimeters from the entry site to reduce infection risk. Connect the catheter to a closed external drainage system. Secure with sutures and apply a sterile dressing.

Now, the alternative posterior approach. The Frazier burr hole is your backup when the frontal approach fails or is not feasible, for example in the setting of bifrontal pathology. The Frazier point is located 3 to 4 centimeters lateral to the midline and 6 to 7 centimeters above the inion. You aim for the occipital horn of the lateral ventricle, directing the catheter toward an imaginary point just above the contralateral brow. This approach is less commonly used and more technically challenging, so know it but recognize that Kocher's point is your primary approach.

Complications of EVD placement. Hemorrhage occurs in 1 to 2 percent of placements, but this rate is significantly higher in patients with coagulopathy, so always correct the INR to below 1.4 and platelet count to above 100,000 before placing an EVD if time allows. Malposition occurs in 5 to 10 percent of cases. Always confirm placement with a postprocedure CT scan. An EVD in the contralateral ventricle, the third ventricle, or within brain parenchyma is malpositioned and may need revision if it is not functioning. Infection risk is approximately 5 to 10 percent and increases with prolonged drainage beyond 10 to 14 days. Antibiotic-impregnated catheters reduce this risk. Overdrainage is a procedural complication we will discuss in detail next.

Drainage management is where the day-to-day nuance lives. The transducer must be leveled at the tragus, which approximates the foramen of Monro. Every time the patient's head position changes, the system must be releveled. The drainage height is set in centimeters of water above the transducer. When you set the drain at 20 centimeters of water, CSF will only drain when the intracranial pressure exceeds 20 centimeters of water. A lower setting means more permissive drainage, and a higher setting means more restrictive drainage. Typical starting settings range from 15 to 20 centimeters of water, but this is disease-dependent. For subarachnoid hemorrhage, you may start lower, around 10 to 15 centimeters, to keep the ventricles decompressed and facilitate clot clearance. For other conditions, 20 centimeters is a reasonable starting point.

There are two drainage philosophies: continuous and intermittent. In continuous drainage, the drain is left open and CSF drains freely whenever ICP exceeds the set height. This provides steady ICP control but does not allow you to trend the true ICP waveform. In intermittent drainage, you clamp the drain and open it periodically, usually for a few minutes every hour, or when the ICP exceeds a threshold like 20 millimeters of mercury. This lets you monitor true ICP but risks ICP spikes during clamped periods. Institutional practice varies, and both approaches are acceptable, but you should be able to articulate the rationale for whichever you use.

Troubleshooting a non-functioning EVD follows a systematic approach. If there is no CSF output, consider the following in order. First, check for kinked tubing or closed stopcocks by inspecting the entire length of the system. Second, consider a clotted catheter, and attempt gentle flushing with 1 to 2 milliliters of preservative-free saline, though some attendings avoid flushing due to infection risk. Third, consider collapsed ventricles from overdrainage, which you confirm with a CT scan. Fourth, consider catheter malposition, again confirmed with imaging. If there is excessive CSF output, verify the drain height setting. An accidentally low drain will produce high output. Also consider whether the patient has a CSF leak elsewhere in the system. Overdrainage complications include slit ventricle syndrome, where the ventricles collapse around the catheter and it becomes obstructed by choroid plexus or ependyma. In patients with posterior fossa lesions, overdrainage of the supratentorial compartment can cause upward transtentorial herniation, a life-threatening emergency. Chronic overdrainage can also produce subdural hygromas or subdural hematomas from tearing of bridging veins as the brain falls away from the dura.

Weaning an EVD is a stepwise process. The classic protocol raises the drain height incrementally, for example from 10 to 15 to 20 to 25 centimeters of water, spending 12 to 24 hours at each level while monitoring the ICP and clinical exam. An alternative approach is clamping the drain entirely and monitoring ICP for 24 to 48 hours while it is clamped. Failure criteria include ICP consistently above 20 to 22 millimeters of mercury, clinical deterioration such as new lethargy or worsening neurological exam, or new or worsening hydrocephalus on CT scan. If the patient fails a wean trial, you return to the previous drainage setting and reattempt in several days.

Shunt dependency rates are disease-specific. For subarachnoid hemorrhage patients, approximately 20 to 30 percent will require a permanent shunt. For intraventricular hemorrhage patients, the rate is roughly 10 to 15 percent. For posterior fossa tumor patients, most do not need permanent shunting after tumor resection, as 60 to 70 percent of cases of hydrocephalus resolve once the obstruction is removed. This is an important point for the boards: do not rush to place a permanent shunt in a posterior fossa tumor patient.

Moving to VP shunts. The three components are the proximal catheter, which sits in the lateral ventricle, the valve and reservoir assembly, and the distal catheter, which terminates in the peritoneal cavity. Valve types fall into two categories: fixed pressure and programmable. Fixed-pressure valves come in low, medium, and high settings, and once implanted, their pressure setting cannot be changed without surgical revision. Programmable valves, such as the Codman Certas, Medtronic Strata, and Integra valves, can be adjusted non-invasively using an external magnetic device. This is a major advantage because you can fine-tune the drainage without reoperation. However, be aware that MRI can alter the setting of some programmable valves, so the setting must be verified and reset after every MRI.

Shunt complications are common. Overall malfunction rates reach 30 to 40 percent at one year in the pediatric population, lower in adults. Obstruction is the most common cause of malfunction, and proximal obstruction, meaning the ventricular catheter becoming occluded by choroid plexus, is more common than distal obstruction. Infection rates are 5 to 10 percent, highest in the first six months after placement, with Staphylococcus epidermidis as the most common organism. Overdrainage leads to slit ventricle syndrome in chronic cases and subdural collections in acute or subacute cases. When evaluating a potentially malfunctioning shunt, you order a shunt series, which consists of skull, chest, and abdominal X-rays that trace the entire course of the tubing and check for disconnection, kinking, or migration. You also get a CT head to evaluate ventricular size compared to prior imaging.

Now let's discuss alternative CSF diversion procedures. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy, or ETV, involves creating a fenestration in the floor of the third ventricle, allowing CSF to bypass a downstream obstruction and flow directly into the prepontine cistern. ETV is most effective for non-communicating hydrocephalus, particularly aqueductal stenosis. Success rates are 70 to 80 percent in appropriately selected patients. The ETV Success Score is a validated tool that predicts outcome based on patient age, etiology of hydrocephalus, and prior shunting history. ETV is not appropriate for communicating hydrocephalus because the problem is not at the level of the ventricular outflow.

The lumboperitoneal shunt diverts CSF from the lumbar thecal sac to the peritoneal cavity. It is used for communicating hydrocephalus, specifically conditions like normal pressure hydrocephalus and pseudotumor cerebri, also known as idiopathic intracranial hypertension. A critical point for the boards: lumboperitoneal shunts are contraindicated in obstructive hydrocephalus because draining CSF from below can create a pressure gradient that causes tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum.

Let's cover normal pressure hydrocephalus in detail. NPH presents with the classic Hakim triad: gait apraxia, urinary incontinence, and dementia. Know the order. Gait is first to appear and first to respond to shunting. Urinary incontinence appears next. Dementia appears last and is least responsive to treatment. The gait in NPH is classically described as a magnetic gait, where the feet seem glued to the floor, with short shuffling steps and a wide base. Imaging shows ventriculomegaly out of proportion to sulcal enlargement, with an Evans index greater than 0.3. The Evans index is the ratio of the maximum width of the frontal horns to the maximum internal diameter of the skull, measured on axial CT or MRI.

The CSF tap test involves draining 30 to 50 milliliters of CSF via lumbar puncture and assessing gait before and after. Improvement in gait predicts a positive response to shunting. However, the tap test has limited sensitivity, around 50 to 60 percent, meaning a negative tap test does not rule out shunt-responsive NPH. Extended lumbar drainage over 3 to 5 days at a rate of 10 milliliters per hour is more sensitive and is used when the tap test is equivocal and clinical suspicion remains high. For NPH patients who are shunt candidates, a VP shunt with a programmable valve is the standard approach. You typically start at a medium pressure setting and adjust downward if the patient does not improve or upward if overdrainage symptoms develop.

Now let's walk through a clinical case in the style of your oral board exam.

You are called to the emergency department for a 58-year-old woman found unresponsive. Her GCS is 7. CT head shows diffuse subarachnoid hemorrhage with a Fisher Grade 4 pattern and acute hydrocephalus with bilateral temporal horn dilation. CTA reveals a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm.

The examiner asks, what is your immediate management? You answer: This is a Hunt and Hess Grade 4 subarachnoid hemorrhage with acute hydrocephalus. My first priority is airway management. She needs intubation for airway protection given her GCS of 7. I will then place an external ventricular drain at Kocher's point on the right side to treat the acute hydrocephalus and monitor ICP. I will set the drain at 15 centimeters of water initially and send CSF for cell count, glucose, protein, gram stain, and culture as a baseline.

The examiner says, you place the EVD. The opening pressure is 32 centimeters of water. You set the drain at 15 centimeters. Over the next hour, the drain puts out 25 milliliters per hour of clear CSF. Is this too much?

This is the first trap question. In the immediate post-placement period, 25 milliliters per hour is not alarming. The patient had an opening pressure of 32 with acute hydrocephalus. CSF is draining because the ICP exceeds the drain setting, which is exactly what should happen. As the ICP normalizes, the output will decrease. I would not reflexively raise the drain height right now, but I would monitor the output trend over the next few hours. If it remains persistently elevated at this rate beyond 4 to 6 hours, I would reassess and potentially raise the drain height. The key point is that early high output reflects decompression, not a problem.

The examiner continues. It is now post-bleed day 7. The patient has improved to a GCS of 11 after aneurysm coiling. The EVD output has been 8 to 10 milliliters per hour. The nurse calls because the drain has not put out any CSF in the last 3 hours. What do you do?

I assess the patient clinically first. If there is acute neurological decline, that is an emergency. Assuming the patient is at baseline, I troubleshoot the drain systematically. I check all stopcocks and tubing for kinks. I lower the drain height briefly to see if flow resumes. If not, I flush the catheter gently with 1 to 2 milliliters of preservative-free saline. If the catheter remains occluded, I order a CT head to evaluate ventricular size and catheter position. The differential is kinked tubing, clotted catheter, collapsed ventricles, or catheter malposition.

The examiner asks, the CT shows stable ventricular size and the catheter is in good position. What now? I would continue to monitor. If the ventricles are stable and not enlarged, the catheter may simply be temporarily occluded by choroid plexus or debris. I may attempt a repeat gentle flush. If the drain remains nonfunctional and the patient needs ongoing CSF diversion, I replace the catheter.

Now the examiner shifts. It is post-bleed day 14. The CSF cell count shows 450 white blood cells with 85 percent neutrophils, glucose of 25 milligrams per deciliter, and protein of 220 milligrams per deciliter. CSF gram stain shows gram-positive cocci. What is your diagnosis and management?

This is ventriculitis. The CSF profile is classic: neutrophilic pleocytosis, low glucose, elevated protein, and positive gram stain. I send CSF cultures immediately. I start empiric intravenous vancomycin plus intrathecal vancomycin at 10 to 20 milligrams daily, administered through the EVD. Gram-positive cocci in the setting of an EVD suggest Staphylococcus, either epidermidis or aureus. I remove the existing EVD and place a new one on the contralateral side. I do not simply change the catheter over a guidewire because this does not adequately address the infected tract. I follow daily CSF cultures and cell counts. Once cultures are negative for 48 to 72 hours, I reassess the need for ongoing intrathecal antibiotics.

The examiner throws another trap. The patient eventually recovers. You attempt to wean the EVD. You raise the drain to 20 centimeters of water, and after 12 hours the ICP climbs to 24. The patient becomes more somnolent. Do you proceed with VP shunt placement?

Not yet. A single failed wean attempt does not automatically commit the patient to a permanent shunt. I lower the drain back to 15 centimeters, let the patient stabilize, and reattempt the wean in 3 to 5 days. Many SAH patients will eventually pass a wean trial. If she fails two or three wean attempts over a period of 10 to 14 days, and the CSF is clear of infection, then I proceed with VP shunt placement with a programmable valve.

One more trap question the examiner might ask: A 6-year-old presents with acute hydrocephalus from a posterior fossa medulloblastoma. Do you place an EVD first, or proceed directly to tumor resection?

This depends on the clinical scenario. If the child is acutely deteriorating from hydrocephalus with signs of herniation, I place an EVD emergently to stabilize. However, if the child is neurologically stable with controlled hydrocephalus, many surgeons proceed directly to tumor resection because removing the obstruction often resolves the hydrocephalus. Placing an EVD preoperatively carries a risk of upward transtentorial herniation during or after posterior fossa surgery if the supratentorial ventricles are overdrained. If I do place a preoperative EVD, I set it at a high level, around 20 to 25 centimeters of water, and clamp it during the posterior fossa portion of the operation to prevent upward herniation. The board answer is that you recognize the risk and tailor your approach to the clinical status of the patient.

One final examiner question: A 12-year-old with a VP shunt presents with headache and vomiting. CT shows slit ventricles. Is the shunt working?

This is one of the classic traps. Slit ventricles do not mean the shunt is working. The shunted patient with slit ventricles and symptoms of elevated ICP may have slit ventricle syndrome, where chronic overdrainage has caused the ventricles to collapse and the brain to become less compliant. The proximal catheter may be intermittently obstructed by ventricular wall or choroid plexus, causing episodic elevations in ICP that do not produce ventriculomegaly because the ventricles have lost their ability to expand. The ventricles are small, but the pressure is high. This is a clinical diagnosis. You tap the shunt reservoir and measure the opening pressure. If the pressure is elevated with slit ventricles, the shunt is malfunctioning despite the small ventricle size. Treatment may involve shunt revision with an antisiphon device, upgrading to a programmable valve set at a higher pressure, or in some cases subtemporal decompression.

Let me close with the board pearls. One, Kocher's point is 1 centimeter anterior to the coronal suture, 2 to 3 centimeters lateral to midline, aim for the medial canthus and tragus. Two, always level the EVD transducer at the tragus. Three, overdrainage in posterior fossa pathology can cause upward herniation, a lethal complication. Four, ventriculitis diagnosis requires trending CSF cell counts, not a single value, because SAH patients will have elevated white cells in the CSF at baseline from the hemorrhage itself. The shift to neutrophilic predominance, falling glucose, and rising protein are the key indicators. Five, NPH triad is gait first, then incontinence, then dementia, and gait is the most responsive to shunting. Six, slit ventricles on CT do not equal a functioning shunt. Seven, ETV is for obstructive hydrocephalus and lumboperitoneal shunts are for communicating hydrocephalus. Never put an LP shunt in a patient with obstructive hydrocephalus. Eight, the ETV Success Score guides your patient selection for endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Nine, programmable valve settings must be checked after MRI. Ten, shunt series means skull, chest, and abdominal X-rays to trace the entire catheter course.

That covers CSF diversion and EVD management. You're ready for this one.
