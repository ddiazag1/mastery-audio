questions:
  - q: List the signs of a preganglionic injury.
    a: Horner syndrome from first thoracic root avulsion; winged scapula and rhomboid weakness because long thoracic and dorsal scapular nerves arise at root level; hemidiaphragm elevation from phrenic involvement; severe deafferentation pain; paraspinal denervation on electromyography; a preserved sensory nerve action potential in a clinically anaesthetic limb; and pseudomeningoceles on myelography.
    probe: Explain the preserved sensory potential.
    probeA: The dorsal root ganglion sits in the intervertebral foramen, distal to the point where a preganglionic avulsion tears the rootlets from the cord. So in an avulsion the sensory cell body remains in continuity with its peripheral axon, which therefore does not undergo Wallerian degeneration and continues to conduct, giving a normal sensory nerve action potential. But the central connection to the cord is gone, so the patient is clinically anaesthetic. A normal sensory potential in an anaesthetic limb is therefore pathognomonic of a lesion proximal to the ganglion, and it is one of the most useful pieces of information in the whole workup.
  - q: Why does preganglionic versus postganglionic decide everything?
    a: A preganglionic avulsion has no proximal stump to sew to, so it cannot be grafted and requires nerve transfer from a donor outside the injured territory. A postganglionic rupture has a stump and can be grafted. That single distinction determines the entire reconstructive plan.
    probe: Which roots tend to avulse and which tend to rupture, and why?
    probeA: The lower roots, C8 and T1, tend to avulse, and the upper roots, C5 and C6, tend to rupture. The anatomical reason is that C5 and C6 have strong fibrous attachments to the transverse processes and relatively robust epineurium, so under traction they fail distally, outside the foramen, leaving a stump. C8 and T1 lack those attachments and have thinner supporting tissue, so they tend to fail proximally, at the rootlets. That is why upper plexus injuries are more often graftable and lower plexus injuries more often require transfer or are unreconstructable.
  - q: Localise using branches, proximal to distal.
    a: Rhomboids implicate the dorsal scapular nerve and the fifth root. Serratus anterior implicates long thoracic from the fifth, sixth and seventh roots. Diaphragm implicates phrenic and the fifth root. Supraspinatus and infraspinatus implicate the suprascapular nerve at Erb point on the upper trunk. Latissimus implicates thoracodorsal from the posterior cord.
    probe: Deltoid weak but triceps normal. Where is the lesion?
    probeA: In the posterior cord region but distal to the origin of the thoracodorsal and upper subscapular nerves, or more likely an isolated axillary nerve lesion. Deltoid is supplied by the axillary nerve and triceps by the radial nerve, and both arise from the posterior cord, so a posterior cord lesion would usually affect both. Sparing of triceps with deltoid weakness therefore points distal to the cord, at the axillary nerve, typically at the quadrilateral space or after anterior shoulder dislocation or proximal humeral fracture. I would confirm with sensation over the regimental badge area and with electrodiagnostics of deltoid and teres minor.
  - q: Erb versus Klumpke: mechanism, pattern, prognosis.
    a: Erb involves the fifth and sixth roots from forced separation of head and shoulder, losing shoulder abduction, external rotation and elbow flexion, giving the waiter's tip posture with preserved hand function. Klumpke involves the eighth cervical and first thoracic roots from traction on an abducted arm, giving intrinsic weakness and a claw, often with Horner syndrome and a worse prognosis.
    probe: Why is the lower plexus prognosis worse anatomically?
    probeA: Two reasons. First, the regeneration distance: the C8 and T1 fibres destined for the hand intrinsics have to travel from the plexus to the hand, which at a millimetre a day exceeds the twelve to eighteen month endplate window, so even a successful proximal repair arrives too late. Second, the lower roots preferentially avulse rather than rupture, so there is frequently no proximal stump to graft and the reconstructive options are limited to distal transfers. Upper plexus targets such as biceps and deltoid are much closer and much more often graftable, which is why elbow flexion and shoulder stability are realistic goals and intrinsic hand function is usually not.
  - q: Give me the timeline for investigation and operation.
    a: Electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks, including paraspinals and sensory potentials. Imaging by computed tomographic myelography or magnetic resonance myelography, most informative at three to four weeks once haemorrhage settles. Reassess at three months. Operate by three to six months if there is no recovery.
    probe: Why can you not simply wait and see for a year?
    probeA: Because the endplate clock is running from the moment of injury, not from the moment I decide to operate. Denervated muscle becomes unresponsive to reinnervation at around twelve to eighteen months, and I have to add the regeneration time from the repair site to the target, which for elbow flexion is several months. So operating at twelve months means the axons arrive at eighteen or later, by which point the biceps will not accept them. Waiting a year to see what happens guarantees the reconstruction fails. That is why the deadline is three to six months, and why a documented avulsion goes earlier still.
  - q: State the reconstruction priorities in order.
    a: Elbow flexion first, because it converts a flail arm into a useful one. Then shoulder stability, abduction and external rotation. Then protective hand sensation, especially median. Then wrist and finger extension, then finger flexion. Intrinsics last and generally unachievable in a severe injury.
    probe: Which transfer would you use for the first priority?
    probeA: The Oberlin transfer: a redundant fascicle of the ulnar nerve, usually one driving flexor carpi ulnaris, coapted to the motor branch of biceps. Commonly doubled, adding a median nerve fascicle to the brachialis branch, which gives more reliable and stronger flexion. It works because the donor is expendable, the coaptation is close to the target so the regeneration distance is short, and it does not depend on a proximal stump, so it is available in avulsion. The alternative in a patient whose ulnar nerve is not usable is an intercostal to musculocutaneous transfer, or free functioning gracilis transfer in a late presentation.
  - q: Which injuries do not wait for serial examination?
    a: Sharp penetrating injury with suspected transection, explored and repaired within days. Vascular injury, explored emergently. Open contaminated or blast injury, debrided and tagged with repair at about three weeks. And a documented avulsion, which will not recover and should go early rather than after months of observation.
    probe: How often does vascular injury accompany plexus trauma?
    probeA: Commonly enough that it must be actively excluded rather than assumed absent, and the figures quoted in the trauma literature are in the range of ten to twenty percent for significant injuries in high-energy closed plexus trauma, higher still in penetrating injury. The important clinical point is that a palpable distal pulse does not exclude subclavian or axillary injury, because collateral flow around the shoulder can preserve it. So in a high-energy injury with a first rib or clavicle fracture and a flail arm, I would obtain angiography rather than rely on the pulse.
  - q: He has intractable burning pain in an anaesthetic arm. Options?
    a: Deafferentation pain after avulsion. Start with gabapentinoids, tricyclics and serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, with psychological and rehabilitation support. Dorsal root entry zone lesioning is the established surgical treatment with good long term response. Spinal cord stimulation is generally ineffective in complete avulsion because the dorsal columns are deafferented.
    probe: Why does that make spinal cord stimulation fail?
    probeA: Because spinal cord stimulation works by activating large myelinated fibres in the dorsal columns, and those fibres are the central projections of the dorsal root ganglion cells. In a preganglionic avulsion the rootlets have been torn from the cord, so those afferents no longer reach the dorsal columns at all. There is therefore nothing at the target for the stimulator to activate in the deafferented segments, and paraesthesia cannot be steered into the painful territory. That is exactly why dorsal root entry zone lesioning is the operation that works here: the pain is generated by hyperactive deafferented second-order neurons in the dorsal horn, and the lesion destroys the generator rather than trying to modulate an input that no longer exists.
cases:
  - label: Motorcyclist, flail arm
    stem: A 22-year-old comes off a motorcycle at speed. The right arm is flail and anaesthetic. There is ptosis and miosis on the right. Chest radiograph shows an elevated right hemidiaphragm and a fractured first rib. Radial pulse is present.
    q1: What does this constellation tell you before any imaging?
    a1: Horner syndrome indicates first thoracic root avulsion; the elevated hemidiaphragm indicates phrenic involvement and therefore fifth root injury; a first rib fracture indicates very high energy. Together these suggest a pan-plexus injury with preganglionic components, which carries the worst prognosis.
    p1: He has a Horner syndrome. Why does that specifically mean T1?
    pa1: Because the sympathetic supply to the face leaves the spinal cord as preganglionic fibres in the ventral roots of the first and second thoracic segments, ascends in the sympathetic chain to synapse in the superior cervical ganglion, and the postganglionic fibres then travel with the internal carotid artery to reach the eye and the face. An avulsion of the T1 root therefore interrupts that pathway at its origin, producing ptosis from loss of Müller's muscle, miosis from loss of the pupillary dilator, and anhidrosis. It matters clinically because it is a preganglionic sign: it localises the injury proximal to the dorsal root ganglion, tells me the T1 root is avulsed rather than ruptured, and therefore that there is no stump to graft and intrinsic hand function is not recoverable.
    q2: What do you do in the first twenty four hours?
    a2: Complete the trauma survey; these patients are usually polytrauma. Specifically assess the vascular status, because subclavian or axillary injury accompanies plexus trauma and is an emergency even with a present distal pulse. Look for associated cervical spine, scapular, clavicular and humeral injury and for coexisting cord injury. Then document a meticulous MRC-graded examination as the baseline.
    p2: How would you assess the vascular status if the pulse is present?
    pa2: I would not rely on the pulse, because collateral flow around the shoulder through the suprascapular and circumflex scapular vessels can preserve a distal pulse despite a subclavian or axillary injury. So I would compare blood pressures in both arms looking for a significant differential, examine for an expanding haematoma, a bruit or a thrill, assess capillary refill and limb temperature, and then obtain CT angiography of the subclavian and axillary vessels rather than accepting a clinical assessment. The threshold should be low: a first rib fracture with a flail arm from a high-energy mechanism is exactly the pattern associated with vascular injury, and a missed subclavian injury with a delayed pseudoaneurysm or thrombosis is both limb-threatening and a preventable disaster.
    q3: At four weeks, electromyography shows paraspinal denervation with preserved sensory nerve action potentials in an anaesthetic limb, and myelography shows pseudomeningoceles at C8 and T1 with intact rootlets at C5 and C6. Interpret and plan.
    a3: C8 and T1 are preganglionic avulsions, confirmed by paraspinal denervation, preserved sensory potentials despite anaesthesia, and pseudomeningoceles. C5 and C6 are likely postganglionic and may be graftable. So this is a mixed injury: graft where there is a proximal stump, transfer where there is not.
    p3: Which transfers would you plan for C8 and T1, given they are avulsed?
    pa3: Honestly, very few that restore hand function, and I would say that rather than invent a plan. Intrinsic hand function after C8 and T1 avulsion is not realistically recoverable, because there is no proximal stump and the regeneration distance to the hand exceeds the endplate window even if there were. So the reconstruction concentrates on C5 and C6 territory: grafting from the C5 and C6 stumps if they are viable, spinal accessory to suprascapular for shoulder abduction and external rotation, and a triceps branch to axillary transfer, with elbow flexion addressed by grafting or by intercostal to musculocutaneous transfer, noting that the usual Oberlin donor is unavailable because the ulnar nerve is itself avulsed. For the hand, the realistic options later are wrist and finger tenodesis, arthrodesis, or free functioning muscle transfer, and protective sensation via a median sensory transfer.
    q4: He asks whether he will use the hand again. Answer him.
    a4: I would be direct. With C8 and T1 avulsed, intrinsic hand function is not realistically recoverable, and I would not promise it. The achievable goals in priority order are elbow flexion, then shoulder stability, then protective sensation. I would explain the twelve to eighteen month endplate deadline, why we operate at around three months rather than waiting, and refer him to a specialist plexus centre inside that window.
    p4: What about the pain? He has not asked yet but he will.
    pa4: I would raise it myself rather than wait, because deafferentation pain after avulsion is severe, develops in a large majority, and patients are unprepared for it. I would explain that it is a burning, crushing pain felt in a limb that has no feeling, that it arises from hyperactive deafferented neurons in the dorsal horn rather than from damage in the arm, and that it often begins within days to weeks. Management starts medically with gabapentinoids, tricyclics and serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, alongside psychological support and rehabilitation, and expectation-setting matters because these drugs modify rather than abolish it. Where it remains intractable, dorsal root entry zone lesioning is the established operation with good long-term response in avulsion pain specifically. I would tell him that spinal cord stimulation does not work in complete avulsion, so he is not offered something that will fail.

Let's master brachial plexus injury evaluation and management for the oral boards.

This is the highest difficulty topic in peripheral nerve and the one candidates most often decline to engage with. That is exactly why it is worth owning. The examiner is not expecting you to be a plexus surgeon. They are expecting you to localize the lesion, identify the injuries that require urgent action, understand the timeline that governs everything, and know when to refer. Let's build the anatomy carefully, because without it nothing else is possible.

The brachial plexus arises from the ventral rami of the fifth cervical through first thoracic nerve roots. The standard organization is roots, trunks, divisions, cords, and branches, which most people remember with a mnemonic such as Randy Travis drinks cold beer.

The fifth and sixth cervical roots join to form the upper trunk. The seventh cervical root continues alone as the middle trunk. The eighth cervical and first thoracic roots join to form the lower trunk. Each trunk splits into an anterior and a posterior division. All three posterior divisions unite to form the posterior cord. The anterior divisions of the upper and middle trunks form the lateral cord. The anterior division of the lower trunk alone continues as the medial cord. The cords are named for their relationship to the axillary artery.

The terminal branches follow. The posterior cord gives the axillary and radial nerves, along with the upper and lower subscapular and thoracodorsal nerves. The lateral cord gives the musculocutaneous nerve and the lateral root of the median nerve. The medial cord gives the ulnar nerve, the medial root of the median nerve, and the medial brachial and antebrachial cutaneous nerves.

Now the branches that matter for localization, because these are the ones that tell you how proximal the injury is. The dorsal scapular nerve to the rhomboids arises directly from the fifth cervical root. The long thoracic nerve to serratus anterior arises from the fifth, sixth, and seventh cervical roots, again directly from the roots. The phrenic nerve receives a contribution from the fifth cervical root. The suprascapular nerve arises from the upper trunk at Erb point. Memorize these, because their involvement localizes the lesion proximally, and their preservation localizes it distally.

That brings us to the single most important distinction in the entire topic: preganglionic versus postganglionic injury.

The dorsal root ganglion sits in the neural foramen. A preganglionic injury is an avulsion of the root from the spinal cord, proximal to the ganglion. A postganglionic injury is a rupture or stretch distal to the ganglion. The distinction is decisive because a preganglionic avulsion cannot be repaired directly; there is no proximal stump to sew to. It requires nerve transfer from a donor outside the injured territory. A postganglionic rupture has a proximal stump and can be grafted.

Learn the signs of preganglionic injury and be able to list them. Horner syndrome, meaning ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis, indicates avulsion of the first thoracic root with injury to the sympathetic fibers heading to the superior cervical ganglion, and it is a strong indicator of a severe proximal injury with poor prognosis. Winged scapula from long thoracic nerve palsy and rhomboid weakness indicate root level injury because those nerves come directly off the roots. Hemidiaphragm elevation from phrenic nerve involvement indicates fifth cervical root injury, and you can see it on a chest radiograph or confirm it with fluoroscopic sniff testing. Severe intractable deafferentation pain, often described as crushing or burning in an anesthetic limb, is characteristic of avulsion. Paraspinal muscle denervation on electromyography indicates injury proximal to the dorsal ramus, which branches immediately at the root, and is therefore a reliable electrodiagnostic marker of preganglionic injury. And the crucial electrophysiologic paradox: in a preganglionic avulsion, the sensory nerve action potential is preserved despite clinical anesthesia, because the sensory cell body in the dorsal root ganglion remains connected to its peripheral axon even though its central connection is severed. Clinically numb with a normal sensory nerve action potential equals preganglionic. That sentence alone will impress an examiner.

Imaging supports this. Computed tomographic myelography, historically the gold standard, or high resolution magnetic resonance myelography, demonstrates pseudomeningoceles at the avulsed levels and absence of the rootlets. Pseudomeningocele strongly suggests avulsion, although it is not perfectly sensitive or specific: avulsion can occur without a pseudomeningocele, and a pseudomeningocele can be present with rootlets still partially intact. Imaging is usually most informative at roughly three to four weeks after injury, once acute hemorrhage has resolved.

Now the clinical patterns. Upper plexus injury, involving the fifth and sixth cervical roots, produces the Erb palsy pattern: loss of shoulder abduction and external rotation and of elbow flexion, with the arm adducted, internally rotated, and the forearm pronated, the so called waiter's tip posture. Hand function is preserved. This is the pattern of a fall onto the shoulder with forced separation of the head and shoulder, and of obstetric injury from shoulder dystocia. Lower plexus injury, involving the eighth cervical and first thoracic roots, produces the Klumpke pattern with intrinsic hand weakness and a claw hand, often with Horner syndrome, classically from traction with the arm abducted overhead. Pan-plexus injury produces a flail, anesthetic arm and carries the worst prognosis.

The common mechanisms are motorcycle accidents with traction, which dominate the adult population, penetrating trauma, iatrogenic injury from positioning or from regional blocks or from surgery in the neck or axilla, radiation induced plexopathy, and obstetric injury. Always consider the neoplastic plexopathy, particularly a Pancoast tumor at the apex of the lung involving the lower trunk, and be able to distinguish radiation plexopathy from tumor recurrence: radiation plexopathy tends to involve the upper plexus, is relatively painless, and shows myokymic discharges on electromyography, whereas tumor recurrence tends to involve the lower plexus, is severely painful, and may show a mass on imaging with Horner syndrome.

Now the evaluation. Begin with the trauma survey, because these patients are frequently polytrauma victims. Specifically assess the vascular status, since subclavian or axillary arterial injury accompanies plexus injury with some frequency and a vascular injury is an emergency. Assess for associated fractures of the clavicle, scapula, cervical spine, and humerus, and for pneumothorax and for a cervical spinal cord injury, which can coexist and confound the examination.

Then perform a meticulous, documented motor and sensory examination using the Medical Research Council grading scale for every muscle group, and repeat it serially, because the trajectory over time is your most important data. Document the vascular examination, the presence or absence of Horner syndrome, and the character and severity of pain.

Electrodiagnostic studies should be obtained at roughly three to four weeks, for the reason discussed under nerve injury generally: denervation changes on electromyography require two to three weeks to develop, and distal conduction studies remain falsely normal before Wallerian degeneration completes. Include paraspinal muscles and sensory nerve action potentials specifically for the preganglionic determination.

Now the management algorithm, which turns entirely on mechanism and timing.

For a sharp penetrating injury with a suspected transection, explore and repair early, within days. For a vascular injury, explore emergently, and the plexus can often be addressed at the same setting or tagged for later. For an open injury with contamination or a blast or avulsive mechanism, debride and tag the ends and return at roughly three weeks.

For a closed traction injury, which is the common scenario, observe and reassess. Many upper trunk stretch injuries recover spontaneously. Obtain baseline electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks and repeat at roughly three months. Look for an advancing Tinel sign and for clinical and electrical evidence of reinnervation. The operative threshold is generally at three to six months without evidence of recovery.

And here is the principle that must anchor your answer: you cannot wait indefinitely, because the motor endplate degrades irreversibly beyond roughly twelve to eighteen months of denervation. Given regeneration at one millimeter per day and the long distances in the upper limb, waiting past six months for a proximal injury means the axons cannot reach the target in time. So for a pan-plexus injury or a documented avulsion, do not wait for spontaneous recovery that will not come; operate early, at roughly three months. For an incomplete upper trunk injury with signs of recovery, you can observe longer. Articulating this tradeoff between giving spontaneous recovery a chance and beating the endplate clock is the core judgment being examined.

Reconstruction follows a priority order, and you should state the priorities before the techniques, because the arm cannot be fully restored and you must choose. The priorities in descending order are: elbow flexion first, then shoulder abduction and stabilization, then hand sensation especially in the median distribution for protective purposes, then wrist and finger extension, then finger flexion, with intrinsic hand function last and generally unachievable in a severe injury.

The techniques are neurolysis where the nerve is in continuity with a positive nerve action potential across the lesion, nerve grafting with sural nerve for postganglionic ruptures with an available proximal stump, and nerve transfer for preganglionic avulsions or long delays. Name the standard transfers: the Oberlin transfer of an ulnar nerve fascicle to the biceps branch of the musculocutaneous nerve for elbow flexion, often performed as a double transfer adding a median fascicle to the brachialis branch; the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve for shoulder abduction and external rotation; and a triceps branch of the radial nerve to the axillary nerve. Other donors include intercostal nerves, the medial pectoral nerve, the thoracodorsal nerve, the phrenic nerve in some practices, and the contralateral seventh cervical root in pan-plexus injury, each with its own tradeoffs. For late presentation beyond the endplate window, pivot to free functioning muscle transfer, typically gracilis, and to tendon transfers, arthrodesis, and pain management.

Pain deserves specific attention. Deafferentation pain after avulsion is often severe and refractory. Medical management with gabapentinoids, tricyclics, and other neuropathic agents comes first. Dorsal root entry zone lesioning is the established surgical procedure for refractory avulsion pain, with good response rates, and naming it demonstrates real command of the topic.

For obstetric brachial plexus palsy, know that a large majority recover spontaneously, that the key prognostic milestone is recovery of biceps function by roughly three months of age, and that absence of elbow flexion at three months is the conventional indication to consider surgical exploration and reconstruction, generally performed between three and nine months.

Finally, know when to refer. Brachial plexus reconstruction is a subspecialized practice with substantial volume dependence. Saying that you would perform the initial assessment, the urgent vascular and open injury management, the serial examinations, and the electrodiagnostic and imaging workup, and then refer to a peripheral nerve center within the operative window, is a completely acceptable and often the correct answer. What is not acceptable is observing a patient into the second year and then referring, and the examiner may construct exactly that scenario to see whether you understand the clock.

Be able to localise precisely using the branches, because that is the anatomy the examiner is really testing. Work proximal to distal. Rhomboid weakness implicates the dorsal scapular nerve and therefore the fifth cervical root itself. Serratus anterior weakness, producing medial scapular winging, implicates the long thoracic nerve from the fifth, sixth and seventh roots, again at root level. Diaphragmatic elevation implicates the phrenic nerve and the fifth root. Supraspinatus and infraspinatus weakness implicates the suprascapular nerve arising from the upper trunk at Erb point. Pectoralis major clavicular head weakness implicates the lateral pectoral nerve from the lateral cord, while the sternal head is medial cord. Latissimus dorsi weakness implicates the thoracodorsal nerve from the posterior cord. Deltoid weakness with preserved triceps suggests an isolated axillary lesion rather than a posterior cord lesion, whereas both weak implicates the posterior cord. Reciting a chain like that, out loud, in order, is worth a great deal.

Add sensory localisation. The medial brachial and medial antebrachial cutaneous nerves arise from the medial cord, so numbness of the medial arm and forearm places the lesion at the medial cord, lower trunk, or the eighth cervical and first thoracic roots, and importantly not in the ulnar nerve. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is the terminal sensory continuation of the musculocutaneous nerve from the lateral cord and supplies the lateral forearm. The axillary nerve supplies the regimental badge patch over the deltoid.

Understand the mechanism in biomechanical terms, since it predicts the pattern. Forced separation of the head and shoulder places maximal traction on the upper roots and produces the Erb pattern; traction on an abducted arm above the head loads the lower roots and produces the Klumpke pattern. The fifth and sixth roots are anchored by strong fibrous attachments to the transverse processes, which is why they tend to rupture postganglionically, whereas the eighth cervical and first thoracic roots lack those attachments and tend to avulse preganglionically. That single anatomical fact explains why lower plexus injuries carry a worse prognosis and more often require transfers rather than grafts.

Know the supporting investigations in more detail. Plain radiographs of the chest may show an elevated hemidiaphragm from phrenic palsy and transverse process or first rib fractures that indicate high energy root level trauma; a clavicle fracture suggests injury at the level of the divisions. Fluoroscopic sniff testing confirms diaphragmatic paralysis. Computed tomographic myelography remains excellent for demonstrating rootlet avulsion and pseudomeningoceles, and high resolution magnetic resonance myelography is increasingly equivalent and avoids an intrathecal puncture; both are most informative at roughly three to four weeks once acute haemorrhage has settled. Angiography is indicated where subclavian or axillary vascular injury is suspected. Serial clinical examination with documented Medical Research Council grading remains the single most valuable investigation.

Expand the operative principles. At exploration the plexus is approached through a supraclavicular incision, extended infraclavicularly and occasionally with clavicular osteotomy for combined lesions. Identify normal anatomy first, working from the scalene triangle, with the phrenic nerve on the anterior surface of anterior scalene running lateral to medial and the long thoracic nerve piercing middle scalene; these are both landmarks and structures to protect. Use intraoperative nerve action potential recording across each element and intraoperative somatosensory and motor evoked potentials to distinguish a recovering element from a neuroma in continuity. Neurolyse where a nerve action potential is present, and resect to healthy fascicles and reconstruct where it is absent.

Prioritise reconstruction explicitly and in order, because the arm cannot be fully restored. Elbow flexion first, because it converts a flail arm into a useful one. Then shoulder stability, abduction and external rotation. Then protective hand sensation, especially in the median distribution. Then wrist and finger extension, then finger flexion. Intrinsic function last and generally unachievable. Match the technique to the lesion: graft a postganglionic rupture with an available proximal stump; transfer for a preganglionic avulsion or when the regeneration distance would defeat the endplate clock. Where an entire plexus is avulsed, extraplexal donors become necessary, including intercostal nerves, the spinal accessory nerve, the phrenic nerve in some practices, and the contralateral seventh cervical root, each with real trade offs that you should acknowledge rather than gloss.

Handle pain as a primary problem rather than an afterthought. Deafferentation pain after avulsion is often immediate, severe and refractory, described as crushing or burning in an anaesthetic limb. Start with gabapentinoids, tricyclics and serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, add psychological and rehabilitation support, and know that dorsal root entry zone lesioning is the established surgical treatment for refractory avulsion pain with good long term response rates. Spinal cord stimulation is generally ineffective in complete avulsion because the dorsal columns are deafferented, which is a nuance worth knowing.

Finally, frame the whole case around the clock and around referral. Serial examination, electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks, imaging at three to four weeks, reassessment at three months, and operation by three to six months in the absence of recovery, because regeneration at one millimetre per day must reach the target before the motor endplate degrades at roughly twelve to eighteen months. Sharp injuries, vascular injuries and documented avulsions do not wait. And because plexus reconstruction is volume dependent and subspecialised, the correct answer is often that you would perform the resuscitation, the vascular and open injury management, the serial assessment and the imaging, and refer to a peripheral nerve centre inside the operative window. Referring late is the failure; referring early is good practice.

To summarize. Know the roots, trunks, divisions, cords, and branches, and know that the dorsal scapular, long thoracic, and phrenic nerves come off proximally and therefore localize proximally. Distinguish preganglionic from postganglionic using Horner syndrome, winging, diaphragm elevation, paraspinal denervation, severe deafferentation pain, preserved sensory nerve action potentials in an anesthetic limb, and pseudomeningoceles on myelography. Electrodiagnostics at three to four weeks. Operate early for sharp injuries, vascular injuries, and documented avulsions; observe traction injuries to three to six months. Reconstruct in priority order starting with elbow flexion. Beat the twelve to eighteen month endplate deadline. And refer to a plexus center within the window rather than after it.
