Let's master systematic interpretation of the head CT for the oral boards.

This is the single most important imaging skill you will demonstrate in the examination room. The examiner slides a scan across the table and says nothing. What happens in the next fifteen seconds determines the tone of the entire case. Candidates fail here not because they cannot recognize an epidural hematoma, but because they blurt out the obvious finding, anchor on it, and miss the second abnormality that changes management. Let's build a system that makes that impossible.

Start with the discipline of the opening statement. Before you describe anything, orient yourself out loud. Say what the study is, what sequence or window you are looking at, and whether there is contrast. Something like: this is a non-contrast axial CT of the head, displayed in brain windows. This takes three seconds, it is always correct, and it buys you time to actually look at the film. It also signals to the examiner that you are systematic rather than reactive. If you cannot tell whether contrast was given, say so and explain how you would know: contrast enhancement of the vessels and the choroid plexus, and a higher attenuation in the dural venous sinuses.

Understand what the greyscale actually means, because the examiner may ask you to justify a call. Attenuation is measured in Hounsfield units, a scale anchored at minus one thousand for air and zero for water. Fat sits at roughly minus one hundred, cerebrospinal fluid at zero to fifteen, white matter at roughly twenty five to thirty, grey matter at roughly thirty five to forty, acute clotted blood at roughly fifty to one hundred, and cortical bone above one thousand. Window width and window level determine what you can see: a narrow brain window of roughly eighty width and forty level maximizes grey white contrast, a subdural window of roughly one hundred fifty to two hundred width pulls a thin collection away from the adjacent inner table, and a bone window of around two thousand width shows fracture lines. If you suspect a thin subdural and cannot see it, ask explicitly for wider windows. That request alone earns credit because it shows you understand the physics rather than just the pictures.

Now the search pattern. Use the blood can be very bad mnemonic, which walks you from the outside in and forces you to touch every compartment. Blood. Cisterns. Brain. Ventricles. Bone. If you prefer a different order, that is fine, but you must have one and you must use it every single time, including when the answer is obvious. The examiner is watching for the system as much as the finding.

Begin with blood. Hyperdense material on a non-contrast scan measures roughly fifty to one hundred Hounsfield units in the acute phase, compared with grey matter at around thirty five to forty and white matter at around twenty five to thirty. Your job is to localize blood to a compartment, because the compartment determines the diagnosis. Epidural blood is lens shaped, or biconvex, does not cross suture lines, but does cross dural reflections such as the falx and tentorium. Subdural blood is crescent shaped, crosses suture lines freely, but does not cross the midline at the falx. Subarachnoid blood fills the cisterns and sulci and follows the contours of the brain surface. Intraparenchymal blood sits within the substance of the brain. Intraventricular blood layers dependently within the ventricular system. Name the compartment before you name the diagnosis, and you will never confuse an epidural with a subdural under pressure.

Know why the shapes differ, because that is the anatomy question hiding behind the pattern. The dura is adherent to the inner table at the sutures, so epidural blood collecting between bone and dura is confined by those attachments and balloons into a lens. The subdural space has no such tethering, so blood spreads freely across the convexity, but it cannot cross the falx because the falx is a dural reflection that reaches the surface. Epidural blood classically comes from a torn middle meningeal artery under arterial pressure, which is why it expands fast and why the lucid interval exists; subdural blood classically comes from torn bridging veins, which is why it is commoner in the elderly and in alcoholics whose atrophied brains stretch those veins.

Learn to estimate hematoma volume out loud, because the examiner will ask how big it is and a number is worth more than an adjective. Use the ABC over two method: multiply the greatest diameter on the slice with the largest clot, by the diameter perpendicular to it on that same slice, by the number of slices containing clot times the slice thickness, then divide by two. That approximates the ellipsoid volume in cubic centimetres. Being able to say this is roughly forty cubic centimetres rather than this looks big is exactly the difference the examiner is grading.

Then attach the numbers to decisions. For an epidural hematoma, the commonly cited surgical thresholds are a volume greater than thirty cubic centimetres regardless of the Glasgow Coma Scale, or a thickness greater than fifteen millimetres, or midline shift greater than five millimetres, or any epidural in a patient with a Glasgow Coma Scale below nine with anisocoria, which goes to the operating room immediately. For an acute subdural hematoma, the thresholds are thickness greater than ten millimetres or midline shift greater than five millimetres regardless of the Glasgow Coma Scale; and in a patient with a Glasgow Coma Scale below nine, operate also if the score has dropped by two or more points, if the pupils are asymmetric or fixed, or if the intracranial pressure exceeds twenty millimetres of mercury. Quote these as guideline thresholds informing judgement rather than as absolute rules, and always state that the clinical examination and its trajectory govern.

Two traps deserve specific mention. First, the isodense subdural hematoma. Between roughly one and three weeks, a subdural collection passes through a phase where its attenuation matches that of the adjacent cortex, and it can become nearly invisible. Your clues are indirect: effacement of the sulci on one side, inward buckling of the grey white junction, midline shift that seems disproportionate to any visible lesion, and a ventricle that is compressed without an obvious cause. If the shift does not match what you can see, say that out loud and ask for contrast or an MRI. Second, the bilateral subdural. Symmetric bilateral collections cancel out the midline shift, so the brain looks central and you relax. Look instead at the ventricles, which will appear small and slit like, and at the sulci, which will be effaced across both convexities.

Add two more traps that catch candidates. A hyperdense collection in an anaemic patient can look deceptively isodense, because clot attenuation tracks haemoglobin concentration; conversely a patient on anticoagulation may show a fluid level or a hypodense layer within a hyperdense clot, which indicates unclotted blood and active bleeding. And a vertex subdural is easily missed because axial slices cut it tangentially; if the history fits and the convexity looks clean, ask for coronal reformats.

Next, the cisterns. This is the part candidates skip, and it is the part that tells you whether the patient is about to die. Walk the basal cisterns in order: the suprasellar cistern, which should look like a five or six pointed star, the ambient cisterns wrapping around the midbrain, the quadrigeminal cistern behind the midbrain, which should look like a smile or a W, and the fourth ventricle and prepontine cistern. Two questions for each: is it filled with blood, and is it effaced? Blood in the basal cisterns in a patient without significant trauma means aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise, and your next sentence should be that you would obtain a CT angiogram. Effacement of these cisterns means the brainstem is being compressed and that herniation is imminent or in progress. If you see an effaced quadrigeminal cistern, stop describing and start managing. The examiner wants to see you recognize a surgical emergency.

Use the pattern of subarachnoid blood to localize the aneurysm before the angiogram comes back. Blood dominant in the anterior interhemispheric fissure suggests an anterior communicating artery aneurysm; blood dominant in one Sylvian fissure suggests a middle cerebral bifurcation aneurysm; blood confined to the perimesencephalic cisterns with a normal angiogram suggests benign perimesencephalic non-aneurysmal haemorrhage; and isolated posterior fossa or fourth ventricular blood suggests a posterior inferior cerebellar artery aneurysm. Then grade it. State the Hunt and Hess grade from the clinical picture and the modified Fisher grade from the scan, since the modified Fisher grade is what predicts vasospasm: thin subarachnoid blood without intraventricular haemorrhage is grade one, thin blood with intraventricular haemorrhage grade two, thick blood without intraventricular haemorrhage grade three, and thick blood with intraventricular haemorrhage grade four.

Then the brain itself. Work through it in a fixed order and compare right with left at every level, because the contralateral side is your built in control. Assess the grey white differentiation, which is the earliest sign of ischemia and of cerebral edema. Loss of the insular ribbon and obscuration of the lentiform nucleus are classic early middle cerebral artery infarct signs, often visible within the first few hours when the rest of the scan looks normal. Look for a hyperdense vessel sign, where the clot itself is visible as a bright middle cerebral artery or a bright basilar artery. Assess for mass effect: sulcal effacement, ventricular compression, and midline shift. Measure the shift at the septum pellucidum and state the number in millimeters, because the examiner will ask. Look for any focal hypodensity, which means edema, infarct, or an old encephalomalacic cavity, and for any focal hyperdensity beyond blood, which could be calcification or a densely cellular tumor.

For the stroke case, be able to score it. The Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score divides the middle cerebral artery territory into ten regions and subtracts a point for each showing early ischaemic change, so ten is normal and a score of six or less indicates a large established core that predicts poor response to reperfusion and higher haemorrhagic risk. Saying the score, then saying that you would proceed to CT angiography and perfusion to define the occlusion and the core-penumbra mismatch, is a complete and current answer.

For trauma, know the two classifications the examiner may reach for. The Marshall classification grades diffuse injury by the state of the cisterns and the degree of midline shift and by whether a mass lesion has been evacuated. The Rotterdam score is the more predictive modern tool, scoring basal cistern status, midline shift, the presence of an epidural mass lesion, and intraventricular or subarachnoid blood. You do not need to recite every tier, but you should know that both are driven principally by the cisterns and the shift, which is precisely why your search pattern puts the cisterns second.

Recognize the injuries that hide on CT. Diffuse axonal injury classically produces small petechial haemorrhages at the grey white junction, in the corpus callosum particularly at the splenium, and in the dorsolateral brainstem, and the CT may look almost normal in a deeply comatose patient. When the clinical picture and the scan disagree that badly, say so and ask for magnetic resonance with susceptibility weighted imaging, which is far more sensitive. Likewise, remember contrecoup injury: a patient struck occipitally will often have inferior frontal and anterior temporal contusions on the opposite side, because those are the regions that scrape across the rough floor of the anterior and middle fossae. If you see scalp swelling at one pole, deliberately examine the opposite pole.

Then the ventricles. Are they enlarged, and if so, is the pattern communicating or obstructive? Temporal horn dilation is your most sensitive early sign of hydrocephalus, because the temporal horns should be barely visible in a normal adult. Look for transependymal flow, the periventricular hypodensity that indicates acute pressure. Then ask the critical question: where is the obstruction? Trace the pathway. Enlarged lateral ventricles with a normal third and fourth ventricle points to aqueductal stenosis. Enlargement of all four ventricles points to a communicating process, such as blood or infection or a fourth ventricular outlet problem. Enlarged lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth localizes to the aqueduct. This is a favorite examiner sequence, and it is pure anatomy.

Distinguish hydrocephalus from atrophy while you are there, because the examiner may hand you an elderly patient's scan and watch what you do. In hydrocephalus the ventricles are disproportionately enlarged relative to the sulci, the temporal horns are prominent, the third ventricle is rounded, and the frontal horns are convex. In atrophy the sulci enlarge in proportion to the ventricles. The Evans index, the ratio of the maximal width of the frontal horns to the maximal inner skull diameter on the same axial slice, is abnormal above zero point three and supports ventriculomegaly.

Finally, the bone and the extracranial soft tissues. Switch to bone windows and say so explicitly, because forgetting bone windows is one of the most common and most punished errors. In bone windows, look for fractures, particularly of the calvarium, the skull base, and the temporal bone. Distinguish a fracture from a suture line: sutures are serrated, symmetric, and occur in expected locations, whereas fractures are sharp, lucent, and often asymmetric. Look for pneumocephalus, because air inside the head means a communication with the outside world, either through a fracture, the sinuses, or the mastoid air cells, and that raises the question of a cerebrospinal fluid leak and meningitis risk. Check the air sinuses for opacification, which may indicate a basilar skull fracture with blood. Check the scalp for soft tissue swelling, which tells you where the impact was and directs you to look harder at the underlying brain and at the contrecoup site.

Two bone-window specifics are worth having ready. A depressed fracture is generally considered for elevation when the depression exceeds the thickness of the adjacent skull, and an open depressed fracture raises the question of debridement and antibiotics; over a dural venous sinus, however, elevation risks catastrophic bleeding and conservative management is often wiser. And tension pneumocephalus, in which air under pressure splays the frontal lobes apart to produce the Mount Fuji sign, is a surgical emergency requiring decompression rather than observation.

Now let's talk about how to say all of this. The examiner does not want a monologue. Build your read-out in three parts. First, the headline finding with its measurements and location. Second, the secondary findings and the mass effect. Third, your interpretation and your immediate next step. For example: this is a non-contrast axial head CT in brain windows. There is a large right sided biconvex hyperdense extra-axial collection over the temporoparietal convexity measuring approximately two centimeters in maximal thickness, consistent with an acute epidural hematoma. There is effacement of the right lateral ventricle and approximately eight millimeters of midline shift to the left. The basal cisterns are patent but the right ambient cistern is narrowed. On bone windows I would look for an overlying temporal bone fracture and for injury to the middle meningeal artery groove. This is a surgical epidural hematoma and I would take this patient to the operating room emergently for craniotomy and evacuation while ensuring the coagulation profile is checked and reversed if abnormal.

That structure demonstrates recognition, thoroughness, and decisiveness in about thirty seconds. Practice it until it is automatic.

A few points of examination strategy. When you do not know, describe. A precise description of an abnormality you cannot name is far better than a confident wrong diagnosis, and the examiner will often accept the description and move on. Say what you see, where it is, and what you would do to narrow it down. Second, never say the scan is normal without completing your search. If you have genuinely completed the system and see nothing, say that you have reviewed blood, cisterns, brain, ventricles, and bone and identified no acute abnormality, and then ask for the additional imaging you would want. A normal scan in the examination room usually means the pathology is on the next study. Third, always ask for the prior study if one might exist. Comparison with a prior is legitimate clinical practice and examiners rarely penalize it, though they may tell you none is available.

Know the artifacts so you do not chase them. Beam hardening between the dense petrous bones produces the Hounsfield dark bands across the posterior fossa that can mimic or conceal a lesion, which is the single greatest weakness of CT in the posterior fossa. Motion produces streaking and blurred margins. Metallic streak artifact from dental work, clips, or coils obscures adjacent tissue. Partial volume averaging at the vertex and the skull base blurs small lesions. Naming an artifact as an artifact, and then saying what study you would use instead, is a far stronger answer than describing a lesion that does not exist.

Adapt to the paediatric patient, because the thresholds change. Children have open sutures and fontanelles, so they tolerate volume expansion differently; the normal paediatric brain is more water dense with less developed myelin, so grey white differentiation looks different at different ages. Radiation dose matters enormously across a lifetime of imaging, so state that you would use a dose reduction protocol, rapid sequence magnetic resonance where available, and ultrasound through an open fontanelle in infants. And always ask whether the injury pattern fits the history: multiple hemorrhages of differing ages, interhemispheric subdural blood, and skull fractures inconsistent with the reported mechanism should raise non-accidental trauma, which obliges a skeletal survey, ophthalmological examination for retinal haemorrhages, and child protection involvement.

Anticipate the follow up questions. If you identify a hemorrhage, expect to be asked about the coagulation status, the blood pressure target, and the reversal agent. If you identify hydrocephalus, expect to be asked where you would place the drain and at what level you would set it. If you identify herniation, expect to be asked about hyperosmolar therapy, hyperventilation, and the timing of decompression. If you identify a mass, expect to be asked what sequence you want next and what your differential is based on the location and the enhancement pattern.

Finally, understand the limits of the modality so you can state them. Non-contrast CT is superb for acute blood, bone, and mass effect, and it is fast and available. It is poor for the posterior fossa because of beam hardening artifact from the surrounding bone, poor for early ischemia in the first several hours, poor for small lesions near the vertex and the skull base, and poor for anything requiring soft tissue contrast resolution such as a small metastasis or a low grade glioma. Sensitivity for subarachnoid hemorrhage is very high within the first six hours, approaching ninety eight to one hundred percent with a modern scanner and an expert reader, but it falls off substantially after twelve to twenty four hours as the blood degrades. That decay is exactly why the lumbar puncture question exists, and knowing the number lets you answer it cleanly.

To summarize the framework you must carry into the room. Orient yourself out loud. Run blood, cisterns, brain, ventricles, bone, every single time. Localize blood to a compartment before naming a diagnosis. Estimate volume with ABC over two and attach the number to a surgical threshold. Treat effaced basal cisterns as an emergency. Always look at bone windows. Deliver your read in three parts: headline, secondary findings, plan. Describe rather than guess when uncertain. Do this consistently and the imaging portion of the oral examination becomes the part of the case where you build credibility rather than lose it.
