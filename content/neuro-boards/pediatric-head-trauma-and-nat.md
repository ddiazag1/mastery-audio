Welcome back. This session covers pediatric head trauma and non-accidental trauma, a topic that is both clinically critical and heavily tested on the oral boards. You will be expected to recognize injury patterns, initiate the correct workup without hesitation, and navigate the medical, surgical, and legal dimensions of suspected child abuse. Let's build this from the ground up.

Let's start with the foundation. Traumatic brain injury is the leading cause of death and disability in children. That statement alone tells you why this topic matters, but what makes pediatric head trauma fundamentally different from adult head trauma is the anatomy and physiology of the developing brain. Children are not small adults, and the examiner will expect you to articulate exactly why.

First, the head-to-body ratio. In infants and young children, the head is proportionally much larger relative to the body compared to adults. This raises the center of gravity, which is why young children fall head-first far more often than adults do. Second, the calvarium is thinner in children, providing less bony protection against impact forces. Third, and critically important, the pediatric brain is unmyelinated or incompletely myelinated, which makes it significantly more susceptible to shear injury. Myelin provides structural integrity to axons, and without it, acceleration-deceleration forces cause diffuse axonal injury far more readily. Fourth, in infants, the fontanelles and cranial sutures remain open. This is a double-edged sword. Open sutures can accommodate volume expansion, meaning that an infant can harbor a large subdural hematoma or significant cerebral edema before showing obvious signs of increased intracranial pressure. This leads to delayed presentation, which is why infants with abusive head trauma are so frequently missed on initial evaluation. Fifth, children are more susceptible to diffuse injury patterns rather than the focal contusions and lacerations you see in adults. When you put all of this together, you understand why the same mechanism that might cause a concussion in an adult can cause devastating diffuse injury in an infant.

Now, injury patterns vary by age, and the examiner may test you on this. In infants under one year of age, the most common mechanisms are falls and non-accidental trauma. Non-accidental trauma is in fact the leading cause of traumatic death in this age group, and that fact alone should make your antenna go up every time you see an injured infant. In toddlers, ages one through four, falls remain the primary mechanism, but non-accidental trauma is still very much in the differential. In school-age children, ages five through fourteen, you see bicycle injuries, sports-related trauma, and pedestrian versus vehicle injuries. In adolescents, the pattern shifts to motor vehicle collisions, sports injuries, and assaults.

Now let's go deep on non-accidental trauma. The terminology has evolved. The older term shaken baby syndrome has been replaced by the more accurate term abusive head trauma, abbreviated AHT. This change reflects our understanding that the mechanism is not always isolated shaking. It can be violent shaking, direct impact, or a combination of both. The pathophysiology is driven by acceleration-deceleration forces. When an infant is violently shaken, the brain moves within the skull. Bridging veins, which span the subdural space from the cortical surface to the dural sinuses, are torn by these forces, producing subdural hematomas. The same forces damage retinal vessels, producing retinal hemorrhages. And the shearing forces across the poorly myelinated white matter cause diffuse axonal injury.

This brings us to the classic triad of abusive head trauma, and you must know this cold. The triad is subdural hematomas, often bilateral and of varying ages, retinal hemorrhages that are multilayered and too numerous to count, and encephalopathy, which manifests as seizures, apnea, or altered consciousness. When you see this triad in an infant with no adequate trauma history, you are looking at abusive head trauma until proven otherwise. That is not a suggestion. That is the standard of care.

Before we move on from foundational concepts, you need to understand two additional pediatric-specific entities. The first is scalp hematoma in infants. A subgaleal hematoma in a neonate or young infant can be life-threatening. The subgaleal space is a potential space between the periosteum and the galea aponeurotica, and it is vast. In an infant whose total blood volume is approximately 80 milliliters per kilogram, a large subgaleal hematoma can sequester enough blood to cause hemorrhagic shock. You must recognize this and resuscitate accordingly. By contrast, a cephalohematoma is subperiosteal, does not cross suture lines, much like an epidural hematoma does not cross suture lines, and is usually benign and self-limited.

The second entity is the growing skull fracture, which is unique to children under three years of age. The sequence is as follows. A linear skull fracture occurs in a young child. If there is an underlying dural tear, which there often is in this age group because the dura is tightly adherent to the inner table, then brain pulsations prevent the fracture from healing. Over weeks to months, the fracture edges progressively separate, and brain and meninges herniate through the gap, forming a leptomeningeal cyst. The child presents with an enlarging palpable skull defect. CT confirms widened fracture diastasis with brain or meninges herniating through. Treatment is surgical: you repair the dura and perform cranioplasty. The board pearl here is that any linear skull fracture in a child under three should have follow-up imaging to ensure it is healing and not evolving into a growing fracture.

Now let's move into the clinical case. A four-month-old infant is brought to the emergency department by the parents. They report that the baby rolled off the couch and has not been acting right since. On your assessment, the infant is not tracking visually, is not crying, and has diffusely decreased tone. The anterior fontanelle is full and tense. There is no external bruising visible on the scalp or body. You obtain a CT of the head, which shows bilateral thin subdural hematomas with mixed densities, indicating both acute and chronic blood. There is diffuse cerebral edema with loss of gray-white matter differentiation. No skull fracture is identified.

Pause here and think about what this case is telling you. Now let's walk through the examiner interrogation, because this is where the oral board gets intense.

The examiner may open with a statement rather than a question. "This presentation is concerning for non-accidental trauma." Your response is affirmative, and you explain the red flags systematically. First, the patient is an infant, the highest-risk age group for abusive head trauma. Second, there are bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages. The mixed density on CT, showing both acute hyperdense blood and chronic hypodense or isodense blood, means there have been multiple bleeding events at different times. This is not a single-event injury. Third, the mechanism provided by the parents is inconsistent with the injury severity. A baby rolling off a couch is a short fall from less than three feet. Short falls from household furniture extremely rarely cause significant intracranial injury. The landmark data from Chadwick demonstrated that falls from less than four feet cause fatal head injury in only 0.02 per million falls. Bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages with diffuse cerebral edema requires far greater force or repetitive injury. The mechanism is implausible, and the discrepancy between the reported history and the imaging findings is itself a red flag. Fourth, the absence of a skull fracture actually supports a shaking mechanism rather than impact, though both mechanisms can coexist.

The examiner will then expect you to lay out the mandatory workup for suspected non-accidental trauma, and the word mandatory is deliberate. There are four components you must not omit.

First, ophthalmology consultation. You need a dilated fundoscopic examination performed by an ophthalmologist, not just your own bedside exam. Retinal hemorrhages are present in 60 to 85 percent of abusive head trauma cases. The pattern matters enormously. In abusive head trauma, retinal hemorrhages are multilayered, meaning they occur at the preretinal, intraretinal, and subretinal levels simultaneously. They are too numerous to count and extend to the periphery of the retina. This pattern is highly specific for abusive head trauma. Now, it is true that a few scattered retinal hemorrhages can occur with accidental trauma, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or coagulopathy, but extensive multilayered hemorrhages extending to the retinal periphery are virtually pathognomonic for abusive head trauma. If the examiner asks whether your own fundoscopic exam is sufficient, the answer is no.

Second, skeletal survey. This includes anteroposterior and lateral views of all long bones, chest, pelvis, skull, and spine. You are looking for healing fractures of different ages. The findings most specific for abuse are classic metaphyseal corner fractures, also called bucket-handle fractures, which occur at the metaphyseal-epiphyseal junction from traction and twisting forces. Posterior rib fractures are also highly specific for abuse because they result from anteroposterior compression of the chest, as occurs when an infant is squeezed. Spiral fractures of long bones in non-ambulatory infants are suspicious because a child who cannot walk should not have torsional forces applied to their extremities. A critical follow-up point: you should repeat the skeletal survey in two weeks. Healing fractures that are not visible on the initial survey become more apparent as periosteal reaction and callus formation develop.

Third, laboratory workup. You order a complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, coagulation studies including PT, PTT, and fibrinogen to rule out a bleeding diathesis, lipase to screen for abdominal trauma, urinalysis to evaluate for renal trauma, and liver enzymes to detect hepatic contusion. The coagulation studies are particularly important because the defense in legal proceedings will often raise bleeding disorders as an alternative explanation.

Fourth, social work consultation and child protective services notification. This is mandatory. You are a mandated reporter in all fifty states. The legal threshold is reasonable suspicion of abuse, not confirmed abuse. You do not wait for ophthalmology results, skeletal survey interpretation, or lab values before making the report. Delaying the report to gather more evidence can endanger the child and any siblings who may be in the same household. Report immediately.

You should also obtain an MRI of the brain once the patient is hemodynamically stable, typically within 24 to 72 hours. MRI is more sensitive than CT for detecting diffuse axonal injury, for dating hemorrhages based on hemoglobin degradation products, and for identifying ischemic injury. Restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging indicates cytotoxic edema and carries a poor prognosis.

Now the examiner shifts to management. For medical management, you begin with the ABCs. If the GCS equivalent is critically low, you intubate for airway protection and ventilatory control. Maintain normoxia and avoid hypotension, which is particularly damaging in the pediatric brain. Hypotension in an infant is defined as a systolic blood pressure below 60 millimeters of mercury in a neonate or below 70 millimeters of mercury plus twice the age in years for older infants and children. Even a single episode of hypotension in the setting of pediatric TBI doubles mortality.

ICP management follows the same principles as in adults, but with age-adjusted thresholds. In infants, an ICP greater than 20 millimeters of mercury is concerning. Cerebral perfusion pressure targets are age-dependent per the Kochanek and Adelson pediatric TBI guidelines: 40 to 50 millimeters of mercury for infants and 50 to 60 millimeters of mercury for older children. Hyperosmolar therapy with 3 percent hypertonic saline is preferred over mannitol in infants because mannitol can cause hypotension in small children with limited intravascular volume. The typical bolus dose of 3 percent saline is 2 to 5 milliliters per kilogram. You institute seizure prophylaxis and temperature management, avoiding hyperthermia aggressively because elevated temperature worsens secondary brain injury. The open fontanelle can give you a bedside estimation of intracranial pressure, a bulging tense fontanelle suggests elevated ICP, but it is unreliable for quantitative monitoring. If the GCS equivalent is 8 or less, you place a formal ICP monitor.

For surgical management, if there is a large acute subdural hematoma with significant mass effect, you proceed with surgical evacuation via craniotomy. Position the infant supine with the head turned, and be aware that blood loss during craniotomy in an infant can be proportionally massive relative to total blood volume. Have blood products immediately available and coordinate with anesthesia for aggressive volume resuscitation. If the infant has chronic subdural collections, you can perform subdural taps through the anterior fontanelle using a short-bevel needle inserted at the lateral corner of the fontanelle, angled laterally. No more than 15 to 20 milliliters should be drained per side per session to avoid rapid shifts in intracranial contents. These taps can be repeated as needed. If chronic subdural collections recur despite repeated taps, a subdural-peritoneal shunt is an option. For refractory intracranial hypertension that does not respond to maximal medical therapy, decompressive craniectomy is considered.

The examiner will likely ask about prognosis. The outcomes of abusive head trauma are devastating. Mortality ranges from 15 to 25 percent. Among survivors, 50 to 60 percent have significant neurological disability, including cognitive impairment, motor deficits, seizure disorders, visual impairment, and behavioral disorders. Factors that predict worse outcomes include bilateral diffuse injury, loss of gray-white matter differentiation on the initial CT, and restricted diffusion on MRI.

The examiner may then probe your knowledge of the differential diagnosis. You must be prepared to exclude several conditions. Bleeding diathesis, including von Willebrand disease and hemophilia, is excluded by the coagulation studies you already ordered. Glutaric aciduria type 1 is an organic acidemia that causes subdural collections and macrocephaly. It is diagnosed by urine organic acids and should be considered in any infant with unexplained subdural collections. Osteogenesis imperfecta can present with multiple fractures, but classic features include blue sclerae and a positive family history, and the diagnosis is confirmed with genetic testing. Benign enlargement of the subarachnoid spaces, or BESS, causes benign macrocrania in infants with enlarged subarachnoid spaces. This is not the same as subdural hematomas, and the two should not be confused. Birth trauma can cause subdural hematoma from vacuum or forceps delivery, but these typically resolve within the first few weeks of life and would not explain subdural collections in a four-month-old.

Now for the trap questions, and the examiner will set these up carefully. The first trap: "The parents say the baby fell from the couch. Is that mechanism sufficient to cause bilateral subdural hematomas with cerebral edema?" The answer is no. Short falls from household furniture, less than three feet or one meter, extremely rarely cause significant intracranial injury. Bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages with diffuse cerebral edema requires forces far exceeding what a short fall generates. The mechanism is implausible, and the discrepancy between the history and the injury pattern is a red flag for non-accidental trauma.

The second trap: "Would you report to child protective services before confirming the diagnosis?" The answer is yes. As a mandated reporter, you are legally required to report suspicion of abuse, not confirmed abuse. The threshold is reasonable suspicion based on the clinical picture. You have bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages, an implausible mechanism, and a critically ill infant. That exceeds the threshold for reasonable suspicion. Delaying the report to await additional test results can endanger the child and any siblings living in the same home.

The third trap, and this one is more nuanced: "A six-month-old has an isolated linear parietal skull fracture after a witnessed fall from a changing table. Is this abuse?" The answer is not necessarily. Isolated linear skull fractures from short falls do occur in infants and are in fact the most common accidental skull injury in this age group. The key questions are: does the mechanism match the injury? Was the fall genuinely witnessed by a reliable observer? Are there any other concerning features, such as unexplained bruising, retinal hemorrhages, or additional fractures? An isolated linear parietal fracture from a credible witnessed fall is likely accidental. However, you still perform appropriate screening, including a thorough physical exam and consideration of the clinical context.

Let's address legal considerations, because this will come up. Document everything meticulously. Record direct quotes from caregivers verbatim. Note any timeline inconsistencies. Describe all physical exam findings in detail and obtain clinical photographs when possible. Document every consultation you ordered and every result you received. Your medical record may become a legal document in criminal proceedings and custody hearings. Do not make accusations. Do not write that the parents caused the injuries. Instead, document facts objectively and use language such as "findings concerning for abusive head trauma" or "injury pattern inconsistent with the reported mechanism."

Let's review the key evidence you should be aware of. The Kochanek pediatric TBI guidelines, updated in 2019, provide the evidence-based framework for medical management of pediatric traumatic brain injury, including the age-adjusted ICP and CPP thresholds we discussed. The Chadwick data on short-fall mortality established that falls from less than four feet are essentially never fatal, providing the biomechanical foundation for questioning implausible mechanisms. The Duhaime biomechanical studies clarified the forces involved in shaking and impact, showing that shaking alone generates sufficient force to cause bridging vein tears and diffuse axonal injury. The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report on abusive head trauma, published in 2020, provides the current consensus on diagnosis and management. And critically, the Jenny study demonstrated that 30 percent of abusive head trauma cases are missed on initial presentation, usually because the physician did not consider abuse in the differential. In the Jenny study, the missed cases were more likely to involve younger infants, white families, and two-parent households, highlighting the danger of implicit bias in who we consider capable of abuse. Of the children whose abuse was initially missed, 28 percent were re-injured before the correct diagnosis was made, and some of those re-injuries were fatal. That statistic should haunt you every time you evaluate an injured infant.

Let's close with the board pearls that will anchor this topic in your memory. Bilateral subdural hematomas plus retinal hemorrhages plus encephalopathy in an infant equals abusive head trauma until proven otherwise. You are a mandated reporter, and you report suspicion, not confirmed diagnosis. Skeletal survey, ophthalmology consultation, and social work involvement are mandatory, not optional, in any case of suspected non-accidental trauma. Short falls from household furniture do not cause bilateral subdural hematomas of different ages with diffuse cerebral edema. Open fontanelles in infants can mask intracranial hypertension by accommodating volume expansion, leading to delayed presentation. Growing skull fractures are unique to children under three and require surgical repair of the dura. Subgaleal hematomas in neonates can cause hemorrhagic shock because the potential space is large relative to the infant's total blood volume. Document facts, not accusations, because your medical record may become a legal exhibit.

That covers pediatric head trauma and non-accidental trauma. You're ready for this one.
