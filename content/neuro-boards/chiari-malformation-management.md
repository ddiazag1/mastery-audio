Welcome back. This session covers Chiari malformation management, a topic that appears frequently on the ABNS oral boards and one where the examiners expect precise definitions, clear surgical decision-making, and an understanding of when not to operate. We are going to work through all the Chiari subtypes, walk through a clinical case, and then I am going to hit you with the exact questions an examiner would ask. Let's get into it.

Let's start with the foundation. Chiari malformations are a group of structural hindbrain anomalies defined by varying degrees of cerebellar and brainstem descent through the foramen magnum. There are several distinct types and you need to know every one of them.

Chiari I is the most common type presenting in adulthood. It is defined as cerebellar tonsillar ectopia greater than 5 millimeters below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. That 5 millimeter threshold is what you cite on boards. Now, tonsillar descent between 3 and 5 millimeters is considered borderline, and in that range, clinical correlation is essential. A patient with 4 millimeters of tonsillar ectopia and classic Valsalva-provoked suboccipital headaches plus a syrinx is still clinically significant despite not meeting the strict radiographic cutoff. Chiari I is not associated with myelomeningocele. That distinction from Chiari II is critical and the examiners will test you on it. The pathophysiology centers on obstruction of normal CSF flow at the craniocervical junction. The ectopic tonsils act as a plug at the foramen magnum, impeding the pulsatile flow of cerebrospinal fluid between the intracranial and spinal subarachnoid spaces. This obstruction creates a pressure differential that drives CSF into the central canal of the spinal cord, leading to syrinx formation. Syringomyelia is present in 25 to 65 percent of Chiari I patients, depending on the series. That is a wide range, but the association is strong enough that every Chiari I workup demands full spine imaging.

Chiari 1.5 is an intermediate entity. It has tonsillar ectopia like Chiari I, but it also has brainstem descent, specifically the obex descending below the foramen magnum. Think of it as a more severe form of Chiari I with caudal migration of both the tonsils and the lower brainstem. These patients tend to have more severe symptoms and may have higher rates of syringomyelia. The surgical approach is similar to Chiari I but the examiner wants to know you recognize this as a distinct and more severe pattern.

Chiari II is a different disease entirely. This involves herniation of the cerebellar vermis, the fourth ventricle, and the brainstem through the foramen magnum. It is always associated with myelomeningocele or open spinal dysraphism. Always. If someone says Chiari II without myelomeningocele, that is incorrect by definition. Chiari II presents in infancy and childhood. These patients have a constellation of findings including hydrocephalus in approximately 90 percent of cases, brainstem dysfunction, cranial nerve palsies, and the characteristic lemon sign and banana sign on prenatal ultrasound. The tectal beaking on MRI is another hallmark. Symptomatic brainstem compression in Chiari II can be life-threatening, and I will come back to that in the trap questions.

Chiari III is rare and severe. It involves an occipital or high cervical encephalocele that contains cerebellar tissue. These patients have herniation of posterior fossa contents into an encephalocele sac. The prognosis is poor and the condition is typically identified prenatally or at birth.

Chiari 0 is a controversial entity. These patients have syringomyelia with demonstrable CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum, but they do not have tonsillar ectopia. The tonsils are in a normal position. The concept is that you can have the same pathophysiological mechanism, CSF flow obstruction at the craniocervical junction, without the classic radiographic finding of tonsillar descent. Some centers treat these patients with posterior fossa decompression and report syrinx improvement, which supports the concept. Others remain skeptical about the diagnosis. For boards, know that Chiari 0 exists in the literature, acknowledge it, and state that it remains debated.

Now let's talk about syringomyelia in more detail because the examiners will probe this. A syrinx is a central cavitation of the spinal cord filled with CSF. Syringes can be communicating, meaning they connect with the fourth ventricle, or non-communicating, meaning they are isolated within the cord. The classic clinical presentation of syringomyelia is a cape-like or suspended dissociated sensory loss. What does that mean? The patient loses pain and temperature sensation in a shawl-like distribution across the shoulders, arms, and upper trunk because the crossing spinothalamic fibers in the anterior commissure of the cord are disrupted by the expanding cavity. Light touch and proprioception are preserved because the dorsal columns are spared until late in the disease. Patients also develop hand weakness and intrinsic hand muscle atrophy from anterior horn cell destruction. In children, progressive scoliosis may be the presenting sign of a syrinx, and any child with a progressive or atypical scoliosis needs an MRI of the entire spine.

Let's move to the clinical case. A 32-year-old woman presents with an 18-month history of occipital headaches. These headaches are specifically worsened by coughing, Valsalva maneuvers, and straining. She describes the pain as a pressure or aching sensation at the back of her head and upper neck. On examination, she has decreased pinprick sensation bilaterally in the C5 through T2 dermatomes, consistent with a suspended dissociated sensory loss. She has bilateral hand weakness graded at 4 plus out of 5, and her lower extremities demonstrate hyperreflexia. MRI of the brain and spine reveals 12 millimeters of tonsillar ectopia below the foramen magnum with a holocord syrinx extending from C2 to T8. This is a classic presentation. She has a symptomatic Chiari I malformation with a large associated syrinx causing myelopathy. The Valsalva-provoked headaches, the cape-like sensory loss, the hand weakness, and the long segment syrinx all fit together perfectly. This patient needs surgery.

Now let's enter examiner interrogation mode. I am going to go through the exact lines of questioning you will face.

The examiner asks you to describe the clinical features of Chiari I. The most common presenting symptom is suboccipital headache exacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers, coughing, sneezing, or straining. This cough headache pattern is highly characteristic and should immediately raise suspicion for Chiari I in any young adult. Other symptoms include dysphagia from lower cranial nerve compression, downbeat nystagmus which is a classic neuro-ophthalmologic finding in Chiari malformation, lower cranial nerve palsies affecting the ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth nerves, and central cord syndrome from the associated syrinx. In pediatric patients, scoliosis is an important presentation, as I mentioned. Some patients also have sleep apnea from brainstem compression, and that is worth screening for.

The examiner asks about workup. You need MRI of the brain and the entire spine with gadolinium. The brain MRI characterizes the degree of tonsillar ectopia, evaluates for hydrocephalus, and assesses the posterior fossa size. The spine MRI evaluates the extent of any syrinx and rules out other causes such as a spinal cord tumor or tethered cord. Gadolinium is important to exclude neoplastic etiologies. Cine-flow CSF studies, also called phase-contrast or dynamic MRI, can demonstrate CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum in real time. These are not strictly required for diagnosis, but they provide supportive evidence and can be particularly helpful in borderline cases or Chiari 0. If the patient has symptoms suggestive of sleep apnea, order a polysomnography. Brainstem compression from Chiari malformation can cause central sleep apnea, and this can be an additional indication for surgical intervention.

The examiner asks about surgical indications. You operate on symptomatic Chiari I with progressive symptoms. You operate when there is syringomyelia, especially if the syrinx is enlarging on serial imaging. You operate when there is myelopathy, as in our case patient. The controversial scenario is the patient with Valsalva headaches alone, no syrinx, no myelopathy, no other neurological findings. Some surgeons operate on these patients if the headaches are severe and refractory to conservative management. Others observe. For boards, acknowledge the controversy and state that you would offer surgery if conservative measures fail and imaging confirms significant CSF flow obstruction. The one thing that is not controversial: asymptomatic Chiari I without syrinx does not require surgery. I will come back to that.

The examiner asks you to describe your surgical technique. The operation is posterior fossa decompression. Position the patient prone with the head in a Mayfield pin fixation, neck slightly flexed. Perform a midline suboccipital incision. Carry out a suboccipital craniectomy approximately 3 centimeters wide by 3 centimeters tall, centered on the foramen magnum. Remove the posterior arch of C1 as a laminectomy. In some cases, particularly with low-lying tonsils or a Chiari 1.5 pattern, a C2 laminectomy may be needed for adequate decompression. Now comes the key decision point: do you open the dura?

There are two approaches. Extradural decompression alone means you perform the bony decompression, confirm adequate removal of the posterior arch, and close without opening the dura. This is a simpler operation with fewer complications but may be less effective at resolving a syrinx. The alternative is posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty. You open the dura in a Y-shaped fashion, inspect the arachnoid, perform arachnoid dissection to open the foramen of Magendie and restore fourth ventricle CSF outflow, and then expand the dural space with a graft. The duraplasty options include autologous pericranium, which is the preferred graft material, harvested from the suboccipital region at the time of surgery. Other options include bovine pericardium and synthetic dural substitutes. The data on this question is important. Duraplasty shows better outcomes for syrinx resolution, on the order of 80 percent improvement compared to approximately 50 percent with extradural decompression alone. However, duraplasty carries a higher complication rate, particularly CSF leak, pseudomeningocele, and aseptic meningitis. The Heiss meta-analysis demonstrated this difference and is a key reference for boards. For the case we presented, a 32-year-old with a holocord syrinx and myelopathy, you should perform posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty. The syrinx demands the more aggressive approach.

The examiner asks about tonsillar reduction. Some surgeons advocate bipolar cautery shrinkage of the cerebellar tonsils or even subpial resection of the tonsils if they remain obstructive after bony decompression. This is not universally performed. The rationale is that even after adequate craniectomy and C1 laminectomy, bulky tonsils may continue to obstruct CSF flow. Tonsillar cautery or resection can improve the CSF pathway. For boards, state that you would consider tonsillar reduction if the tonsils remain significantly obstructive after bony decompression and dural opening, but that it is not a mandatory step in every case. What is more universally agreed upon is arachnoid dissection to open the foramen of Magendie and restore fourth ventricle outflow. This step is performed during the intradural portion of the operation.

The examiner asks about syrinx management. Posterior fossa decompression alone resolves the syrinx in 60 to 80 percent of cases. This is the critical teaching point: the syrinx is secondary to the CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum, so treating the cause, performing the PFD, addresses the syrinx in the majority of patients. If the syrinx persists or progresses after an adequate posterior fossa decompression, then you consider direct syrinx drainage procedures such as a syringosubarachnoid shunt or a syringopleural shunt. These are second-line interventions.

Now the trap questions. These are designed to test your judgment, and they are exactly what the examiners throw at you.

First trap. An asymptomatic 25-year-old has 8 millimeters of tonsillar ectopia found incidentally on MRI obtained for an unrelated reason. There is no syrinx. Normal neurological examination. What do you do? The answer is observe. You do not operate on asymptomatic Chiari I without syringomyelia. Many people walk around with tonsillar ectopia and never develop symptoms. Your plan is serial clinical follow-up and repeat MRI at 6 to 12 months, then annually if stable. You counsel the patient on symptoms to watch for, particularly Valsalva-provoked headaches, numbness, weakness, or difficulty swallowing, and you tell them to return if any new symptoms develop. Operating on this patient would be wrong.

Second trap. A child with known Chiari II and myelomeningocele develops acute stridor and apneic episodes. What is this? This is symptomatic brainstem compression from the Chiari II malformation, and it can be life-threatening. The stridor reflects vocal cord paralysis from compression of the vagus nerve nucleus in the herniated brainstem. The apnea reflects dysfunction of the medullary respiratory centers. This is an emergency. However, before you rush to the operating room for a posterior fossa decompression, you must evaluate for hydrocephalus. Shunt malfunction in a Chiari II patient can acutely worsen hindbrain herniation by increasing the pressure gradient driving posterior fossa contents downward. If the shunt is malfunctioning, revise the shunt first. If the shunt is functioning and the child is still symptomatic, proceed with urgent posterior fossa decompression.

Third trap. Would you shunt the syrinx directly without doing a posterior fossa decompression? No. This is a mistake. The syrinx is secondary to CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum. You must treat the underlying cause first. Direct shunting of the syrinx without addressing the craniocervical junction pathology has high failure rates and high recurrence rates because the driving mechanism is still present. Always perform posterior fossa decompression first and give it time to work. Only if the syrinx persists or progresses after adequate decompression do you proceed to direct shunt placement as a secondary procedure.

The examiner asks about complications. CSF leak is the most common complication after posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty, occurring in 5 to 15 percent of cases. A meticulous watertight dural closure with the graft reduces this risk. Pseudomeningocele, a subcutaneous collection of CSF at the operative site, can occur and may require aspiration or revision. Aseptic meningitis is a recognized complication, particularly when non-autologous dural substitutes are used. The inflammatory response to the graft material causes fever, headache, and meningismus with sterile CSF. This is treated with steroids and observation. Other complications include cerebellar slump or ptosis, where the cerebellum sags inferiorly after decompression, wound infection, neurological worsening, and recurrence of symptoms requiring reoperation.

Let's turn to the evidence. Milhorat published the largest series on Chiari I malformation, providing foundational data on natural history, clinical presentation, and surgical outcomes. His work established many of the clinical correlations we rely on today. The Heiss meta-analysis compared duraplasty versus extradural decompression alone and demonstrated superior syrinx resolution rates with duraplasty, approximately 80 percent versus 50 percent, which has influenced surgical decision-making. Park and Tubbs have contributed prospective data on posterior fossa decompression outcomes and surgical technique. The AANS and CNS published joint guidelines, most recently updated in 2024, that provide evidence-based recommendations on indications for surgery, the role of duraplasty, and management of associated syringomyelia. The key data point from the aggregated literature: syrinx resolution rates after posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty range from 60 to 80 percent, and symptomatic improvement in headache and neurological deficits is achieved in 70 to 90 percent of appropriately selected patients.

Let's close with the board pearls. These are the non-negotiable facts you must deliver clearly and without hesitation.

First, the 5 millimeter threshold. Chiari I is defined as tonsillar ectopia greater than 5 millimeters below the foramen magnum. Three to five millimeters is borderline and requires clinical correlation.

Second, the syrinx is secondary. Syringomyelia in Chiari I results from CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum. It is not a primary spinal cord pathology. Treating the obstruction treats the syrinx.

Third, duraplasty improves syrinx outcomes. Posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty achieves syrinx resolution in approximately 80 percent of cases compared to 50 percent with extradural decompression alone.

Fourth, never shunt the syrinx without performing posterior fossa decompression first. Always address the underlying pathophysiology at the craniocervical junction. Direct shunting alone has unacceptable failure and recurrence rates.

Fifth, asymptomatic Chiari I without syrinx is observed, not operated on. Do not let the examiner lead you into offering surgery for an incidental finding.

Sixth, in Chiari II with acute brainstem symptoms, always check the shunt before proceeding to posterior fossa decompression. Shunt malfunction can cause or exacerbate brainstem herniation.

That covers Chiari malformation management. You're ready for this one.
