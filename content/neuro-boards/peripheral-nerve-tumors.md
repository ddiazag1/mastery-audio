Let's master peripheral nerve tumors for the oral boards.

This topic rewards structure. There are only a handful of entities that matter, the imaging discriminators are clean, and the operative principles are few but absolute. The examiner's real target is whether you can distinguish a benign lesion you can shell out from a malignant lesion that requires oncologic resection, and whether you understand the syndromic associations that change the entire management picture. Let's build it.

Start with the classification. Peripheral nerve sheath tumors divide into benign and malignant. The benign group is dominated by schwannoma and neurofibroma, with perineurioma and a few rarer entities. The malignant group is the malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor. Separately, there are non-neoplastic and non-nerve-sheath lesions that present as nerve masses, including intraneural ganglion cysts, lipomas and lipofibromatous hamartoma, traumatic neuroma, and metastatic or direct invasion by carcinoma and lymphoma. Having that three part structure lets you answer any mass question.

Now schwannoma, the most common benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor. It arises from Schwann cells and, critically, it grows eccentrically, displacing the parent nerve fascicles to one side rather than incorporating them. It is encapsulated by a true capsule derived from epineurium. Histologically it shows the classic biphasic pattern of Antoni A areas, which are compact and cellular with nuclear palisading forming Verocay bodies, and Antoni B areas, which are loose, myxoid, and hypocellular. It stains diffusely and strongly positive for S one hundred protein, which is the immunohistochemical answer to have ready, and more recently SOX ten.

The clinical consequence of eccentric growth is the operative one: because the fascicles are displaced rather than traversing the tumor, a schwannoma can usually be enucleated from the nerve with preservation of function. You perform an epineurotomy over the surface, find the plane, identify the one or two fascicles that may be adherent, and dissect the tumor free. Preserve function; do not sacrifice the nerve for a benign tumor. Intraoperative nerve stimulation to identify and avoid functional fascicles is a good detail to mention.

Now neurofibroma. It arises from a mixture of Schwann cells, fibroblasts, and perineurial cells, and the crucial difference is that it grows within the nerve, with functioning fascicles running through the tumor substance. It is not truly encapsulated. Histologically it shows spindle cells in a myxoid and collagenous stroma, with the shredded carrot appearance of collagen bundles, and it stains only patchily for S one hundred, reflecting its mixed cell population.

The operative consequence follows directly: because fascicles pass through a neurofibroma, resection generally requires sacrificing the involved fascicles or the nerve segment, with grafting if a critical nerve is involved. Therefore a solitary asymptomatic neurofibroma on a functionally important nerve is often best observed. This schwannoma versus neurofibroma distinction, eccentric and enucleable versus intraneural and not, is the highest yield point in the topic and you should state it explicitly.

Recognize the plexiform neurofibroma as a distinct and more serious entity. It involves multiple fascicles and nerve branches over a long segment, producing the classic bag of worms on palpation. It is essentially pathognomonic of neurofibromatosis type one, it is difficult or impossible to resect completely, and most importantly it carries a significant lifetime risk of malignant transformation. That last point drives surveillance.

Now the syndromes, which change everything.

Neurofibromatosis type one, from a mutation in the NF1 gene on chromosome seventeen encoding neurofibromin, a tumor suppressor that negatively regulates RAS. It is autosomal dominant with a high spontaneous mutation rate. The diagnostic criteria include six or more café au lait macules, two or more neurofibromas or one plexiform neurofibroma, axillary or inguinal freckling, optic pathway glioma, two or more Lisch nodules which are iris hamartomas, a distinctive bony lesion such as sphenoid wing dysplasia or long bone cortical thinning with pseudarthrosis, and a first degree relative with the disease. The neurosurgical associations are optic pathway gliomas, other low grade gliomas, plexiform neurofibromas, scoliosis and dural ectasia, moyamoya especially after radiation, and the lifetime risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, commonly cited around eight to thirteen percent. A key management principle is to avoid radiation in these patients when possible, because of the risk of inducing second malignancies and moyamoya.

Neurofibromatosis type two, from a mutation in the NF2 gene on chromosome twenty two encoding merlin or schwannomin. The hallmark is bilateral vestibular schwannomas. Patients also develop multiple meningiomas, spinal schwannomas, ependymomas, and juvenile posterior subcapsular lenticular opacities. The mnemonic MISME captures multiple inherited schwannomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas. Remember that the schwannomas here are schwannomas, not neurofibromas, despite the confusing name of the syndrome.

Schwannomatosis is a third distinct entity, with multiple schwannomas but without vestibular schwannomas, often associated with SMARCB1 or LZTR1 mutations, and characteristically presenting with pain out of proportion to the lesion burden.

Now malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, which is the entity the examiner most wants you to catch. Roughly half arise in the setting of neurofibromatosis type one, typically from malignant transformation of a preexisting plexiform neurofibroma, and the remainder arise sporadically or after radiation.

Learn the red flags, because recognizing them is the scored behavior. Rapid growth of a previously stable lesion. New or rapidly worsening pain, particularly pain that becomes constant and nocturnal rather than positional. New or progressive neurologic deficit. Size greater than roughly five centimeters. A lesion that is heterogeneous on imaging with ill defined margins, invasion of surrounding tissue, central necrosis, and peritumoral edema. Loss of the target sign. Markedly increased metabolic activity on fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, with a standardized uptake value threshold often cited around three to four, and positron emission tomography is genuinely useful here for selecting the biopsy target within a large heterogeneous plexiform lesion.

Now the imaging discriminators generally. On magnetic resonance, benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors are typically fusiform, oriented along the course of the nerve, with an entering and exiting nerve visible at the poles, which is called the tail sign or the string sign. The split fat sign describes a rim of preserved fat around the lesion, indicating an intermuscular location and a slow growing process. The target sign describes central low T2 signal from dense fibrous tissue with peripheral high signal from myxoid tissue, and it favors a benign lesion, classically a neurofibroma. The fascicular sign describes multiple small ring like structures within the lesion. Benign lesions enhance, often heterogeneously, so enhancement alone does not indicate malignancy. Malignant lesions lose these organized features.

Now intraneural ganglion cyst, which deserves its own mention because it is a favorite examination trap. The classic location is the common peroneal nerve at the fibular head, and the mechanism, established by the articular theory, is that synovial fluid tracks from the superior tibiofibular joint along the articular branch of the nerve and dissects proximally within the epineurium. The clinical presentation is a peroneal palsy with foot drop. The critical management point is that simply decompressing or aspirating the cyst leads to a high recurrence rate, and that durable treatment requires disconnection of the articular branch and addressing the superior tibiofibular joint. Saying that sentence demonstrates current knowledge and is exactly what the examiner is fishing for.

Also recognize lipofibromatous hamartoma, a fibrofatty infiltration of the nerve, most often the median nerve at the wrist, sometimes associated with macrodactyly, appearing as a coaxial cable or spaghetti like appearance of nerve fascicles surrounded by fat. It is not resectable without destroying the nerve, so management is decompression and symptom control, not excision.

Now the management algorithm. For an asymptomatic, small, imaging typical benign lesion, observation with interval imaging is reasonable. For a symptomatic benign lesion causing pain or deficit, or one that is growing, resect it, using the enucleation principle for schwannoma. For a suspected malignant lesion, the approach changes completely: do not attempt enucleation. Obtain tissue with an image guided core needle or a carefully planned incisional biopsy whose tract can be excised with the definitive specimen, involve orthopedic oncology and surgical oncology, stage the patient with chest imaging because the lungs are the principal site of metastasis, and plan a wide en bloc resection with negative margins, which frequently means sacrificing the nerve and sometimes amputation. Adjuvant radiation is standard for high grade lesions and for close margins. Chemotherapy has limited efficacy. Prognosis is poor, with five year survival commonly cited in the range of roughly thirty five to fifty percent, and worse in the neurofibromatosis type one population.

State the cardinal operative principle plainly, because it is the safety point of the topic: never shell out a lesion you have not characterized. An unplanned intralesional excision of a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor contaminates the field, compromises subsequent limb salvage, and worsens outcome. When the clinical picture contains red flags, you biopsy first.

Two further practical points. Intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring, including nerve action potential recording and direct stimulation, should be used for any intraneural dissection, and you should say so. And counsel patients preoperatively that even a technically perfect enucleation of a benign schwannoma carries a real risk of a new sensory or motor deficit, commonly quoted in the range of several percent to more depending on the nerve, which is why asymptomatic lesions on critical nerves are often watched.

Expand the benign differential beyond the two main entities, because the examiner may hand you something that is neither. Perineurioma arises from perineurial cells, typically presents in an adolescent or young adult as a painless, slowly progressive motor mononeuropathy, and shows fusiform enlargement of a nerve segment with a characteristic pseudo onion bulb architecture; it is not resectable without sacrificing the nerve, so management is biopsy for diagnosis and then tendon transfer or nerve transfer for function rather than attempted excision. Granular cell tumour, haemangioma, and desmoid type fibromatosis occur around nerves. Traumatic neuroma follows injury or amputation and is a disorganised mass of regenerating axons in scar, not a neoplasm. Morton neuroma is likewise not a true neuroma but perineural fibrosis of an interdigital nerve. And nerves can be involved by direct extension or perineural spread of carcinoma, classically adenoid cystic carcinoma and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma tracking along the trigeminal or facial nerves, and by lymphoma as neurolymphomatosis.

Know the genetics well enough to counsel. Neurofibromatosis type one arises from mutations in the NF1 gene on chromosome seventeen encoding neurofibromin, a GTPase activating protein that switches off RAS; loss of function therefore drives RAS and downstream MEK signalling, which is precisely why MEK inhibitors such as selumetinib now have a role in symptomatic inoperable plexiform neurofibroma in children. That is a genuinely current answer and worth having. Neurofibromatosis type two arises from mutations in NF2 on chromosome twenty two encoding merlin. Schwannomatosis arises from SMARCB1 or LZTR1 mutations, both also on chromosome twenty two. All three are autosomal dominant, and neurofibromatosis type one in particular has a very high spontaneous mutation rate, so absence of family history does not exclude it. Offer genetic counselling and family screening.

Add the malignant pathway in more detail. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours are aggressive soft tissue sarcomas. Roughly half arise in neurofibromatosis type one, usually by transformation of a preexisting plexiform neurofibroma, and roughly ten percent are radiation associated, typically after a latency of many years. Molecularly, loss of the polycomb repressive complex two components EED or SUZ twelve produces loss of trimethylation of histone three lysine twenty seven, and immunohistochemical loss of that mark is a useful diagnostic adjunct that supports malignancy; S one hundred staining is typically patchy or lost, in contrast to the strong diffuse staining of schwannoma. Staging is by chest imaging because the lungs are the dominant metastatic site, with whole body magnetic resonance or positron emission tomography where the disease burden is high.

State the oncological principles explicitly, because this is the safety point of the topic. Do not enucleate a lesion you have not characterised. If red flags are present, obtain tissue by image guided core needle biopsy or a carefully planned longitudinal incisional biopsy whose tract lies within the future resection field, so it can be excised en bloc. Involve sarcoma multidisciplinary services early. Definitive treatment is wide local excision with negative margins, which frequently means sacrificing the parent nerve and occasionally amputation for an unreconstructable limb lesion. Adjuvant radiotherapy is standard for high grade tumours, large tumours and close margins; chemotherapy, usually doxorubicin and ifosfamide based, has limited efficacy and is generally reserved for metastatic or unresectable disease. Five year survival is commonly cited in the range of roughly thirty five to fifty percent and is worse in the neurofibromatosis type one population and with large, proximal, high grade lesions.

Give surveillance a concrete shape. In neurofibromatosis type one, examine annually with attention to new or growing masses, new pain, and new deficit, and image any lesion that changes. Whole body magnetic resonance can establish a baseline plexiform burden in selected patients. Educate the patient and family about the specific red flags, because they will detect transformation long before the next clinic visit: a lump that starts growing after years of stability, pain that becomes constant and wakes them at night, and new weakness or numbness.

Finally, be concrete about operative technique for the benign case, since that is what you will actually do most often. Position for a generous longitudinal exposure that allows control of the nerve proximal and distal to the lesion. Use loupes or the microscope and intraoperative stimulation throughout. Open the epineurium longitudinally over the surface away from the entering and exiting fascicles, develop the plane circumferentially, and identify the one or two fascicles that enter and leave the capsule; stimulate them before dividing anything, and if a fascicle is functional, consider leaving a small remnant rather than creating a deficit. Deliver the tumour, achieve haemostasis without cautery against the fascicles, and close the epineurium loosely or not at all. Counsel every patient preoperatively that even an ideal enucleation carries a real risk of new numbness or weakness, which is exactly why an asymptomatic lesion on a critical nerve is often best watched.

One practical point on consent, because these are elective operations on people who often feel well. Document explicitly that the purpose is diagnosis and symptom control, that a new sensory deficit in the distribution of the parent nerve is a recognised and not rare outcome even with ideal technique, that motor deficit is possible where motor fascicles are involved, and that a benign lesion may recur. For a patient whose only symptom is a lump, that conversation frequently ends in a decision to observe with interval imaging, which is a perfectly good outcome and demonstrates judgement rather than reluctance.

To summarize. Schwannoma is eccentric, encapsulated, Antoni A and B with Verocay bodies, strongly S one hundred positive, and enucleable. Neurofibroma is intraneural with fascicles running through it, patchy S one hundred, and usually not separable without sacrifice. Plexiform neurofibroma means neurofibromatosis type one and carries malignant potential. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor announces itself with rapid growth, new constant pain, new deficit, large size, heterogeneity with necrosis, and high metabolic uptake, and it demands biopsy and oncologic wide resection rather than enucleation. Remember the intraneural ganglion at the fibular head and the need to disconnect the articular branch. And use the imaging signs, tail, split fat, target, and fascicular, to argue for benignity before you commit to an operation.
