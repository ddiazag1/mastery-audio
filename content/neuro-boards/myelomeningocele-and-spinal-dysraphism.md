Let's master myelomeningocele and spinal dysraphism for the oral boards.

This is the foundational pediatric neurosurgery topic. The examiner can build an entire case from it: prenatal counseling, delivery planning, closure, hydrocephalus, Chiari, tethering, and lifelong multidisciplinary care. It rewards a candidate who understands the embryology, because every lesion in the dysraphism spectrum is a failure at an identifiable stage. Let's build it that way.

Start with the embryology. The neural tube forms by two mechanisms. Primary neurulation, occurring at roughly the third to fourth week of gestation, folds the neural plate into a tube that closes progressively, forming everything from the brain down to approximately the second sacral level. The anterior neuropore closes at about day twenty five and the posterior neuropore at about day twenty seven. Failure of primary neurulation produces the open dysraphisms: anencephaly at the cranial end and myelomeningocele at the caudal end. Secondary neurulation, occurring from roughly the fourth to seventh week, forms the distal cord below the second sacral level by canalization and retrogressive differentiation of the caudal cell mass, and failure here produces the closed lesions affecting the conus and filum, such as lipomyelomeningocele, thickened filum, and terminal syrinx.

Now the classification. Dysraphism divides into open, or spina bifida aperta, where neural tissue is exposed with no skin covering, and closed, or spina bifida occulta, where the defect is covered by skin. That distinction determines urgency: open lesions require closure within days to prevent infection, whereas closed lesions are managed electively.

Myelomeningocele is the prototypical open lesion, in which the neural placode and meninges herniate through the bony defect with cerebrospinal fluid leakage and no skin coverage. Meningocele, by contrast, herniates meninges without neural tissue, and neurologic function is typically normal. Myelocele describes a flat placode flush with the skin surface without a cerebrospinal fluid sac.

Understand the epidemiology and prevention, because this is asked. Incidence has fallen substantially with folic acid fortification and supplementation. The recommendation is four hundred micrograms of folic acid daily for all women of childbearing age, increased to four milligrams daily for women with a previous affected pregnancy, beginning at least one month before conception and continuing through the first trimester, because the neural tube closes before many women know they are pregnant. Risk factors include maternal diabetes, maternal obesity, maternal hyperthermia, and anticonvulsants, particularly valproic acid and carbamazepine. Recurrence risk after one affected child is roughly two to five percent, rising with additional affected pregnancies.

Prenatal diagnosis relies on elevated maternal serum alpha fetoprotein and on ultrasound, which shows the spinal defect directly and shows cranial signs: the lemon sign, describing scalloping of the frontal bones, and the banana sign, describing the curved cerebellum wrapped around the brainstem from the Chiari two malformation with an effaced cisterna magna. Fetal magnetic resonance refines the assessment.

Now prenatal versus postnatal repair, which you must be able to discuss, because it is the major development in the field. The Management of Myelomeningocele Study, or MOMS trial, randomized prenatal repair before twenty six weeks against standard postnatal repair. Prenatal repair reduced the need for shunting at twelve months, roughly forty percent versus roughly eighty two percent, improved motor function and mental development at thirty months, and reduced hindbrain herniation. The costs were significant: increased prematurity and preterm delivery, increased risk of uterine dehiscence at the hysterotomy site, and maternal morbidity, with mandatory cesarean delivery for that and all subsequent pregnancies. Eligibility criteria included gestational age, upper lesion level between the first thoracic and first sacral levels, evidence of hindbrain herniation, normal karyotype, and maternal health criteria. The answer to give is that prenatal repair is offered at experienced fetal centers to carefully selected candidates after thorough counseling about the maternal risks, and that it improves shunt rates and motor outcomes but does not cure the condition.

Now postnatal management. At delivery, which is usually by cesarean section to avoid trauma to the placode though the evidence is not definitive, the immediate priorities are to protect the placode with moist saline soaked nonadherent dressing, position the infant prone or laterally to avoid pressure on the sac, use latex free precautions from the very first contact because these children develop latex allergy at high rates from repeated exposure, start broad spectrum antibiotics, maintain thermoregulation, and perform a careful neurologic examination documenting the functional level, which is more informative than the anatomic level. Examine for associated anomalies, obtain a head ultrasound to establish baseline ventricular size, and obtain renal and bladder ultrasound.

Closure is performed within roughly twenty four to seventy two hours to reduce the risk of ventriculitis and meningitis. The operative principles are these. Dissect the placode free circumferentially at the junction between neural tissue and the surrounding cutaneous ectoderm, excising the dysplastic epithelial tissue to prevent later inclusion dermoid formation. Reconstitute the placode into a tube, or neurulate it, with fine suture to reduce the surface area available for scarring and retethering. Then close in distinct watertight layers: dura, then fascia, then skin. A watertight dural closure is the key technical point, because cerebrospinal fluid leak is the principal early complication and it predisposes to infection and wound breakdown. Large defects may require relaxing incisions or rotational myocutaneous flaps with plastic surgery involvement.

Now hydrocephalus, which is the other half of the problem. Roughly eighty percent of children with myelomeningocele develop hydrocephalus requiring treatment, and the rate is lower after prenatal repair. It frequently manifests or worsens after closure of the defect, because the closure eliminates the cerebrospinal fluid egress that had been decompressing the system. Monitor head circumference, fontanelle, sutures, and ventricular size, and treat with shunting when indicated, typically in the first weeks of life. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy with choroid plexus cauterization is an alternative with variable success in infants and is more successful in older children. Be prepared to discuss shunt complications across a lifetime, since these patients accumulate revisions.

Now the Chiari two malformation, which is essentially universal in myelomeningocele. It involves downward displacement of the cerebellar vermis, the fourth ventricle, and the brainstem through the foramen magnum, with a small posterior fossa, tectal beaking, medullary kinking, and often associated anomalies including corpus callosum dysgenesis, polymicrogyria, and syringomyelia. Symptomatic Chiari two is the leading cause of death in infants with myelomeningocele. The clinical presentation differs by age. In infants, it manifests as brainstem dysfunction: stridor from vocal cord paralysis, apnea, feeding difficulty and aspiration, and opisthotonos. This is a neurosurgical emergency. In older children, it manifests with upper extremity weakness, ataxia, and headache.

The management sequence is the critical point. When a child with myelomeningocele develops symptoms of Chiari two, the first step is always to evaluate and treat shunt malfunction, because a failing shunt is the most common cause of Chiari symptom onset and shunt revision alone frequently resolves the symptoms. Only after the shunt is confirmed to be working do you consider posterior fossa decompression. Reaching for the decompression first is the classic error, and stating the sequence correctly earns real credit.

Now tethered cord, which affects this population for life. Every child with a repaired myelomeningocele has a radiographically low lying, tethered conus; that is the expected postoperative anatomy and it is not by itself an indication for surgery. The diagnosis of tethered cord syndrome is clinical, made on the basis of progressive deterioration: new or worsening weakness, increasing spasticity, new or progressive scoliosis, changing urologic function, new pain, and orthopedic deformity such as progressive foot deformity. Treat the syndrome, not the image. Surgical untethering is indicated for documented progressive deterioration, and retethering can occur repeatedly over a lifetime.

Now the closed dysraphisms, which you should be able to enumerate. Look for the cutaneous stigmata over the lumbosacral midline, which are the clinical clue: a hairy patch, a dimple particularly one above the gluteal crease or off midline, a subcutaneous lipoma, a hemangioma, a dermal sinus tract, or a skin appendage or tail. Any of these warrants imaging, with ultrasound useful in infants under roughly three to six months before ossification, and magnetic resonance thereafter.

Lipomyelomeningocele is a subcutaneous lipoma continuous with a fatty mass attached to the cord, producing tethering. It is subclassified as dorsal, transitional, or terminal. Management is prophylactic or therapeutic untethering with debulking, though the decision to operate prophylactically on an asymptomatic child is genuinely contested, with some centers favoring early surgery and others favoring observation with close surveillance, and you should acknowledge that controversy rather than assert a dogma.

Dermal sinus tract is a midline epithelial lined tract that may extend to the dura or the cord, and it is important because it is a conduit for infection, causing recurrent meningitis and intraspinal abscess, and because it is associated with inclusion dermoid or epidermoid tumors. It should be imaged and surgically excised in its entirety. Never probe or inject a dermal sinus tract.

Diastematomyelia, or split cord malformation, divides the cord into two hemicords. In type one, the hemicords lie in separate dural tubes separated by a bony or osteocartilaginous septum, and this type is more commonly symptomatic and requires removal of the spur. In type two, the hemicords share a single dural tube separated by a fibrous band. It is strongly associated with scoliosis and with cutaneous stigmata, particularly a hairy patch, and it must be identified before any scoliosis correction, because distraction of a tethered split cord can cause catastrophic injury.

Finally, the lifelong multidisciplinary care, which the examiner expects you to articulate because it demonstrates understanding of the real disease. Urology is central: neurogenic bladder requires urodynamic surveillance and clean intermittent catheterization to preserve renal function, and renal failure is a leading cause of late mortality. Orthopedics manages hip dysplasia, foot deformity, and scoliosis. Physical and occupational therapy maximize mobility, with ambulation potential broadly predicted by the functional level, such that lesions below the third lumbar level generally permit community ambulation. Bowel management, skin care to prevent pressure ulcers in insensate areas, latex avoidance throughout life, and attention to the high rates of learning disability despite frequently normal overall intelligence are all part of the answer. And transition to adult care is a recognized gap that you can mention to good effect.

Be able to predict function from the level, because families always ask and examiners always probe. A lesion at the second lumbar level or above generally means no useful ambulation and wheelchair dependence. Third lumbar level preserves quadriceps and knee extension, permitting household ambulation with bracing and crutches. Fourth lumbar level adds medial hamstrings and tibialis anterior and generally permits community ambulation with ankle foot orthoses. Fifth lumbar level preserves most ankle dorsiflexion with weak plantarflexion and usually allows community ambulation. Sacral level lesions walk with minimal or no aids but commonly have bowel, bladder and foot deformity issues. State that the functional level determined by examination is more informative than the anatomical level on imaging, because the two frequently differ.

Give the urological management real substance, because renal failure, not the neural tube defect, is the historical cause of late death. Every infant needs a baseline renal and bladder ultrasound and urodynamic study. The dangerous findings are detrusor sphincter dyssynergia, poor bladder compliance, and a detrusor leak point pressure above roughly forty centimetres of water, because sustained high storage pressure transmits to the upper tracts and causes hydronephrosis, reflux and renal scarring. Management is clean intermittent catheterisation, usually begun in infancy, with anticholinergics or beta three agonists to lower storage pressure, and intradetrusor botulinum toxin or bladder augmentation for refractory cases. Surveillance with serial ultrasound and urodynamics continues lifelong. Bowel management with a structured regimen, and in selected children an antegrade continence enema channel, is equally part of the answer.

Add the orthopaedic and mobility dimension. Hip subluxation and dislocation, knee and foot deformities, particularly equinovarus and vertical talus, and neuromuscular scoliosis are all common and are managed jointly with orthopaedics. Scoliosis in this population warrants a specific caution: new or rapidly progressive curvature should prompt evaluation for tethered cord, syringomyelia, shunt malfunction and symptomatic Chiari before it is attributed to the underlying paralysis, and correction of a curve in a patient with an untreated split cord or tethering risks catastrophic neurological injury from distraction.

Emphasise latex allergy as a systems issue, not a footnote. These children develop latex sensitisation at high rates from repeated exposure during surgery and catheterisation, with reactions ranging from urticaria to intraoperative anaphylaxis. Latex precautions must begin at the very first contact in the delivery room and be maintained for life, and stating that in the delivery room management sequence is a specific marker of expertise.

Round out the closed dysraphism section. Lipomyelomeningocele is subclassified as dorsal, transitional, terminal and the chaotic type, and the transitional and chaotic types carry the highest surgical risk and retethering rate. Terminal myelocystocele is a skin covered lesion in which a dilated terminal central canal herniates into a meningocele sac, often associated with cloacal exstrophy and the OEIS complex. Meningocele manqué describes fibrous bands tethering the cord dorsally, often with a split cord. Caudal regression syndrome ranges from partial sacral agenesis to sirenomelia, is strongly associated with maternal diabetes, and produces a characteristic blunted, club shaped or wedge shaped conus. Dermal sinus tracts must never be probed or injected and must be excised in their entirety to their termination, because they are a conduit for recurrent meningitis and intraspinal abscess and are associated with inclusion dermoids.

Be precise about the closure technique, since the examiner may ask you to describe it. Under general anaesthesia with latex precautions and the infant prone, incise at the junction between the epithelialised zone and normal skin. Dissect circumferentially at the interface between the neural placode and the surrounding cutaneous ectoderm, and excise all dysplastic epithelial tissue from the placode to prevent later inclusion dermoid. Reconstitute the placode into a tube with fine interrupted sutures along the pial edges, which reduces the raw surface available for scarring and retethering. Identify the dura, which is everted and attached to the fascia laterally, dissect it free and close it watertight in the midline. Close the fascia as a separate layer, then the skin. For large defects, obtain relaxing incisions or rotational myocutaneous flaps with plastic surgery. Watertight dural closure is the single most important technical point, because cerebrospinal fluid leak drives wound breakdown, infection and ventriculitis.

Finish with the long term outlook, honestly stated. Survival into adulthood is now the norm with modern care, and roughly three quarters or more reach adulthood. Most have normal or near normal intelligence, though learning difficulties, executive dysfunction and the non-verbal learning profile are common, and hydrocephalus and its complications are the principal determinants of cognitive outcome. Adults face shunt dependence and revisions, tethered cord, renal disease, pressure ulcers in insensate skin, obesity, limited mobility, sexual and fertility issues, and the well documented gap in transition from paediatric to adult services. Naming that transition gap and saying you would plan for it is a mature, high scoring answer.

To summarize. Open lesions come from failed primary neurulation and close within seventy two hours; closed lesions come from failed secondary neurulation and are managed electively. Folic acid prevents. MOMS showed prenatal repair reduces shunting and improves motor outcome at maternal cost. Close in watertight layers after neurulating the placode and excising epithelial remnants. Expect hydrocephalus in about eighty percent. For symptomatic Chiari two, always check the shunt first. Treat tethered cord syndrome clinically, never radiographically. Look for cutaneous stigmata to find the closed lesions. And frame the whole thing as lifelong multidisciplinary care with renal preservation at its center.
