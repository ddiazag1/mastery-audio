Welcome to this viva-style teaching session on cervical spine trauma management. This is a high-yield oral board topic, and the examiners expect precise classification knowledge, sound surgical decision-making, and awareness of the classic traps. Let's build this from the ground up.

We begin by dividing the cervical spine into two biomechanically distinct regions. The occipitocervical junction spans from the occiput through C2. The subaxial spine spans from C3 through C7. These two regions have entirely different bony anatomy, ligamentous stabilizers, injury patterns, and classification systems, so you must keep them conceptually separate in your mind.

Starting with the occipitocervical junction, the key ligamentous anatomy that you must know includes the tectorial membrane, which is the intracranial extension of the posterior longitudinal ligament and attaches to the clivus. The transverse ligament is the primary stabilizer of the atlantoaxial joint, holding the odontoid process against the anterior arch of C1. The alar ligaments connect the tip of the dens to the medial aspects of the occipital condyles and limit axial rotation. The apical ligament connects the tip of the dens to the anterior margin of the foramen magnum. Of all of these, the transverse ligament is the single most important structure. When it fails, C1-C2 instability results, and that changes your entire management algorithm.

Now let's move through the injury patterns of the upper cervical spine systematically.

Occipital condyle fractures are classified by the Anderson-Montesano system into three types. Type I is an impaction fracture of the condyle caused by axial loading. It is stable. Type II is a basilar skull fracture that extends into the occipital condyle. It is also stable. Type III is an avulsion fracture caused by the alar ligament pulling off a fragment of the condyle. A unilateral Type III fracture is potentially unstable. Bilateral Type III fractures are frankly unstable because both alar ligaments are disrupted. The key board point here is that Type III is the one that can be unstable, and bilateral Type III injuries require surgical stabilization with occipitocervical fusion.

Atlanto-occipital dislocation is the next entity. The Traynelis classification divides these into Type I, which is anterior dislocation of the occiput relative to C1; Type II, which is longitudinal distraction; and Type III, which is posterior dislocation. Several radiographic measurements help diagnose this injury. The Powers ratio is the ratio of the basion-to-posterior arch distance divided by the opisthion-to-anterior arch distance. A value greater than 1.0 suggests anterior atlanto-occipital dislocation. The basion-dental interval is the distance from the basion to the tip of the dens, and a value greater than 12 millimeters is abnormal and suggests distraction. The condyle-C1 interval, or CCI, is measured on CT, and a value greater than 2 millimeters is concerning for occipitocervical dissociation. Atlanto-occipital dislocation is almost always fatal or results in devastating neurological injury. Survivors require occipitocervical fusion. There is no conservative management option for this injury.

Moving to the atlas, C1 fractures include the Jefferson fracture, which is a burst fracture of the C1 ring resulting in bilateral lateral mass fractures. This is caused by axial loading, such as diving into shallow water or a direct blow to the vertex. The Rule of Spence is the critical concept here. On an open-mouth odontoid radiograph, if the combined overhang of the C1 lateral masses relative to the C2 lateral masses exceeds 6.9 millimeters, the transverse ligament is presumed to be ruptured, and the fracture is unstable. A stable Jefferson fracture with an intact transverse ligament can be managed with a rigid cervical collar. An unstable Jefferson fracture with transverse ligament rupture requires either halo immobilization or C1-C2 fusion. MRI can directly visualize the transverse ligament and should be obtained whenever a Jefferson fracture is identified.

Next are odontoid fractures, classified by the Anderson-D'Alonzo system. Type I is a tip avulsion fracture of the odontoid process. It is rare and generally stable unless it represents an alar ligament avulsion with associated craniocervical instability, which you should evaluate with flexion-extension imaging or MRI. Type II is a fracture at the base of the dens, at the junction between the odontoid process and the body of C2. This is the most common odontoid fracture type and the most problematic because the fracture line runs through an area of limited blood supply, resulting in high nonunion rates ranging from 15 percent to as high as 85 percent depending on patient age, displacement, and direction of displacement. Type III is a fracture that extends into the cancellous bone of the C2 body. Because it runs through well-vascularized cancellous bone, it has good healing potential and is generally managed conservatively with a collar or halo.

Management of Type II odontoid fractures is one of the most commonly tested decision points on oral boards. For patients younger than 50 years old with less than 5 millimeters of displacement, halo immobilization for 8 to 12 weeks is a reasonable option with acceptable union rates. For patients older than 50, those with displacement greater than 5 millimeters, posterior displacement of the dens, delayed presentation beyond several weeks, or smoking, surgical fixation is preferred. The surgical options include anterior odontoid screw fixation and posterior C1-C2 fusion. Anterior odontoid screw placement involves passing a single lag screw from the anteroinferior body of C2 up through the fracture line and into the tip of the dens. The major advantage is that it preserves C1-C2 rotational motion, which accounts for roughly 50 percent of total cervical rotation. However, anterior odontoid screw fixation requires an intact transverse ligament, a favorable fracture line that runs from anterosuperior to posteroinferior so the screw can compress the fracture, and adequate bone quality. It is contraindicated in patients with transverse ligament disruption, chronic fractures with resorbed bone edges, an unfavorable oblique fracture line running anteroinferior to posterosuperior, severe osteoporosis, or a barrel-shaped chest that prevents the proper caudal-to-cranial trajectory. Posterior C1-C2 fusion is the more commonly performed procedure. The Goel-Harms technique uses C1 lateral mass screws placed under direct visualization with C2 pars interarticularis or pedicle screws, connected by rods with a structural bone graft. The alternative is the Magerl transarticular screw technique, which places bilateral screws from the posterior aspect of C2 across the C1-C2 facet joint. Magerl screws require preoperative CT angiography to assess the course of the vertebral artery, because an anomalous medially positioned vertebral artery at C2 is a contraindication to screw placement on that side.

Hangman's fractures, also called traumatic spondylolisthesis of C2, involve bilateral fractures through the C2 pars interarticularis. The Levine-Edwards classification is the standard. Type I fractures have less than 3 millimeters of displacement and no angulation of C2 on C3. They are stable injuries and are treated with a rigid cervical collar for 8 to 12 weeks. Type II fractures have greater than 3 millimeters of displacement or significant angulation. These can be managed with halo immobilization or surgery depending on clinical context. Type IIA fractures are the critical trap. These have minimal anterior translation but significant angulation at C2-C3 due to a flexion-distraction mechanism. Because the mechanism is distraction, applying cervical traction will worsen the displacement and can cause spinal cord injury. If the examiner presents you a Type IIA hangman's fracture and asks about traction, the answer is absolutely not. These require either halo immobilization in extension or surgical fixation. Type III fractures combine the bilateral pars fracture with a C2-C3 facet dislocation. They are frankly unstable and require surgical reduction and fusion.

Now let's transition to the subaxial cervical spine. The Subaxial Injury Classification and Severity Scale, known as SLIC, is the decision-making framework you must know. SLIC scores three components. First, morphology: no abnormality scores 0, compression scores 1, burst adds an additional 1 point on top of compression for a total of 2, distraction scores 3, and translation or rotation scores 4. Second, the dorsal ligamentous complex: intact scores 0, indeterminate scores 1, and disrupted scores 2. Third, neurological status: intact scores 0, nerve root injury scores 1, complete spinal cord injury scores 2, incomplete spinal cord injury scores 3, and if there is ongoing cord compression with a neurological deficit, you add 1 additional point. The total score guides management. A score of 3 or less favors nonoperative treatment. A score of 4 is equivocal and requires clinical judgment. A score of 5 or greater favors surgical intervention. The dorsal ligamentous complex includes the supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, and facet joint capsules. MRI is the best modality for evaluating DLC integrity. Signs of DLC disruption on MRI include interspinous edema or widening, facet joint fluid or widening, and perched or jumped facets.

Let's move into a clinical case. A 70-year-old man on warfarin presents after a ground-level fall. He reports neck pain. His neurological examination is intact with no motor or sensory deficits. CT of the cervical spine demonstrates a Type II odontoid fracture with 6 millimeters of posterior displacement of the dens relative to the C2 body.

Immediate management begins with application of a rigid cervical collar if not already in place. You perform a thorough neurological examination documenting motor strength, sensory levels, reflexes, and rectal tone. You obtain CT imaging of the entire spine because noncontiguous spinal fractures occur in roughly 10 to 15 percent of patients. You order an MRI of the cervical spine to evaluate for transverse ligament integrity, spinal cord signal change, and disc pathology. You reverse his anticoagulation and check coagulation studies.

Now the examiner asks about your definitive management plan. This is a 70-year-old patient with a displaced Type II odontoid fracture. The displacement is 6 millimeters, which exceeds the 5-millimeter threshold. The patient is elderly. These two factors alone make this a surgical case. But there is a deeper reason, and this is what the examiner wants to hear. Halo immobilization in elderly patients carries an unacceptably high complication and mortality rate. The landmark data from Tashjian and colleagues demonstrated that halo vest immobilization in patients older than 65 with Type II odontoid fractures was associated with a mortality rate of up to 40 percent, primarily from pulmonary complications including pneumonia, aspiration, and respiratory failure. Nonunion rates in elderly patients with halo treatment range from 40 to 80 percent. Therefore, surgical fixation is the preferred approach.

The procedure of choice for this patient is a posterior C1-C2 fusion using the Goel-Harms technique. You place C1 lateral mass screws by dissecting the C1 posterior arch, identifying the C1-C2 joint, and placing screws into the C1 lateral mass under fluoroscopic guidance. For C2, you place pars interarticularis screws or pedicle screws depending on anatomy. Preoperative CT angiography is mandatory to evaluate the vertebral artery course at C1 and C2. An anomalous vertebral artery, particularly a high-riding vertebral artery at C2, may preclude safe screw placement on that side and requires either modification of screw trajectory or use of an alternative construct. A structural iliac crest autograft or allograft is placed between the C1 and C2 posterior elements to promote arthrodesis.

The examiner now asks why you would not use an anterior odontoid screw in this patient. Several reasons. First, the patient is 70 years old with likely osteoporotic bone, which compromises screw purchase. Second, the fracture has 6 millimeters of posterior displacement, which often produces a fracture line orientation unfavorable for anterior screw compression. Third, the chronicity of the injury may be uncertain given the patient was on anticoagulation and may have sustained this fracture days before presenting. Fourth, elderly patients frequently have barrel-shaped chests that limit the steep caudal-to-cranial trajectory needed for anterior screw placement.

The examiner pivots to unilateral and bilateral facet dislocations. Unilateral facet dislocation results from a flexion-rotation mechanism and produces approximately 25 percent anterolisthesis of the vertebral body above on the level below. On CT, you see the reverse hamburger sign where the facets appear reversed on the affected side. Bilateral facet dislocation results from a flexion-distraction mechanism and produces 50 percent or greater anterolisthesis. These are highly unstable injuries.

For closed reduction, the patient must be awake and cooperative for serial neurological examinations. You start with 10 pounds of inline cervical traction and add 5 pounds per level. For example, for a C5-C6 dislocation, you start at 10 pounds and can increase in 5-pound increments up to approximately 35 to 40 pounds, checking a lateral radiograph and performing a neurological exam after each weight increment. If the patient develops any new neurological deficit during traction, you immediately reduce the weight and obtain urgent imaging.

The controversy over pre-reduction MRI is a classic board topic. The concern is that a herniated disc anterior to the spinal cord could be compressed against the cord during reduction, causing neurological deterioration. Some authors advocate for MRI before any closed reduction attempt to rule out a disc herniation. Others argue that this delays reduction and that closed reduction performed in an awake, examinable patient is safe because any new deficit will be immediately detected. The practical answer for the boards is that if the patient is awake and examinable, you may proceed with closed reduction under careful neurological monitoring. If the patient is obtunded or cannot be examined, obtain an MRI first. If closed reduction fails, you proceed to open surgical reduction and fixation. When MRI demonstrates a significant disc herniation at the level of dislocation, the safest surgical sequence is anterior discectomy first to remove the compressive disc, followed by posterior open reduction and lateral mass fixation, and potentially an anterior interbody graft and plate to complete a circumferential construct.

The examiner now asks about clearing the cervical spine. There are two validated clinical decision rules. The NEXUS criteria require that a patient have no posterior midline tenderness, no focal neurological deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, and no distracting injury to be cleared clinically without imaging. The Canadian C-Spine Rule is a stepwise algorithm. First, any high-risk factor such as age over 65, dangerous mechanism, or paresthesias mandates imaging. Second, any low-risk factor such as simple rear-end collision, ambulatory at any time, or delayed onset of neck pain allows safe assessment of range of motion. Third, the patient must be able to actively rotate the neck 45 degrees to each side to be cleared clinically. For obtunded patients, the protocol is controversial. CT of the cervical spine has very high sensitivity and many institutions clear the spine based on a negative high-quality CT alone. Others require supplemental MRI, particularly to evaluate for ligamentous injury and cord pathology. The trend in the literature supports CT alone as adequate for clearance in obtunded patients when the scan is read as normal, but institutional protocols vary.

Now for trap questions. If the examiner asks whether you would put a 70-year-old in a halo, you answer no. Cite the Tashjian data showing mortality rates approaching 40 percent and nonunion rates up to 80 percent in elderly patients treated with halo immobilization for Type II odontoid fractures. Halo vests in the elderly cause pulmonary complications, aspiration, skin breakdown, pin-site infections, and falls. Surgical fixation has superior outcomes in this population.

If the examiner describes a Type IIA hangman's fracture and asks about traction, you must not apply traction. The mechanism is flexion-distraction, and the injury pattern involves primarily angulation with minimal translation. Applying traction to a distraction injury will separate the fracture fragments further and risks catastrophic spinal cord injury. The management is either halo immobilization applied in slight extension or surgical fixation, depending on clinical stability.

If the examiner tells you there is a disc herniation at C5-C6 with bilateral locked facets and asks your plan, you navigate this carefully. If the patient is awake and neurologically deteriorating, some surgeons will attempt closed reduction with traction because the reduction itself decompresses the canal. However, if the MRI shows significant ventral cord compression from the disc, the safest sequence is an anterior approach first for discectomy to remove the herniated disc, followed by a posterior approach for open reduction and lateral mass screw-rod fixation, and then potentially returning anteriorly for interbody grafting and plating if needed.

Turning to complications of cervical spine trauma surgery, vertebral artery injury is the most feared complication during C1-C2 screw placement. The vertebral artery courses through the foramen transversarium of C1 and C2 and is at risk during both lateral mass screw placement at C1 and pars or pedicle screw placement at C2. CT angiography must be obtained preoperatively. If there is a dominant vertebral artery on one side, the surgical plan must protect that side, and you may need to modify or abandon screw placement if the artery is at risk. Spinal cord injury during reduction is another concern, which is why awake, serial neurological examinations during closed reduction are mandatory. Hardware failure occurs most commonly in osteoporotic bone and is another reason to augment constructs or consider alternative fixation strategies in elderly patients.

Let's review the key evidence. The Tashjian study published in 2000 in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery demonstrated that halo vest immobilization in patients over 65 with Type II odontoid fractures had a 40 percent mortality rate and an 80 percent nonunion rate, compared with significantly better outcomes with surgical fixation. This is the single most important evidence citation for odontoid fracture management on the boards. The SLIC was originally published by Vaccaro and colleagues in 2007 in Spine and has been validated in subsequent studies as a reliable framework for guiding operative versus nonoperative management of subaxial cervical injuries. The AOSpine upper cervical injury classification was developed to provide a more comprehensive and reliable classification of occipitocervical injuries, building on the earlier Anderson-Montesano, Anderson-D'Alonzo, and Levine-Edwards systems. The Grauer modification of the Anderson-D'Alonzo classification further subdivides Type II odontoid fractures into IIA with minimal displacement, IIB with fracture displacement in a direction favorable for anterior screw fixation, and IIC with comminution or unfavorable fracture line, which helps guide choice between anterior screw and posterior fusion.

Let's close with board pearls. For classification recall, remember that upper cervical injuries each have their own named classification: Anderson-Montesano for occipital condyle, Traynelis for atlanto-occipital dislocation, Anderson-D'Alonzo for odontoid, and Levine-Edwards for hangman's fractures. For subaxial injuries, SLIC is your single unifying framework. The most common trap on the boards is the elderly patient with a Type II odontoid fracture. The examiner wants to hear you say that halo is dangerous in the elderly and that surgical fixation is preferred. The second most common trap is the Type IIA hangman's fracture. Never apply traction to a distraction injury. The third trap is the pre-reduction MRI question with locked facets and a disc herniation. Know the controversy, state both sides, and articulate a reasoned approach that prioritizes patient safety. Remember that the Rule of Spence threshold is 6.9 millimeters for combined lateral mass overhang in Jefferson fractures. Remember that the basion-dental interval threshold is 12 millimeters for atlanto-occipital dissociation. Remember that the Powers ratio threshold is 1.0. And remember that SLIC scores of 5 or greater are operative, 3 or less are nonoperative, and 4 is a judgment call.

One final pearl. When the examiner asks about vertebral artery evaluation before C1-C2 surgery, the answer is always CT angiography. A high-riding or anomalous vertebral artery changes your surgical plan. If both vertebral arteries are anomalous, you may need to abandon screw-based fixation entirely and use a wiring technique such as a Gallie or Brooks fusion with structural graft, even though these are biomechanically inferior.

That covers cervical spine trauma management. You're ready for this one.
