questions:
  - q: Explain, from first principles, why a patient can look stable and then crash.
    a: The Monro-Kellie doctrine. The skull is a fixed box; added volume is first buffered by displacing cerebrospinal fluid and compressing the venous compartment. Those buffers are finite, so the compliance curve is flat and then turns steeply upward, and a small extra volume produces a large pressure rise.
    probe: What sign on the intracranial pressure trace warns you before the mean rises?
    probeA: P2 exceeding P1. The three peaks are the percussion wave from arterial pulsation, the tidal wave reflecting compliance, and the dicrotic wave. Normally P1 is tallest; as compliance is exhausted the tidal wave rises and P2 overtakes P1, giving a rounded upward-sloping waveform. That change precedes any rise in the mean value, so it is the warning rather than the event. Lundberg A or plateau waves are already pathological.
  - q: Uncal herniation. Walk me through the structures in the order they fail.
    a: Third nerve first, parasympathetic pupillomotor fibres being superficial, so a dilated sluggish then fixed ipsilateral pupil before any extraocular weakness. Then the cerebral peduncle giving contralateral hemiparesis, then the posterior cerebral artery giving occipital infarction, then the reticular activating system and consciousness.
    probe: The hemiparesis is on the same side as the blown pupil. Explain.
    probeA: That is Kernohan's notch phenomenon, and it is a false localising sign. The expanding mass pushes the brainstem across the midline until the contralateral cerebral peduncle is compressed against the opposite free edge of the tentorium. Since that peduncle carries fibres destined for the side of the lesion, the resulting hemiparesis is ipsilateral to the mass and therefore ipsilateral to the blown pupil. The pupil is the reliable localiser, not the limb. Operating on the wrong side because of the hemiparesis is exactly the error this sign exists to prevent.
  - q: What are Duret haemorrhages and what do they mean?
    a: Brainstem haemorrhages in the pons and midbrain caused by shearing of perforating branches of the basilar artery as the brainstem is driven downward in central herniation. They indicate a terminal event and carry a grim prognosis, and I would say so plainly.
    probe: How does that change the family conversation?
    probeA: It moves the conversation from uncertainty to preparation. I would explain that the injury has now extended into the brainstem, that this is not something surgery can reverse, and that the outlook is extremely poor. I would avoid offering an operation as a gesture. I would shift the discussion toward what he would have wanted, involve palliative care, and allow the family time and repeated conversations rather than one. I would also be careful not to state it as absolute certainty in isolation, because the radiological finding should be interpreted alongside the clinical examination once confounders are excluded.
  - q: A child with a posterior fossa tumour and hydrocephalus gets an external ventricular drain and deteriorates. Why?
    a: Upward transtentorial herniation. Releasing supratentorial pressure creates a gradient that drives the vermis and midbrain up through the incisura, compressing the superior cerebellar arteries and the aqueduct. Drain cautiously at a high level, and proceed promptly to definitive posterior fossa decompression.
    probe: How would you have set the drain to reduce that risk?
    probeA: By draining at a deliberately high level and in small volumes, rather than opening the drain to free drainage at a low level. Practically, I would set it at around twenty to twenty-five centimetres of water, drain only enough to control pressure and improve conscious level, avoid rapid decompression, and accept a modestly raised pressure rather than normalising it. The definitive answer is timing: the drain is a bridge placed at the start of the case, with tumour resection proceeding in the same session, rather than a treatment in its own right that leaves the posterior fossa mass in situ for days.
  - q: Give me your cerebral perfusion pressure target and justify both ends.
    a: Roughly sixty to seventy millimetres of mercury. Driving higher with vasopressors risks acute respiratory distress syndrome; below about fifty risks ischaemia. After injury autoregulation is often lost, so flow becomes pressure passive and both hypotension and hypertension are directly harmful.
    probe: What is the equation you are using?
    probeA: Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. The practical corollary is that I can raise perfusion pressure from either side of the equation, and in a patient whose intracranial pressure is the abnormal term, lowering it is preferable to escalating vasopressors. I would also say that the arterial line must be zeroed at the external auditory meatus rather than at the heart, or the number is falsely reassuring by ten to fifteen millimetres of mercury.
  - q: Mannitol or hypertonic saline?
    a: Mannitol at roughly zero point two five to one gram per kilogram, monitoring osmolality and volume status, works rheologically then osmotically but causes diuresis. Hypertonic saline expands intravascular volume rather than depleting it, so it is preferred in the hypotensive or hypovolaemic patient.
    probe: What sodium are you targeting and what is the risk of overshoot?
    probeA: A sodium of around one hundred and forty-five to one hundred and fifty-five, with an osmolality up to about three hundred and twenty. I would monitor sodium and osmolality at least six-hourly during active therapy. The risks of overshoot are hypernatraemia with its own encephalopathy, renal impairment, and, if the sodium later falls rapidly, rebound cerebral oedema, which is the one that catches people out. With mannitol specifically I would watch the osmolar gap rather than osmolality alone, and watch for the hypovolaemia and renal injury that follow the diuresis.
  - q: Where does hyperventilation belong?
    a: As a bridge, never a therapy. It lowers pressure by hypocapnic vasoconstriction, which by definition risks ischaemia, and the effect wanes within hours as bicarbonate buffering equilibrates. Target thirty to thirty five millimetres of mercury while the theatre is being readied, and never below thirty, because profound hypocapnia causes ischaemia in exactly the injured brain you are trying to protect. Wean it rather than stopping abruptly, to avoid rebound vasodilatation.
    probe: What happens if you stop it abruptly?
    probeA: Rebound. Cerebral vessels have adapted to the hypocapnia through cerebrospinal fluid bicarbonate buffering, so abruptly restoring normocapnia produces relative hypercapnia at the vessel, causing vasodilatation, a rise in cerebral blood volume and a rebound rise in intracranial pressure. So hyperventilation is weaned gradually rather than stopped, and ideally not sustained long enough for that adaptation to become established in the first place.
  - q: Summarise the decompressive craniectomy evidence without overstating it.
    a: DECRA applied early decompression for moderately raised pressure and found worse functional outcomes. RESCUEicp applied it as last-tier therapy for refractory hypertension and reduced mortality but shifted survivors into greater dependency. So it is salvage, not routine, and the family must be counselled about quality of survival.
    probe: How big does the flap need to be, and why?
    probeA: At least twelve centimetres in anteroposterior diameter, and in practice a frontotemporoparietal flap extending to the floor of the middle fossa. The reason is that a small craniectomy does not decompress, it strangulates: brain herniates through the defect, the cortical veins are compressed at the bone edge, and the result is venous infarction and contusion of the herniated cortex. RESCUEicp and DECRA both specified large flaps for this reason. A decompression that is too small converts a treatment into an injury.
cases:
  - label: The deteriorating head injury
    stem: A 24-year-old motorcyclist arrives with a GCS of 8, intubated at scene. Pupils equal at 3 millimetres. CT shows bifrontal contusions with mild effacement of the basal cisterns, no evacuable collection. An intracranial pressure monitor reads 18 millimetres of mercury on admission to intensive care.
    q1: Set out your management for the first hours.
    a1: Tier zero physiology: head up thirty degrees, neck midline with no constricting collar, adequate sedation and analgesia, normothermia, normocapnia, seizure prophylaxis, correction of coagulopathy, and strict avoidance of hypotension and hypoxia, both of which independently worsen outcome. Maintain cerebral perfusion pressure at sixty to seventy.
    p1: His pressure is 18. Do you treat that?
    pa1: Not with tier one therapy, no. Eighteen is below the conventional treatment threshold of twenty to twenty-two, so I would not reach for hyperosmolar therapy or cerebrospinal fluid drainage on that number alone. What I would do is everything in tier zero properly, because those measures are free of harm and are what stop eighteen becoming twenty-eight, and I would watch the waveform and the trend rather than the isolated value. The nuance worth stating is that the threshold is not absolute: a pressure of eighteen with a rising trend, an exhausted-compliance waveform and effaced cisterns concerns me considerably more than a transient twenty-five in a patient with open cisterns and a normal trace.
    q2: Six hours later the pressure is 28 and the trace shows P two exceeding P one. Interpret and act.
    a2: P two above P one means compliance is exhausted, which precedes a rise in the mean value and is an early warning. With sustained pressure above twenty two I escalate: drain cerebrospinal fluid if a ventricular catheter is in place, give hyperosmolar therapy, and repeat the CT to exclude a new surgical lesion.
    p2: Which hyperosmolar agent here, and why?
    pa2: Hypertonic saline, for two reasons specific to this patient. He is a polytrauma motorcyclist who may be volume-depleted or actively bleeding elsewhere, and mannitol is an osmotic diuretic that would worsen that, whereas hypertonic saline expands the intravascular volume and supports the blood pressure. Second, avoiding hypotension matters more in traumatic brain injury than the marginal differences between the agents. Practically I would give three percent saline as a bolus of around two hundred and fifty millilitres, or twenty-three point four percent through a central line for acute herniation, monitoring sodium and osmolality at least six-hourly, targeting a sodium of one hundred and forty-five to one hundred and fifty-five. If he were euvolaemic with good renal function, mannitol at zero point twenty-five to one gram per kilogram would be equally reasonable.
    q3: Repeat CT shows expansion of the contusions with effaced cisterns but still nothing focally evacuable. Pressure is now 35 despite tier two measures. Options?
    a3: This is refractory intracranial hypertension. The tier three options are barbiturate coma titrated to burst suppression, therapeutic hypothermia, or decompressive craniectomy. Barbiturates cause hypotension and remove the neurological examination; hypothermia lowers pressure but has not shown outcome benefit. I would proceed to bifrontal decompressive craniectomy.
    p3: Why not barbiturates first, given they avoid an operation?
    pa3: Because they are not the lesser intervention they appear to be. Barbiturate coma causes profound hypotension requiring vasopressors, which is exactly the secondary insult I am trying to avoid, and it abolishes the neurological examination for days because of the long context-sensitive half-time of thiopentone or pentobarbital. It also causes immunosuppression, infection, ileus and hypothermia. And critically, the evidence that it improves outcome is weak: it reliably lowers intracranial pressure but has never demonstrated an outcome benefit, whereas RESCUEicp showed decompression reduces mortality. So in a young patient with a survivable injury and refractory pressure, I would decompress rather than commit him to a barbiturate coma with its own morbidity and no demonstrated benefit. I would reserve barbiturates for the patient who is not a surgical candidate or as a bridge.
    q4: The family asks whether the operation will make him better. What do you say?
    a4: I would be honest: it reliably lowers pressure and it saves lives, but RESCUEicp showed that the survival it buys includes a shift toward greater dependency, including vegetative state and severe disability. I would say we are choosing survival with an uncertain quality, not a return to normal, and that we will reassess as he declares himself.
    p4: They ask what he would look like in a year if he survives. Give them numbers.
    pa4: I would give them the RESCUEicp distribution rather than a single prediction. At six months in that trial, among surgical patients roughly a fifth were in a vegetative state or had lower severe disability, around thirty percent had upper severe disability, meaning dependent but conscious and interactive, and around thirty percent had moderate disability or good recovery, meaning independent. Mortality was roughly halved compared with medical management, from about half to about a quarter. At twelve months the picture improved somewhat, with more patients in the favourable categories, which is an important point because early assessments understate eventual recovery in young patients. I would say plainly that I cannot tell them which of those groups he will be in, that his youth is in his favour, and that we will know far more over the coming weeks than we can say today.

Let's master the herniation syndromes and their imaging correlates for the oral boards.

This topic is a favorite because it fuses anatomy, imaging, and emergency decision making into one question, and because the examiner can escalate it in real time. The scan gets worse, the pupil blows, the pressure climbs, and the examiner watches what you do. Your advantage is that herniation is mechanically deterministic. If you know which structure moves through which opening and what it compresses on the way, you can predict every clinical sign and every imaging finding without memorizing lists. Let's build it that way.

Begin with the compartments. The cranial vault is divided by rigid dural folds into functional boxes. The falx cerebri separates the two hemispheres in the midline but has a free inferior edge anteriorly. The tentorium cerebelli separates the supratentorial compartment from the posterior fossa, with a central opening, the tentorial incisura or hiatus, through which the midbrain passes. The foramen magnum is the exit at the base. Herniation is simply brain being pushed from a compartment of higher pressure to one of lower pressure through one of these openings. Five patterns matter.

The first is subfalcine herniation, also called cingulate herniation. The cingulate gyrus is pushed beneath the free edge of the falx toward the contralateral side. This is the most common pattern and usually the earliest visible on imaging. On CT you see midline shift, measured at the septum pellucidum, with displacement of the cingulate gyrus across the midline and effacement of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle. The critical structure at risk is the anterior cerebral artery, particularly the pericallosal branch, which can be compressed against the falx and produce a medial frontal and paramedian infarct with contralateral leg weakness. The other consequence is entrapment of the contralateral lateral ventricle: the foramen of Monro on the side being pushed toward gets obstructed, and the contralateral ventricle paradoxically dilates while the ipsilateral one is compressed. Recognizing that trapped ventricle is a strong examination answer.

For the examination, know how to describe it: there is approximately nine millimeters of left to right midline shift at the septum pellucidum with subfalcine herniation of the cingulate gyrus and effacement of the left lateral ventricle. Then give your threshold. Shift of roughly five millimeters is significant, and shift approaching or exceeding ten millimeters in a patient with a depressed level of consciousness generally warrants surgical decompression if there is an evacuable lesion. Do not recite thresholds as absolutes, because the clinical examination governs, but have the numbers.

The second and most heavily examined is uncal herniation, a form of descending transtentorial herniation. The uncus, the medial temporal lobe, is pushed medially over the edge of the tentorium into the suprasellar cistern. Build the clinical picture anatomically. The first structure encountered is the oculomotor nerve, running in the ambient cistern between the posterior cerebral and superior cerebellar arteries. The parasympathetic pupillomotor fibers run superficially on the dorsomedial surface of the nerve, so they are compressed first. That produces the earliest sign: an ipsilateral dilated, sluggish, then fixed pupil, the classic blown pupil, before any extraocular weakness. Later, the somatic motor fibers fail and you get ptosis and the eye deviated down and out.

Next the cerebral peduncle is compressed, producing contralateral hemiparesis, because the corticospinal tract decussates below. But know the false localizing sign: if the brain shifts far enough, the contralateral cerebral peduncle is pushed against the opposite tentorial edge, producing a notch called the Kernohan notch, and the resulting hemiparesis is ipsilateral to the mass. This is the classic trap. The pupil localizes correctly, the motor examination may not. Say this if hemiparesis and pupillary findings appear discordant, and you will score well.

Continuing, the posterior cerebral artery is compressed against the tentorial edge, producing occipital infarction with contralateral homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing. Then the reticular activating system in the midbrain is compromised, producing progressive depression of consciousness. On imaging, look for effacement of the suprasellar cistern, which should have that five or six pointed star shape and instead becomes asymmetric and then obliterated, widening of the ipsilateral ambient cistern early and effacement later, and rotation and distortion of the midbrain. Late findings include Duret hemorrhages, which are brainstem hemorrhages in the pons and midbrain caused by shearing of the perforating branches of the basilar artery as the brainstem is driven downward. Duret hemorrhages carry a grim prognosis and you should say so.

The third is central or downward transtentorial herniation, where the diencephalon and brainstem are pushed downward through the incisura in an axial rather than lateral direction. This occurs with diffuse cerebral edema or bilateral masses rather than a single focal lesion. Clinically, you see a rostral to caudal deterioration: initially small reactive pupils and Cheyne Stokes respirations from diencephalic involvement, progressing to midposition fixed pupils and hyperventilation with midbrain involvement, then to apneustic and finally ataxic breathing as the pons and medulla fail. The decorticate posturing of diencephalic dysfunction converts to decerebrate posturing as the lesion descends below the red nucleus. On imaging, you see flattening of the pons, obliteration of the prepontine and perimesencephalic cisterns, and downward displacement of the pineal gland. This pattern also stretches the pituitary stalk and can produce diabetes insipidus.

The fourth is tonsillar herniation, where the cerebellar tonsils descend through the foramen magnum and compress the medulla. This is the one that kills immediately, because the medulla contains the respiratory and cardiovascular centers. Clinically you see the Cushing response of hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respiration, along with neck stiffness, and then respiratory arrest. On imaging, look for crowding at the foramen magnum, obliteration of the cisterna magna and the perimedullary cisterns, and tonsils extending below the foramen magnum. This is the pattern that makes lumbar puncture dangerous in a patient with a posterior fossa mass or significant mass effect, and you should volunteer that if the examiner offers you a lumbar puncture in a patient with papilledema and a mass.

The fifth is upward or ascending transtentorial herniation, where a posterior fossa mass pushes the cerebellar vermis and midbrain upward through the incisura. On imaging the quadrigeminal cistern, normally a smile or W shape, is effaced and flattened, and the cerebellum appears to bulge upward. The superior cerebellar arteries can be compressed, producing cerebellar infarction, and the aqueduct is obstructed. Here is the high yield clinical scenario: a patient with a posterior fossa mass and obstructive hydrocephalus gets an external ventricular drain, the supratentorial pressure is suddenly released, and the pressure gradient now favors upward herniation, which the drain has just created. That is why you must be cautious with aggressive drainage in posterior fossa pathology, why you set the drain relatively high, drain slowly, and why definitive posterior fossa decompression should follow promptly. Examiners love this because it punishes the reflexive answer.

There is also external herniation, where brain extrudes through a craniectomy defect or a skull fracture. Beyond this, be aware of the syndrome of the trephined and paradoxical herniation in a patient with a large craniectomy defect who undergoes lumbar drainage, where atmospheric pressure drives the brain inward. The treatment is counterintuitive: Trendelenburg position, stop the drainage, hydrate, and replace the bone flap. That is a genuine examination scenario.

Now the management, because recognizing herniation earns you nothing if you cannot act. Move in tiers and say them in order. First, the immediate airway and physiology: secure the airway, ensure adequate oxygenation, avoid hypotension, and elevate the head of bed to thirty degrees with the neck midline and the cervical collar not obstructing venous outflow, which is a small detail examiners appreciate. Second, hyperosmolar therapy: either mannitol at roughly zero point two five to one gram per kilogram, watching volume status and serum osmolality, or hypertonic saline, which is often preferred in the hypotensive or hypovolemic patient because it expands intravascular volume rather than depleting it. Third, brief hyperventilation to a partial pressure of carbon dioxide around thirty to thirty five millimeters of mercury as a temporizing bridge only, understanding that it works through vasoconstriction and therefore risks ischemia, and that its effect wanes within hours as bicarbonate buffering equilibrates. It is a bridge to the operating room, not a therapy. Fourth, treat the cause: evacuate the hematoma, resect the mass, divert the cerebrospinal fluid, or perform a decompressive craniectomy. Sedation and analgesia to control metabolic demand, seizure control, normothermia or targeted temperature management, and correction of coagulopathy all belong in the discussion.

The sentence the examiner wants to hear is that imaging findings of herniation in a patient with a corresponding clinical decline constitute a surgical emergency and that medical measures are a bridge while the operating room is being readied. Do not linger on medical management while the patient herniates in front of you.

Ground all of this in the Monro Kellie doctrine, because the examiner may ask you to justify why herniation happens at all. The skull is a rigid box containing brain at roughly eighty percent of the volume, blood at roughly ten percent, and cerebrospinal fluid at roughly ten percent. Because the total volume is fixed, any increase in one compartment must be offset by a decrease in another or the pressure rises. The first buffers are displacement of cerebrospinal fluid into the spinal subarachnoid space and compression of the venous compartment. Those buffers are finite, which produces the characteristic intracranial compliance curve: for a long period, added volume produces almost no rise in pressure, and then, once compensation is exhausted, a very small additional volume produces a steep and accelerating rise. That is why a patient can look stable and then deteriorate precipitously, and saying this explicitly explains the clinical course better than any description of the scan.

Know the pressure numbers and the derived targets. Normal adult intracranial pressure is roughly five to fifteen millimetres of mercury; treatment is generally triggered above twenty to twenty two. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure, with a usual target of roughly sixty to seventy millimetres of mercury; driving it much higher with vasopressors risks acute respiratory distress syndrome, and letting it fall below about fifty risks ischaemia. Understand autoregulation: the healthy brain holds cerebral blood flow constant across a mean arterial pressure of roughly fifty to one hundred fifty millimetres of mercury, but after injury autoregulation is often lost, so flow becomes pressure passive and both hypotension and hypertension become directly harmful.

Be able to read the intracranial pressure waveform if offered. The normal trace has three peaks: P one, the percussion wave from arterial pulsation; P two, the tidal wave reflecting compliance; and P three, the dicrotic wave. When P two exceeds P one, compliance is exhausted, and that is an early warning that precedes any rise in the mean value. Lundberg A waves, also called plateau waves, are sustained elevations to fifty millimetres of mercury or more lasting five to twenty minutes and are pathological and ominous; B waves are shorter oscillations of lesser amplitude and indicate reduced compliance.

Attach the physiology to the herniation syndromes you have just described. The Cushing reflex, comprising hypertension, bradycardia and irregular respiration, is not a random triad: rising intracranial pressure compromises brainstem perfusion, the sympathetic response raises systemic pressure to restore it, and the resulting baroreceptor activation produces reflex bradycardia. It is a late and pre-terminal sign, and you should say so rather than treating it as a useful early marker.

Now give the management tiers with specifics, because vague answers fail here. Tier zero is the physiology: head elevated to thirty degrees, neck midline with no constricting collar or tape obstructing jugular outflow, adequate sedation and analgesia, normothermia, normocapnia, seizure prophylaxis where indicated, correction of coagulopathy, and avoidance of hypotension and hypoxia, both of which independently worsen outcome in traumatic brain injury. Tier one adds cerebrospinal fluid drainage through an external ventricular drain if one is in place, and hyperosmolar therapy. Mannitol is given at roughly zero point two five to one gram per kilogram, works by an initial rheological effect and then an osmotic gradient, and requires monitoring of serum osmolality, generally kept below about three hundred twenty milliosmoles per kilogram, with attention to the diuresis it causes and the risk of hypovolaemia and renal injury. Hypertonic saline, in concentrations from three percent up to twenty three point four percent, is often preferred in the hypotensive or hypovolaemic patient because it expands intravascular volume rather than depleting it; a common target is a sodium of roughly one hundred forty five to one hundred fifty five, and rapid correction or overshoot risks osmotic demyelination.

Tier two adds deeper sedation and neuromuscular blockade to reduce metabolic demand and prevent coughing and ventilator dyssynchrony. Tier three comprises the salvage measures: barbiturate coma, titrated to burst suppression on continuous electroencephalography, which reduces cerebral metabolic rate but causes hypotension, immunosuppression and loss of the neurological examination; therapeutic hypothermia, which lowers pressure reliably but has not demonstrated outcome benefit and carries coagulopathic and infectious risk; and decompressive craniectomy.

Be ready to discuss the craniectomy evidence, because it is the highest yield controversy in this topic. The DECRA trial applied early bifrontotemporoparietal decompression for moderately raised pressure and found worse functional outcomes in the surgical arm, which argues against early prophylactic decompression. The RESCUEicp trial applied last tier decompression for genuinely refractory intracranial hypertension and found reduced mortality but a shift of survivors into states of greater dependency, including vegetative state and severe disability, with benefit becoming clearer at longer follow up. The defensible position, and the one to articulate, is that decompressive craniectomy is a salvage procedure for refractory pressure rather than an early routine, that it reliably lowers pressure and saves lives, and that the family must be counselled honestly about the quality of survival rather than being told the operation simply works. Technically, the flap must be large, generally at least twelve centimetres in anteroposterior diameter, because a small craniectomy causes cortical strangulation at the bone edges, venous infarction and brain herniation through the defect.

Finally, remember hyperventilation properly. It lowers pressure by producing hypocapnic cerebral vasoconstriction, which reduces cerebral blood volume; the effect is rapid but wanes over hours as bicarbonate buffering restores pH, and abrupt discontinuation causes rebound hyperaemia. Because it works by vasoconstriction it directly risks ischaemia, so it is a bridge to a definitive intervention and nothing more. Target a partial pressure of carbon dioxide of roughly thirty to thirty five millimetres of mercury, avoid going below thirty except transiently in a herniating patient en route to the operating room, and say that you would use it while the theatre is being readied rather than as a therapy in its own right.

To summarize. Five patterns: subfalcine under the falx compressing the anterior cerebral artery and trapping the contralateral ventricle; uncal over the tentorium taking the third nerve first, then the peduncle, then the posterior cerebral artery, with the Kernohan notch as the false localizer and Duret hemorrhages as the terminal event; central downward with rostral to caudal deterioration; tonsillar through the foramen magnum causing the Cushing response and respiratory arrest; and upward through the incisura, classically precipitated by overdrainage in posterior fossa pathology. Read the cisterns on every scan, because cisternal effacement is your earliest and most reliable imaging warning. And always finish with the tiered management ending in definitive surgical decompression.
