questions:
  - q: STN or GPi for Parkinson disease? Give me the evidence.
    a: VA CSP-468 randomised 299 patients to bilateral STN versus bilateral GPi and found equivalent motor outcomes at twenty-four months. COMPARE similarly found equivalent UPDRS Part III improvement at twelve months. The difference lies in secondary outcomes, not motor efficacy.
    probe: What are those secondary differences?
    probeA: Medication reduction, which is substantially greater with subthalamic stimulation, and that matters in a patient burdened by dyskinesia or by drug side effects. Against that, VA CSP-468 found more decline in processing speed and working memory in the subthalamic group, and more depression, with the pallidal group showing a small improvement in mood. Pallidal stimulation also treats dyskinesia directly rather than indirectly through drug reduction, and it is more forgiving of programming. Subthalamic leads use less battery and the target is smaller. So the motor result is a draw and the choice is made on cognition, mood and drug burden.
  - q: So what actually separates the two targets?
    a: STN allows greater medication reduction, which matters in a patient troubled by dyskinesia or drug burden. GPi has fewer cognitive and behavioural adverse effects — VA CSP-468 showed slightly more cognitive and mood adverse effects in the STN group. So the target is chosen by the patient's profile.
    probe: Which would you choose for a patient with mild cognitive impairment?
    probeA: Pallidal. Mild cognitive impairment is a recognised risk factor for further cognitive decline after subthalamic stimulation, and the VA CSP-468 data showed more decline in processing speed and working memory in that arm. Choosing GPi gives equivalent motor benefit without that particular liability, and it does not depend on aggressive dopaminergic reduction, which itself can precipitate apathy. I would also want formal neuropsychology first to confirm this is mild impairment and not early dementia, because established dementia is a contraindication to stimulation at either target.
  - q: Which dystonia responds best, and by how much?
    a: Primary generalised dystonia with a DYT1 or TOR1A mutation — seventy to ninety percent improvement in the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale at one to three years. It is the best surgical outcome of any dystonia subtype.
    probe: What target do you use?
    probeA: The globus pallidus internus, bilaterally. That is the established target for primary generalised dystonia and the one used in the randomised trials, including Vidailhet and the Kupsch German study. Subthalamic stimulation has been used in dystonia and has some supporting evidence, with the possible advantage of a faster onset of benefit and lower stimulation energy, but pallidal remains the standard of care. The important counselling point is that the response in dystonia is delayed, developing over weeks to months rather than immediately as in Parkinson tremor, so both the patient and the programming team need patience.
  - q: What did Vidailhet establish?
    a: The 2005 NEJM randomised sham-controlled trial of bilateral GPi stimulation in primary generalised dystonia showed fifty-one percent improvement in the Burke-Fahn-Marsden motor score at one year, establishing level one evidence for deep brain stimulation in dystonia.
    probe: Why was a sham-controlled design important there?
    probeA: Because the outcome measure is a clinical rating scale scored by observation, so it is vulnerable to both patient expectation and assessor bias, and because dystonia fluctuates spontaneously. Without blinding you cannot distinguish a genuine stimulation effect from placebo, from the microlesion effect of electrode insertion, or from natural variation. Vidailhet handled this by implanting every patient and then randomising them to three months of active or sham stimulation with blinded video assessment, which isolates the effect of the current itself from the effect of the operation. That design is what makes it level one evidence rather than another case series, and it is why dystonia stimulation is on firmer ground than several other functional indications.
  - q: What did SANTE show?
    a: Published 2010, randomising one hundred and ten adults with medication-refractory focal epilepsy to bilateral anterior nucleus of thalamus stimulation versus sham. Forty percent seizure reduction at one year, rising to sixty-nine percent at five years. It established ANT stimulation for refractory focal epilepsy.
    probe: Does it produce seizure freedom?
    probeA: Rarely, and that is the honest counselling point. SANTE produced a median seizure reduction of roughly forty percent at one year rising to sixty-nine percent at five years, with about three-quarters of patients being responders by five years and a small minority, around sixteen percent, achieving at least six months of seizure freedom. So it is a palliative, seizure-reducing therapy, not a curative one. That is precisely why it is offered to patients who are not candidates for resective surgery, and why I would not offer it to someone with a well-localised resectable focus, where anterior temporal lobectomy gives sixty to eighty percent Engel class I.
  - q: What did PD SURG address?
    a: Lancet Neurology 2010, comparing deep brain stimulation plus best medical therapy against best medical therapy alone in Parkinson disease, supporting surgery over medical management alone for appropriately selected patients with advanced disease and motor complications.
    probe: How does EARLYSTIM extend that?
    probeA: EARLYSTIM moves the question from whether to when. PD SURG established that stimulation plus medical therapy beats medical therapy alone in advanced disease with established motor complications. EARLYSTIM then asked whether waiting for advanced disease is necessary, enrolling a younger cohort with a mean disease duration of about seven and a half years and motor complications present for three years or less, and showed superiority over best medical therapy for quality of life, motor disability, activities of daily living and motor complications. So together they establish that stimulation works, and that the window opens at the onset of troublesome fluctuations rather than at the end of the medical road.
  - q: Summarise EARLYSTIM and why it changed practice.
    a: It examined subthalamic stimulation earlier in the disease course — within seven and a half years of onset and three years of motor fluctuations — rather than waiting for advanced disease, and showed superiority over best medical therapy for quality of life, motor disability, motor complications and time with good mobility without dyskinesia. It widened the surgical window.
    probe: Does that mean you should operate on everyone early?
    probeA: No, and the trial does not support that reading. EARLYSTIM enrolled a specific population: a mean age of about fifty-two, disease duration of at least four years, motor complications present for three years or less, and crucially preserved levodopa responsiveness with no cognitive impairment or significant psychiatric disease. What it changed is that a patient with troublesome fluctuations should not be made to wait for years of deterioration before referral. What it did not establish is benefit in patients without motor complications, and the EARLYSTIM-style approach in very early disease without fluctuations remains investigational. The mandatory levodopa challenge and neuropsychological assessment still gate the decision.
  - q: Awake with microelectrode recording, or asleep under imaging guidance?
    a: Retrospective studies and one prospective trial have shown equivalent clinical outcomes between the two approaches. So the choice turns on patient tolerance, the team's expertise, and whether physiological confirmation is needed, rather than on demonstrated superiority of either.
    probe: Which patient could not tolerate an awake procedure?
    probeA: A patient who cannot lie still and cooperate for several hours: significant anxiety or claustrophobia, severe off-state dystonia or painful rigidity, prominent dyskinesia, severe cervical dystonia or tremor that prevents stable positioning, orthopnoea or respiratory compromise, and cognitive impairment or confusion that prevents cooperation with testing. Children almost always require general anaesthesia, which is why paediatric dystonia cases are typically done asleep. Severe medication-off symptoms are the commonest practical reason, since the awake procedure requires the patient to be off their dopaminergic drugs for twelve hours or more. In all of those, asleep imaging-guided implantation is the appropriate choice, and the outcome data support it.
cases:
  - label: Choosing a target
    stem: A 61-year-old man has had Parkinson disease for nine years. He now has motor fluctuations with three hours of off time daily and troublesome peak-dose dyskinesia. His UPDRS Part III is 52 off and 23 on, and he takes 1,200 milligrams of levodopa daily. Neuropsychology shows mild executive dysfunction. There is no hallucination or depression.
    q1: Is he a candidate? Show your reasoning with numbers.
    a1: Yes. His levodopa challenge shows a fifty-five percent improvement, from fifty-two off to twenty-three on, comfortably exceeding the mandatory thirty percent threshold on the UPDRS motor score — the best predictor of stimulation success. He has motor fluctuations and dyskinesia refractory to medical optimisation, which is the classic indication.
    p1: His neuropsychology shows mild executive dysfunction. Is that a contraindication?
    pa1: Not a contraindication, but a genuine risk factor that shapes the plan. Established dementia is a contraindication, because these patients do poorly, decline further after surgery and cannot engage with programming. Mild executive dysfunction sits in between: it is common in Parkinson disease at nine years, it does not preclude surgery, but it predicts a higher risk of further cognitive decline, particularly with subthalamic stimulation, and it is one of the strongest arguments for choosing the pallidal target. So I would proceed, choose GPi, ensure the neuropsychological assessment is formal and documented as a baseline, involve his family in the decision, and arrange repeat assessment postoperatively. What I would not do is ignore it or treat the surgery as cognitively neutral.
    q2: STN or GPi? Argue both and commit.
    a2: Motor outcomes are equivalent — VA CSP-468 at twenty-four months and COMPARE at twelve months both showed that. STN allows greater medication reduction, attractive given his twelve hundred milligrams and dyskinesia. GPi has fewer cognitive and behavioural adverse effects, and VA CSP-468 showed slightly more cognitive and mood problems with STN. Given his mild executive dysfunction, I would choose GPi.
    p2: His main complaint is dyskinesia. Does that not favour GPi anyway?
    pa2: It does, and it is a second independent reason to choose it here. Pallidal stimulation suppresses dyskinesia directly, through its effect on the pallidal output, so the benefit is immediate and does not depend on reducing his medication. Subthalamic stimulation improves dyskinesia mainly indirectly, by allowing a substantial reduction in levodopa, which means the benefit is contingent on achieving that reduction and can be lost if his medication has to be increased again. In a patient whose dominant problem is peak-dose dyskinesia, that distinction matters. Combined with his executive dysfunction, both arguments point to GPi, which makes this a relatively clear choice rather than a coin toss.
    q3: He asks whether he should have had this done years ago.
    a3: Possibly, and EARLYSTIM is the reason. It studied subthalamic stimulation within seven and a half years of onset and three years of motor fluctuations, and showed superiority over best medical therapy for quality of life, motor disability and time with good mobility. That widened the window from advanced disease to earlier fluctuation. But it does not mean operating on everyone early — he still needed demonstrable levodopa-responsive fluctuations to benefit.
    p3: Is there a risk in operating too early?
    pa3: Yes, and it is the reason EARLYSTIM should not be over-applied. The principal risk is diagnostic: at four to seven years from onset an atypical parkinsonian syndrome can still be masquerading as Parkinson disease, and implanting a patient with multiple system atrophy or progressive supranuclear palsy exposes them to hardware and surgical risk for a condition that will not respond. Beyond that, operating early commits a relatively young patient to decades of device management, battery changes and revision surgery, with the cumulative infection and hardware-failure risk that entails, and exposes them to surgical risk at a time when medical therapy is still delivering good control. So the gate remains a documented levodopa response, genuinely troublesome motor complications, and normal neuropsychology, rather than disease duration alone.
    q4: Six months after implantation his motor score is better but his wife reports he is more impulsive and low in mood. What do you do?
    a4: Take it seriously rather than attributing it to disease progression. Stimulation-induced mood and behavioural change is a recognised adverse effect, more so with subthalamic than pallidal targets. I would review the stimulation parameters and active contacts — a ventral contact spreading to limbic territory can produce exactly this — try reprogramming, and review his medication reduction, since rapid dopaminergic withdrawal can itself cause apathy and low mood. I would involve neuropsychiatry.
    p4: You reprogramme and it does not help. What then?
    pa4: Then I stop treating it as a stimulation problem and treat it as a psychiatric one requiring active management. I would involve neuropsychiatry formally rather than for an opinion, obtain imaging to confirm lead position if I have not already, and review the dopaminergic regimen in both directions: a dopamine agonist is a well-recognised cause of impulse control disorder and should be reduced or stopped, while overly rapid withdrawal of dopaminergic medication after surgery causes a recognised apathy and depression syndrome that responds to partially reinstating it. I would screen formally for depression and for suicidality, which carries a documented excess risk after subthalamic stimulation, and involve his wife directly since families usually detect these changes first. Treatment may include an antidepressant, and in refractory cases I would consider whether a more dorsal contact or a reduction in amplitude helps, accepting a slightly worse motor result for a better behavioural one.

Let's master deep brain stimulation targets for the oral boards.

Deep brain stimulation is one of the most rewarding topics in functional neurosurgery, and the examiner expects you to move fluently between target selection, surgical technique, programming principles, and complication management. You need to know every target-indication pair cold, and you need to understand the nuances of STN versus GPi for Parkinson disease because that comparison will almost certainly appear. Let's build this systematically.

Start with the concept itself. Deep brain stimulation involves chronic high-frequency electrical stimulation of specific deep brain nuclei through implanted electrodes connected by subcutaneous extension wires to an implantable pulse generator, or IPG, placed in the chest wall or less commonly the abdomen. The mechanism of DBS is not fully understood, and this is worth stating because the examiner may ask. The outdated model was that DBS simply inhibits the target nucleus, mimicking a lesion. The current understanding is more nuanced. DBS likely disrupts pathological oscillatory activity within basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuits, particularly beta-band oscillations in the thirteen to thirty Hertz range that correlate with parkinsonian bradykinesia and rigidity. This explains why DBS mimics the clinical effects of lesioning but with the critical advantages of adjustability and reversibility.

The hardware has three components. The electrode is a thin lead with four cylindrical contacts spaced one and a half millimeters apart. Modern directional leads have segmented contacts that divide the middle two rings into three sectors, allowing current to be steered radially away from side-effect-producing structures. The extension wire runs subcutaneously from the cranial lead behind the ear, down the neck, to the IPG. The IPG is the battery and electronics unit placed in an infraclavicular pocket, similar to a cardiac pacemaker. Rechargeable IPGs last fifteen to twenty-five years.

Two surgical techniques are in common use. The historical gold standard is awake surgery with microelectrode recording, or MER, where single-unit neuronal recordings confirm you are in the correct nucleus and macrostimulation testing confirms benefit and screens for side effects. The alternative is asleep surgery with intraoperative MRI guidance, where lead placement is confirmed by direct visualization on real-time MRI under general anesthesia. Retrospective studies and one prospective trial have shown equivalent clinical outcomes between the two approaches. You should be comfortable describing both.

Now let's go through each target and its indications systematically, because you must have these pairs memorized.

The VIM, the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus, is the target for essential tremor and other tremor-dominant conditions. The VIM is a thalamic relay nucleus that receives cerebellar afferent input via the dentatorubrothalamic tract and projects to motor cortex. Stimulation of the VIM suppresses tremor immediately, often dramatically. However, VIM stimulation does not improve bradykinesia, rigidity, or gait dysfunction. For this reason, VIM is not the preferred target for Parkinson disease, because it would address only one of the cardinal motor features while leaving the others untreated. VIM DBS is first-line for medication-refractory essential tremor.

The STN, the subthalamic nucleus, is the most common DBS target for Parkinson disease. The STN is a small, lens-shaped structure approximately four to five millimeters in diameter located in the subthalamus. It is a glutamatergic nucleus and the only excitatory nucleus in the basal ganglia indirect pathway. The critical anatomy for targeting is that the STN lies lateral to the red nucleus, anterior to the medial lemniscus, and superior to the substantia nigra pars reticulata. The therapeutic target is the dorsolateral sensorimotor territory of the STN. If your stimulating contact drifts ventromedially, you enter limbic and associative territories, which is where mood and behavioral side effects originate. STN stimulation improves all cardinal motor features of Parkinson disease: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and motor fluctuations. A major advantage of STN DBS is the ability to reduce levodopa equivalent daily dose by thirty to fifty percent, which in turn reduces levodopa-induced dyskinesias.

The GPi, the globus pallidus internus, is an alternative target for Parkinson disease and the first-line DBS target for dystonia. The GPi is the primary output nucleus of the basal ganglia, sending GABAergic projections to the thalamus via the ansa lenticularis and lenticular fasciculus. The posteroventral motor territory is the surgical target. For Parkinson disease, GPi stimulation directly suppresses dyskinesias. For dystonia, primary generalized dystonia with a DYT1 or TOR1A mutation shows the best response, with seventy to ninety percent improvement in the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale. GPi is also the target for tardive dyskinesia.

The ANT, the anterior nucleus of the thalamus, is the target for medication-refractory epilepsy. The ANT is a component of the Papez circuit, connecting to the cingulate gyrus, hippocampus, and mammillary bodies. Bilateral ANT stimulation modulates seizure propagation through this limbic circuit. The SANTE trial, which I will discuss in detail shortly, established this indication.

The centromedian-parafascicular complex, or CM-Pf, is an investigational target for Tourette syndrome. And the nucleus accumbens and anterior limb of the internal capsule, abbreviated NAcc and ALIC respectively, are targets for obsessive-compulsive disorder under an FDA humanitarian device exemption.

Moving to the clinical case.

You are consulted on a sixty-two-year-old right-handed man with a ten-year history of Parkinson disease. He was initially well controlled on levodopa-carbidopa, but over the past two years he has developed progressively worsening motor fluctuations. His levodopa wears off at three-hour intervals, he has troublesome peak-dose dyskinesias, and he experiences unpredictable off periods that significantly limit his function. He has tried adjunctive entacapone, rasagiline, and extended-release formulations without adequate control. On examination in the on-medication state, he has mild residual resting tremor of the right hand and moderate choreiform dyskinesias of both arms. In the off-medication state, tested after a twelve-hour levodopa washout, he has severe bilateral resting tremor, marked cogwheel rigidity, significant bradykinesia on finger tapping and rapid alternating movements, and a shuffling gait with reduced arm swing. His UPDRS Part III motor score is fifty-two in the off state and twenty-three in the on state, representing a fifty-five percent improvement with levodopa. MRI of the brain shows no structural abnormalities and no evidence of atypical parkinsonism. Neuropsychological testing reveals an MMSE of twenty-eight out of thirty, with intact executive function and no evidence of dementia.

Now let's go through the examiner interrogation.

Examiner: "Is this patient a good candidate for DBS?"

Your answer: Yes, this is an excellent candidate. The core selection principle is levodopa responsiveness. The minimum threshold is thirty percent improvement in UPDRS Part III on levodopa challenge. This patient shows fifty-five percent improvement, which is excellent. DBS improves the same symptoms that levodopa improves because both modulate the same basal ganglia circuitry. His motor fluctuations and dyskinesias are classic indications, his cognition is intact, and he has failed adequate medication optimization. The contraindications I screen for include atypical parkinsonism: multiple system atrophy with cerebellar ataxia or severe early autonomic failure, progressive supranuclear palsy with vertical gaze palsy and early falls, and corticobasal degeneration with asymmetric cortical signs such as alien limb or apraxia. I also exclude dementia defined as MMSE below twenty-four, active untreated psychiatric disease, coagulopathy, and inability to tolerate surgery.

Examiner: "Would you choose STN or GPi for this patient?"

Your answer: Both targets produce equivalent improvement in motor function. This has been demonstrated in two major randomized trials. The COMPARE trial randomized patients to STN versus GPi and found similar UPDRS Part III improvement at twelve months. The VA CSP-468 trial, also known as the VA cooperative study, randomized 299 patients to bilateral STN versus bilateral GPi DBS and found equivalent motor outcomes at twenty-four months.

The differences between the targets are clinically important. STN has two key advantages. First, it allows a thirty to fifty percent reduction in dopaminergic medication, which reduces medication costs and side effects. Second, it has lower battery drain because the therapeutic effect is achieved at lower stimulation amplitudes, extending IPG battery life. STN has disadvantages as well. It may worsen mood and cognition in patients who are vulnerable, particularly those with preexisting depression or borderline executive function. The VA CSP-468 trial showed slightly more cognitive and mood adverse effects in the STN group. STN also requires more precise targeting because the nucleus is small and the therapeutic window between motor benefit and side effects is narrower.

GPi has its own advantages. It directly and reliably suppresses dyskinesias, it has a safer cognitive and psychiatric profile, and the larger target volume makes it more forgiving of small targeting errors. GPi disadvantages include the inability to reduce dopaminergic medications and higher battery drain requiring more frequent IPG replacements or recharges.

For this specific patient with predominant motor fluctuations and dyskinesias, intact cognition, no psychiatric comorbidity, and a fifty-five percent levodopa response, either target is reasonable. Most high-volume centers would favor STN given the medication reduction benefit and the patient's intact cognitive profile. I would discuss both options with the patient and the multidisciplinary team.

Examiner: "Walk me through your surgical approach for bilateral STN DBS."

Your answer: I would perform frame-based stereotaxy, which remains the most accurate method, though frameless systems with equivalent accuracy are available. The morning of surgery, I apply a stereotactic frame, typically a Leksell or CRW frame, under local anesthesia. I obtain a stereotactic MRI with the frame in place, or I fuse a preoperative MRI to a stereotactic CT if the patient cannot tolerate MRI with the frame. For STN targeting, I use T2-weighted axial sequences where the STN appears as a hypointense structure lateral to the red nucleus and anterior to the medial lemniscus. I can also use susceptibility-weighted imaging for enhanced visualization. My target coordinates for the STN are approximately twelve millimeters lateral to the midcommissural point, two millimeters posterior to the midcommissural point, and four millimeters inferior to the intercommissural line. I refine these coordinates based on direct visualization of the nucleus on the patient's own anatomy.

For the awake MER-guided technique, I make a parasagittal burr hole roughly three centimeters lateral to midline, anterior to the coronal suture. I advance a microelectrode along the planned trajectory to the target. As the electrode descends through the thalamus, I record low-frequency thalamic neuronal activity. When the electrode enters the STN, I hear the characteristic signature: high-frequency, irregular, tonic firing at fifteen to thirty Hertz with a distinctly higher neuronal density and background activity compared to surrounding structures. The STN signal is unmistakable to an experienced electrophysiologist. Below the STN, entry into the substantia nigra pars reticulata produces regular, high-frequency firing at seventy to eighty Hertz. If I do not encounter the expected STN signature, my trajectory is off and I need to make a second pass with a corrected trajectory.

After confirming I am in the STN with MER, I perform macrostimulation. I stimulate at each millimeter along the trajectory through the nucleus, looking for clinical benefit and screening for side effects. Improvement in contralateral rigidity and tremor at low stimulation thresholds, typically one to three volts, confirms I am in the sensorimotor territory. Side effects to watch for are critical and highly localizable. If I see contralateral tonic contraction of the face or limbs, I am stimulating the internal capsule, meaning the lead is too medial or too anterior. If the patient reports paresthesias, the current is spreading posteriorly to the medial lemniscus. If I provoke conjugate eye deviation or diplopia, I am too medial, near cranial nerve III fibers or the medial longitudinal fasciculus. If the patient reports mood changes such as sudden tearfulness or euphoria, I am in the ventromedial limbic territory of the STN. Once I identify the optimal location, I remove the microelectrode and place the permanent DBS lead, securing it with a burr hole cap or anchor. I then repeat the process on the contralateral side. The IPG is placed in a subcutaneous infraclavicular pocket, connected via tunneled extension wires, either on the same day or as a staged procedure.

Examiner: "How do you program this patient's device postoperatively?"

Your answer: Initial programming begins two to four weeks after surgery to allow the microlesion effect to subside. The microlesion effect is transient clinical improvement from electrode insertion itself, which confounds early assessments. I perform a monopolar review, testing each contact individually with the contact as cathode and the IPG case as anode. Starting at the most ventral contact, I gradually increase voltage using a standard frequency of one hundred thirty Hertz and pulse width of sixty microseconds. At each contact, I identify the therapeutic threshold and the side effect threshold. The contact with the widest therapeutic window becomes the active contact.

Standard programming parameters for STN in Parkinson disease are amplitude of one and a half to three and a half volts or two to five milliamps in constant current devices, frequency of one hundred thirty Hertz, and pulse width of sixty to ninety microseconds. There are important variations. If the patient has levodopa-resistant freezing of gait, some evidence supports lowering the frequency to sixty Hertz, which may improve axial symptoms. For dystonia treated with GPi DBS, some centers use lower frequencies of forty to sixty Hertz, though the standard one hundred thirty Hertz is also effective. Wider pulse widths recruit larger fiber populations but also increase side effects and battery drain. Directional leads add another dimension. If a standard ring contact produces both therapeutic benefit and a side effect at the same voltage, I can switch to a directional configuration and steer current toward the therapeutic zone and away from the offending structure, effectively widening the therapeutic window.

Examiner: "Tell me about GPi DBS for dystonia."

Your answer: GPi is the first-line DBS target for dystonia. The posteroventral motor GPi is targeted, the same region used for pallidotomy. The most important clinical nuance is that the response to GPi DBS in dystonia is delayed. Unlike Parkinson disease, where tremor suppression is essentially immediate with STN or VIM stimulation, dystonia improvement evolves over weeks to months. The full benefit may not be apparent for six to twelve months. This is critical because families and patients must be counseled about this delayed timeline, and the temptation to abandon or revise a well-placed lead prematurely must be resisted. The best responders are DYT1 or TOR1A mutation carriers, with seventy to ninety percent improvement on the Burke-Fahn-Marsden scale. The Vidailhet 2005 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine showed fifty-one percent improvement at one year with bilateral GPi DBS in primary generalized dystonia. Secondary dystonias respond less predictably and less robustly.

Examiner: "What about DBS for epilepsy?"

Your answer: The anterior nucleus of the thalamus is the FDA-approved target for medication-refractory focal epilepsy, based on the SANTE trial. The SANTE trial, published in 2010, randomized one hundred ten adults with medication-refractory focal epilepsy to bilateral ANT stimulation versus sham stimulation. At the end of the blinded phase, the stimulation group showed a forty percent reduction in seizure frequency compared with baseline, while the sham group showed a fourteen and a half percent reduction, a statistically significant difference. At five-year follow-up in the open-label extension, the median seizure reduction was sixty-nine percent, with sixteen percent of patients achieving seizure freedom for at least six months. The FDA approved the device in 2018. The main alternative is responsive neurostimulation, or RNS, which detects seizure onset electrographically and delivers stimulation to the focus. RNS requires an identifiable seizure focus, whereas ANT DBS is a network-level intervention useful when the onset zone is multifocal or in eloquent cortex.

Examiner, and this is a trap: "Your patient with Parkinson disease says his main complaint is freezing of gait. He freezes multiple times daily, even when his medications are working. Should you offer DBS?"

Your answer: This is a critical distinction. Freezing of gait that persists in the on-medication state, meaning it is not levodopa-responsive, responds poorly to DBS. The principle that DBS improves what levodopa improves applies here. Levodopa-responsive freezing, the type that occurs during off periods, may improve with DBS because you are essentially extending the on-state. But if freezing is present even in the on state and it is the predominant complaint, DBS is unlikely to help, and frankly, the presence of medication-resistant freezing of gait should raise concern for an atypical parkinsonian syndrome such as progressive supranuclear palsy, where DBS is contraindicated. I would not offer this patient DBS based on freezing alone.

Examiner: "You've placed the left STN lead and you're performing macrostimulation. On contact two, you see contralateral facial pulling. What does this mean and what do you do?"

Your answer: Contralateral facial pulling during macrostimulation indicates that current is spreading to the corticobulbar fibers within the internal capsule. This means the lead or at least that contact is positioned too medially or too anteriorly, placing it adjacent to the genu or posterior limb of the internal capsule. My response depends on context. First, I check the other contacts. If a more dorsal or lateral contact provides therapeutic benefit without capsular side effects, the lead position may still be acceptable because I can simply program around that contact. If directional leads are in use, I can steer current laterally and away from the capsule. If all contacts produce capsular effects at low thresholds, the lead trajectory needs to be revised with a more lateral or posterior pass.

Examiner: "On postoperative day one, the patient becomes acutely confused with contralateral hemiparesis. What happened?"

Your answer: The most concerning diagnosis is intracerebral hemorrhage, which occurs in one to two percent of cases per lead. I obtain an emergent non-contrast CT of the head. If the scan confirms an intracerebral hemorrhage, management depends on the size and clinical impact. A small, asymptomatic hemorrhage along the lead tract can be observed with serial imaging and close neurological monitoring. A large hemorrhage with significant mass effect and progressive neurological decline may require surgical evacuation, which is a difficult situation because the DBS lead is in the operative field. Pneumocephalus, seizure, and cerebral edema are on the differential, but hemorrhage must be excluded first.

Now let's consolidate the key trial evidence.

The VA CSP-468 trial, the Veterans Affairs Cooperative Studies Program trial, randomized 299 patients to bilateral STN versus bilateral GPi DBS. At twenty-four months, both groups showed similar motor improvement on the UPDRS Part III. STN patients had a greater reduction in dopaminergic medications. The STN group had slightly more adverse cognitive and mood effects, including worsening on measures of processing speed and depression.

The COMPARE trial also randomized Parkinson disease patients to STN versus GPi and found equivalent UPDRS motor improvement at twelve months. Secondary analyses suggested GPi may be preferable in patients with preexisting mood or cognitive concerns.

The PD SURG trial, published in Lancet Neurology in 2010, compared DBS plus best medical therapy versus best medical therapy alone. DBS provided a clinically and statistically significant improvement in quality of life and motor function, establishing Level I evidence for DBS in advanced Parkinson disease with motor fluctuations.

The EARLYSTIM trial examined STN DBS earlier in the disease course, within seven point five years of motor fluctuation onset, rather than waiting for advanced disease. Patients randomized to early DBS plus medical therapy had significantly better quality of life at two years compared with best medical therapy alone. This trial shifted practice toward earlier consideration of DBS.

The SANTE trial, as discussed, established bilateral ANT stimulation for medication-refractory focal epilepsy with forty percent seizure reduction at one year and sixty-nine percent at five years.

The Vidailhet 2005 study in the New England Journal of Medicine was a randomized sham-controlled trial of bilateral GPi DBS in primary generalized dystonia, showing fifty-one percent improvement in the Burke-Fahn-Marsden motor score at one year, establishing Level I evidence for DBS in dystonia.

Now let's close with board pearls.

Target-indication pairs, memorized: VIM for essential tremor. STN for Parkinson disease, first-line in most centers. GPi for Parkinson disease as an alternative, and GPi first-line for primary generalized dystonia and tardive dyskinesia. ANT for medication-refractory focal epilepsy. CM-Pf for Tourette syndrome, investigational. Nucleus accumbens and ALIC for OCD under humanitarian device exemption.

STN stereotactic coordinates: twelve millimeters lateral, two millimeters posterior, four millimeters inferior to the midcommissural point. Refine with direct targeting on T2-weighted MRI.

MER signature for STN: high-frequency irregular tonic firing at fifteen to thirty Hertz with high background neuronal density. Below the STN, the substantia nigra pars reticulata fires regularly at seventy to eighty Hertz.

Capsular spread equals too medial. If macrostimulation causes contralateral tonic muscle contraction, you are stimulating the internal capsule. Move the active contact laterally or dorsally, use directional steering, or revise the lead trajectory.

Paresthesias equal too posterior. Current is hitting the medial lemniscus.

Diplopia or gaze deviation equals too medial. Current is reaching oculomotor fibers.

Dystonia response is delayed. Weeks to months for full effect. Do not abandon a well-placed GPi lead prematurely.

DBS improves what levodopa improves. On-state freezing of gait will not respond. Medication-resistant symptoms suggest atypical parkinsonism and contraindicate DBS.

STN allows medication reduction. GPi does not. Both produce equivalent motor outcomes. Choose based on the patient's cognitive, psychiatric, and medication profile.
