Welcome back. Today we are covering cavernous malformation management. This is a high-yield vascular topic that shows up on the oral boards with predictable regularity, and the examiners have very specific answers they want to hear. If you know the natural history numbers, the Zabramski classification, the brainstem safe entry zones, and the classic trap questions, you will handle this one cleanly. Let's build it from the ground up.

Let's start with the foundation. A cavernous malformation, also called a cavernoma or cavernous angioma, is a low-flow vascular malformation composed of thin-walled sinusoidal vascular channels lined by a single layer of endothelium. There is no intervening brain parenchyma between the vascular channels. That is the defining histological feature and what distinguishes it from other vascular malformations. There is no smooth muscle in the walls, no elastin, and no mature vessel architecture. The channels are prone to repeated microhemorrhages, and the surrounding brain develops a characteristic hemosiderin ring from the breakdown products of prior bleeds. There are no large feeding arteries and no large draining veins in the malformation itself, which is why these lesions are angiographically occult. That point matters enormously and will come up in your viva.

Prevalence is 0.4 to 0.8 percent of the general population based on autopsy and MRI screening studies. They account for roughly 5 to 13 percent of all central nervous system vascular malformations. There are two forms you must distinguish. Sporadic cases make up about 80 percent and typically present with a single lesion. Familial cases account for roughly 20 percent, present with multiple lesions, and follow autosomal dominant inheritance. The familial form is linked to three genes. CCM1 maps to the KRIT1 gene on chromosome 7q. CCM2 maps to the malcavernin gene, also called osmosensing scaffold for MEKK3, on chromosome 7p. CCM3 maps to the PDCD10 gene on chromosome 3q. CCM3 mutations tend to produce a more aggressive phenotype with earlier onset, higher hemorrhage rates, and an association with meningiomas. The Hispanic familial form, originally described in families from the American Southwest with Mexican heritage, is almost always a CCM1 KRIT1 founder mutation.

Now the Zabramski MRI classification. You must know all four types cold. Type I represents subacute hemorrhage. It is hyperintense on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences, reflecting the presence of methemoglobin. This is the acute or subacute bleed pattern. Type II is the classic appearance, the one everyone calls the popcorn lesion. It shows a reticulated core of mixed signal intensity on both T1 and T2, representing hemorrhage in various stages of evolution, surrounded by a hypointense hemosiderin rim on T2-weighted imaging. This is the most commonly tested image on the boards. Type III represents chronic, organized hemorrhage. It is isointense or hypointense on T1 and hypointense on T2, essentially an old hemosiderin ball. It can be mistaken for other lesions if you are not looking carefully. Type IV lesions are punctate foci that are only visible as tiny hypointense dots on gradient echo or susceptibility-weighted imaging sequences. They are invisible on conventional spin-echo sequences. Type IV lesions are far more common in the familial form and their presence in large numbers should prompt you to think about genetic screening.

Natural history numbers you must quote precisely. The annual hemorrhage rate for sporadic supratentorial cavernous malformations in patients without prior hemorrhage is approximately 0.5 percent per year. Once a lesion has hemorrhaged, the risk of rehemorrhage climbs substantially, often cited at 4 to 5 percent per year in the years immediately following the initial bleed. For brainstem cavernous malformations that have previously hemorrhaged, the annual rehemorrhage rate is significantly higher, reported at up to 6 percent per lesion-year in several prospective series. There is a well-described phenomenon of temporal clustering, meaning that hemorrhages tend to recur within the first two to three years after an initial bleed, and then the rate may settle back toward baseline. This clustering effect is important because it influences surgical decision-making and the urgency of intervention.

Now let's move into the clinical case. A 35-year-old otherwise healthy woman presents to the emergency department with acute onset of diplopia and gait ataxia. She describes the symptoms coming on over several hours. On examination, she has a right sixth nerve palsy and bilateral limb dysmetria with a wide-based gait. She is alert, oriented, and neurologically intact otherwise. An MRI of the brain is obtained. It shows a 1.8-centimeter lesion in the dorsal pons with a hyperintense core on T1, consistent with subacute hemorrhage, surrounded by a mixed-signal reticulated rim with a peripheral hemosiderin ring on T2. There is mild mass effect on the fourth ventricle but no obstructive hydrocephalus. The lesion is consistent with a Zabramski Type I cavernous malformation with acute hemorrhage in the dorsal pons. This is your case for the viva. Let's work through the examiner questions.

The examiner opens with workup. What imaging do you want? The answer is MRI of the brain with and without gadolinium, and you specifically request gradient echo or susceptibility-weighted imaging sequences. GRE and SWI are exquisitely sensitive for hemosiderin and will identify additional lesions that conventional spin-echo sequences miss. This is critical for determining if the patient has a single lesion or multiple lesions, which changes your genetic counseling. Do you want a cerebral angiogram? No. Cavernous malformations are angiographically occult. They have no arterial feeders and no early draining veins visible on conventional angiography. Ordering an angiogram wastes time, costs money, and carries procedural risk for zero diagnostic yield. If the examiner asks whether the angiogram might show anything, you can note that occasionally a faint capillary blush is described, but the lesion is functionally invisible on angiography and the study is not indicated. Do you want a CTA or MRA? Not specifically for the cavernous malformation, though CTA may have been obtained in the initial emergency department workup to exclude other pathology. The key point is that conventional angiography is not needed.

Now, management decision. The examiner asks, what do you do with this brainstem cavernous malformation that has hemorrhaged? This is where you lay out the decision framework. The indications for surgical resection of a cavernous malformation are symptomatic hemorrhage, progressive neurological deficit, medically refractory seizures for supratentorial lesions, and an accessible surgical location. For brainstem lesions specifically, the threshold is higher. You need at least one symptomatic hemorrhage, and ideally the lesion should come to a pial or ependymal surface, because approaching a deeply seated brainstem cavernous malformation through normal pontine tissue carries unacceptable risk. In this case, the lesion is dorsal pontine, which means it abuts the floor of the fourth ventricle. A dorsal pontine cavernous malformation that has hemorrhaged and presents to an ependymal surface is a surgical lesion.

Timing matters. You do not operate in the acute phase. You wait 4 to 6 weeks after the hemorrhage. There are two reasons. First, the acute edema and mass effect resolve, reducing the surgical risk. Second, and more importantly, the hemosiderin plane that forms around the hematoma cavity creates a natural dissection plane between the cavernous malformation and the surrounding brainstem parenchyma. That hemosiderin-stained gliotic plane is your friend in surgery. It allows you to dissect the lesion circumferentially without violating normal brainstem tissue. If you go in too early, the tissue is swollen, friable, and angry, and you lose that plane.

Approach selection for brainstem cavernous malformations uses the two-point method described by Spetzler and colleagues. You draw a line from the center of the lesion to the nearest pial or ependymal surface, and that defines your surgical trajectory. The goal is to traverse the least amount of normal brainstem tissue and to enter through a safe entry zone. For a dorsal pontine lesion like our case, the approach is a suboccipital telovelar approach to the floor of the fourth ventricle. The safe entry zones in the pons include the peritrigeminal zone on the anterolateral surface, the suprafacial triangle above the facial colliculus, and the infrafacial triangle below the facial colliculus between the facial nerve root exit and the striae medullaris. For the midbrain, the lateral mesencephalic sulcus is the key safe entry zone. For the medulla, the posterior median sulcus and the posterolateral sulcus are available. You must name these zones confidently. The examiners want to hear that you understand brainstem functional anatomy and are not just diving into the pons blindly.

For supratentorial cavernous malformations, approach selection is based on depth from the cortical surface, eloquence of surrounding brain, and the geometry of any associated hematoma cavity. Superficial lobar lesions in non-eloquent cortex are straightforward craniotomies. Lesions in eloquent cortex may require awake mapping, cortical and subcortical stimulation, and frameless stereotactic navigation. Deep lesions near the ventricles can sometimes be approached through a transventricular or transcortical-transventricular route.

Now the developmental venous anomaly question. The examiner will almost certainly ask about the DVA, and this is one of the definitive trap questions. A developmental venous anomaly is frequently found in association with a cavernous malformation. It appears as a classic caput medusae pattern on contrast-enhanced MRI, with small medullary veins converging on a single large collecting vein. The DVA is not pathological. It represents the normal venous drainage of that region of brain. It developed because the normal superficial venous drainage failed to form properly during embryogenesis, and the DVA is the compensatory drainage pathway. If you resect the DVA, you eliminate the only venous outflow for that territory of brain, and the result is venous infarction. The answer on the boards is absolute. You never resect the developmental venous anomaly. You resect the cavernous malformation and leave the DVA completely intact. If the examiner pushes you by saying the DVA is right there and seems abnormal, you hold your ground. The DVA stays.

The next trap question involves familial screening. The examiner tells you that the postoperative MRI, or perhaps the preoperative MRI, shows two additional small cavernous malformations in the cerebral hemispheres. Now the patient has at least three lesions. Would you screen the family? Yes. The presence of two or more cavernous malformations in a single patient should raise suspicion for the familial form. Current guidelines recommend genetic counseling and testing for CCM1, CCM2, and CCM3 mutations. First-degree relatives should be offered screening MRI with SWI sequences if a pathogenic mutation is identified in the proband. You do not need to screen family members of a patient with a single sporadic cavernous malformation, but multiple lesions demand genetic evaluation.

Complications of surgical resection include new neurological deficit, which for brainstem cases can mean cranial nerve palsies, hemiparesis, or worsened ataxia. Transient worsening is common and expected. Permanent new deficit rates in experienced brainstem CCM series range from 10 to 20 percent, though many of those deficits improve over months. Rehemorrhage from residual cavernous malformation is another risk. If you leave a fragment behind, it can rebleed. The goal of surgery is gross total resection of the entire cavernous malformation, including the hemosiderin-stained rim if it can be safely removed without injuring normal structures. In brainstem cases, most surgeons accept leaving a thin rim of hemosiderin on the brainstem surface rather than aggressively pursuing every last fleck of discoloration.

Now radiosurgery. The examiner may ask about stereotactic radiosurgery for cavernous malformations. The answer is nuanced. Radiosurgery is not first-line treatment. It is controversial. Some centers have reported that radiosurgery at doses of 12 to 15 Gray to the margin may reduce the rehemorrhage rate for deep or surgically inaccessible cavernous malformations, particularly in the brainstem or thalamus, after a latency period of roughly two years. However, the evidence is retrospective, there are no randomized controlled trials, and there is a real risk of radiation-induced adverse events including edema, new neurological deficits, and even radiation necrosis in eloquent locations. The current consensus is that radiosurgery may be considered for surgically inaccessible lesions that have hemorrhaged at least twice, but it should not be offered as a primary treatment when safe microsurgical resection is feasible. If the examiner asks you whether you would radiate our dorsal pontine lesion, the answer is no, because it presents to a surface and is surgically accessible.

Moving to the evidence base. The landmark natural history data come from several prospective cohort studies. The work by Kondziolka and colleagues in the 1990s established the baseline annual hemorrhage rates. Del Curling and colleagues published early natural history data showing the 0.5 percent per year hemorrhage rate for incidentally discovered lesions. Barker and colleagues at Johns Hopkins published a large meta-analysis confirming the higher rehemorrhage rates after initial hemorrhage and the temporal clustering phenomenon. For brainstem surgical series, the work by Samii and colleagues in Hannover and by Porter and colleagues at Barrow Neurological Institute demonstrated that microsurgical resection of brainstem cavernous malformations is safe and effective in experienced hands, with gross total resection rates above 90 percent and acceptable morbidity. Gross and Du published a comprehensive review of brainstem safe entry zones that is required reading. The genetic testing guidelines were formalized in the Angioma Alliance consensus recommendations, which state that any patient with two or more cavernous malformations on MRI should be referred for genetic counseling and testing.

Now let's close with board pearls. First, rapid-fire points. Cavernous malformations are angiographically occult. Always say that. The Zabramski classification has four types. Type II is the popcorn lesion. Type IV is only visible on GRE or SWI. Annual hemorrhage rate for sporadic unruptured lesions is 0.5 percent per year. Brainstem lesions with prior hemorrhage can reach 6 percent per year. DVAs are never resected. Two or more lesions means genetic screening. Surgical timing is 4 to 6 weeks after hemorrhage to allow the hemosiderin plane to mature. The two-point method guides your surgical trajectory in the brainstem.

Now, three things that will fail you on this question. Number one, saying you would order a cerebral angiogram. The examiners will interpret this as a fundamental misunderstanding of the lesion's pathophysiology. Cavernous malformations are occult on angiography. Number two, saying you would resect the developmental venous anomaly. This tells the examiner you do not understand the functional significance of the DVA and you would cause a venous infarction in your patient. Number three, operating in the acute phase without waiting for the hemosiderin dissection plane to develop. Rushing to surgery in the first week after a brainstem hemorrhage when the tissue is edematous and the planes are obscured is a recipe for a bad outcome.

And never-say mistakes. Never say you would biopsy a cavernous malformation. These are not tumors and a biopsy risks hemorrhage without changing management. Never suggest endovascular embolization. There are no arterial feeders to embolize. Never say radiosurgery is your first choice for a surgically accessible lesion. And never forget to mention the hemosiderin ring when describing the MRI appearance, because it is pathognomonic and demonstrates that you understand the pathophysiology of repeated microhemorrhages.

That covers cavernous malformation management. You're ready for this one.
