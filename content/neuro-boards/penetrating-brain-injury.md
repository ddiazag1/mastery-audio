Welcome back. This session covers penetrating brain injury, a topic the oral board examiners love because it tests your decision-making under pressure, your knowledge of ballistics, your surgical judgment about what to do and critically what not to do, and your understanding of delayed complications that will kill your patient if you forget to screen for them. Let's build this from the ground up.

Let's start with definitions and epidemiology. A penetrating brain injury occurs when an object breaches the calvarium and enters the brain parenchyma. The most common cause by far is a gunshot wound, and you need to distinguish civilian low-velocity gunshot wounds from military high-velocity injuries because the wound ballistics and management differ substantially. A civilian gunshot wound involves a low-velocity projectile, typically traveling at 300 to 600 meters per second, which creates a permanent cavity along the trajectory of the bullet. The tissue destruction is largely confined to this permanent track. A military or high-velocity injury, from a rifle round traveling at 600 to 900 meters per second or faster, generates a temporary cavitation effect that creates a zone of injury far wider than the permanent cavity itself. This temporary cavitation stretches and shears brain tissue radially, producing a much broader region of contusion, edema, and vascular disruption than you would expect from the entrance wound alone. Other mechanisms include stab wounds from knives or screwdrivers, industrial accidents involving nails or metal fragments, and falls onto objects.

Now the numbers, and these matter for prognosis discussions. The overall mortality for gunshot wounds to the head is 90 percent. Seventy percent of victims die at the scene before ever reaching a hospital. Of the survivors who arrive alive at a trauma center, 50 percent will still die. The single strongest prognostic factor is the Glasgow Coma Scale score at presentation after resuscitation, not the field GCS. A patient presenting with a GCS of 3 to 5 and bilateral fixed dilated pupils has near 100 percent mortality and you should be thinking about goals of care and organ donation, not surgical heroics. A patient with a GCS of 8 or higher has a survival rate exceeding 75 percent. Trajectory matters enormously as well. Bihemispheric injuries, meaning the projectile crossed midline, transventricular trajectories, and posterior fossa trajectories all carry the worst prognosis. A perforating injury, where there is both an entrance and an exit wound, carries a worse prognosis than a penetrating injury where the projectile is retained, because the perforating trajectory by definition traversed more brain.

Understanding the ballistics helps you interpret the CT. When a bullet enters the skull, it fractures the calvarium and drives fragments of bone inward. These bone fragments become secondary missiles that lacerate brain tissue along and adjacent to the primary trajectory. The bullet itself causes direct brain laceration, cavitation, contusion, and hemorrhage. The projectile may fragment on impact, ricochet off the inner table of the skull and change direction, or cross midline entirely. On CT you will see the hyperdense bullet or its fragments, the fracture pattern at the entry site, the trajectory through the brain, associated hemorrhage which can include subdural hematoma, epidural hematoma, intracerebral hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage, as well as pneumocephalus and the distribution of bone fragments driven into the parenchyma.

Now let's work through a clinical case. A 30-year-old man presents to your trauma bay with a gunshot wound to the left frontal region. There is a single entrance wound and no exit wound. His GCS is 9, breaking down to E2V3M4. His left pupil is 4 millimeters and reactive. His right pupil is 3 millimeters and reactive. He has a right hemiparesis. He has been intubated in the emergency department. The CT head shows a left frontal entrance wound with indriven bone fragments, a bullet trajectory through the left frontal lobe terminating in the left basal ganglia region, a left frontal contusion with surrounding hemorrhage, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, and no midline shift. A CT angiogram has been obtained and shows no vascular injury.

This is your patient. The examiner is sitting across from you. Let's walk through exactly how you manage this and how you handle the questions they are going to throw at you.

Initial management follows ATLS protocol. Airway, breathing, circulation. This patient is already intubated, which is appropriate for a GCS of 9. You complete your primary survey, your secondary survey, and you assess the GCS after resuscitation, not relying on the field number. You administer tetanus prophylaxis. You start broad-spectrum antibiotics immediately. The standard regimen is cefazolin plus metronidazole, or for broader coverage, vancomycin plus ceftriaxone. These antibiotics are critical because penetrating brain injury is by definition a contaminated wound. Skin, hair, bone fragments, and foreign material have been driven into the brain. You also start seizure prophylaxis for 7 days. The Brain Trauma Foundation recommends levetiracetam or phenytoin. And here is an important nuance the examiners may test you on. Antiepileptic drugs given prophylactically prevent early seizures within the first 7 days but do not prevent the development of late post-traumatic epilepsy. This is a well-established finding. You give them for 7 days and then stop unless the patient has had a clinical seizure.

Now here is the single most important rule in penetrating brain injury management, and if you remember nothing else from this session, remember this. Do not remove embedded objects in the emergency department. If a patient arrives with a knife, a nail, a piece of rebar, or any other object protruding from the skull, you do not touch it. You stabilize it, you immobilize it so it does not move during transport, and you take that patient to the operating room. The reason is simple. That object may be tamponading a dural venous sinus or a major cerebral artery. If you pull it out in the emergency department without vascular control, the patient exsanguinates and dies on your stretcher. Removal happens in the operating room with a craniotomy planned around the object, with proximal and distal vascular control available if the trajectory is near a major vessel, and with blood products in the room ready to transfuse.

Let's talk about imaging in detail. Every penetrating brain injury gets a CT head with bone windows and 3D reconstruction. The bone windows and 3D reconstruction allow you to map the trajectory, identify the fracture pattern, and locate the distribution of bone and metallic fragments. CT angiography is mandatory if the trajectory passes near any major vessel, and by that I mean the internal carotid artery, the middle cerebral artery, the anterior cerebral artery, or a dural venous sinus. If CTA shows a vascular injury, or if the trajectory crosses the sylvian fissure, crosses midline, passes near the skull base or cavernous sinus, or if there is a pattern of subarachnoid hemorrhage that is unexplained by the parenchymal injury alone, you proceed to formal catheter angiography, a DSA. And this is a point the examiners will push on. You must repeat the DSA at 2 to 3 weeks after injury. Traumatic pseudoaneurysms develop in 3 to 5 percent of penetrating brain injuries, and they present with delayed hemorrhage that can be fatal. If you do not screen for them, you will miss them, and your patient will bleed weeks after you thought the acute management was done.

Now surgical management. For this patient with a GCS of 9, left frontal trajectory, bullet lodged in the left basal ganglia, contusion without significant midline shift, and no vascular injury on CTA, the surgical indications are debridement of the contaminated entry wound and watertight dural closure. You are not going after that bullet in the basal ganglia. Let me be very explicit about the surgical approach. You perform a formal craniotomy centered on the entry wound. You debride the scalp by excising 1 to 2 millimeters of wound margin to remove contaminated tissue. You elevate the bone flap and identify the dural entry point. You remove accessible bone fragments that have been driven into the superficial brain cortex. You irrigate gently and thoroughly. You remove necrotic brain tissue at the surface of the wound. But you do not follow the wound tract deep into the brain parenchyma to retrieve deep fragments. That is a cardinal rule. Chasing fragments deep into the brain causes more damage than leaving them in place. Deep bullet fragments and bone fragments become encapsulated by glial scar tissue over time. Lead fragments rarely cause systemic toxicity unless they are sitting in cerebrospinal fluid or within a ventricle, where the slightly acidic pH of CSF can leach lead over time. You achieve hemostasis at the wound surface, repair the dura with a watertight closure using a pericranial graft or a dural substitute if native dura is insufficient, and close the wound in layers.

The examiners will want to know what you would not do surgically. You do not pursue deep bullet fragments. You do not follow the wound tract aggressively into eloquent brain tissue. You do not attempt to remove fragments from the ventricles unless they are causing obstructive hydrocephalus.

They will also ask when you would not operate at all. A patient with a GCS of 3, bilateral fixed and dilated pupils, and a devastating bihemispheric or transventricular trajectory has a mortality approaching 100 percent. In this situation, operating does not change the outcome. You have a direct and honest conversation with the family about prognosis, you discuss goals of care, and you raise the possibility of organ donation.

Let me walk you through the vascular complications because the examiners will probe this area. Traumatic pseudoaneurysm occurs in 3 to 5 percent of cases and presents with delayed hemorrhage days to weeks after the initial injury. This is why CTA or DSA screening at 2 to 3 weeks is recommended for all trajectories near major vessels. Traumatic arteriovenous fistula, specifically a carotid-cavernous fistula, can occur from trajectories through the skull base. The clinical presentation is pulsatile proptosis, chemosis, and an orbital bruit. This is treated with endovascular embolization. Vasospasm can occur as a consequence of traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, just as it does in aneurysmal SAH. Delayed hemorrhage can occur from missed pseudoaneurysm, from progression of contusion, or from coagulopathy.

CSF leak is common when the frontal sinus or skull base is involved. You manage this the same way you manage a CSF leak from a basilar skull fracture. Elevate the head of bed to 30 degrees, instruct the patient to avoid straining, Valsalva, and nose blowing. If the leak persists beyond 7 days, you place a lumbar drain. If it remains refractory to lumbar drainage, you proceed with operative repair.

Now let me prepare you for the trap questions. The examiner says, a patient arrives in your emergency department with a knife embedded in the skull. Would you remove it? Your answer is absolutely not. You do not remove that knife in the emergency department. The blade may be tamponading a dural venous sinus or a branch of the middle meningeal artery or even the internal carotid artery. You stabilize and immobilize the object. You obtain a CT head and a CT angiogram with the object in place. You transport the patient to the operating room. In the OR you prepare for vascular injury. You have blood products in the room, typed and crossmatched. You plan a craniotomy around the object so that you have full visualization. If the trajectory is near a major vessel, you achieve proximal vascular control before you touch the object. You then remove the knife under direct visualization with the ability to immediately address any hemorrhage.

Second trap question. The examiner says, the bullet crossed midline through both frontal lobes and the patient has a GCS of 5. Would you operate? A bihemispheric trajectory is a devastating injury with very high mortality. A GCS of 5 with this trajectory carries a dismal prognosis. However, and this is important, if there is an evacuable hematoma causing mass effect, a limited surgical intervention for hematoma evacuation and wound closure may be reasonable. What you do not do is aggressively pursue the bullet across midline through both hemispheres. That operation will cause more destruction than it prevents. This clinical scenario fundamentally becomes a goals-of-care conversation with the family, informed by the imaging findings and the neurological examination.

Third trap question. The examiner asks, would you give prophylactic antibiotics for a closed skull fracture without penetrating injury? The answer is no. Antibiotics are indicated for penetrating injuries and open skull fractures because these are contaminated wounds. There is no evidence supporting prophylactic antibiotics for closed skull fractures. Cochrane reviews have also demonstrated no benefit for prophylactic antibiotics in closed basilar skull fractures, even those with CSF leak. Do not confuse the two scenarios.

Now the long-term complications you need to have in your head for the follow-up questions. Post-traumatic epilepsy occurs in 30 to 50 percent of penetrating brain injuries, which is the highest rate of any TBI mechanism and far exceeds the rate seen in closed head injury. CSF leak can present in a delayed fashion. Meningitis and brain abscess can occur weeks to months later, which is why the initial debridement and antibiotic coverage are so critical. Pseudoaneurysm with delayed hemorrhage, as we discussed, can present weeks after injury. Lead toxicity from retained fragments is rare and essentially only occurs when bullet fragments are bathed in CSF within the ventricles. Hydrocephalus can develop from intraventricular hemorrhage or from disruption of CSF pathways. And neuropsychiatric sequelae are common and often severe given the typical frontal and temporal lobe involvement.

Let's review the evidence base briefly. The Brain Trauma Foundation has published guidelines specifically for penetrating traumatic brain injury, with Aarabi as a principal author, and these form the foundation for management recommendations including antibiotic and seizure prophylaxis protocols. Military guidelines for penetrating TBI from the Department of Defense address the high-velocity injury patterns seen in combat. The prognostic data from Kaufman and Levy established the relationship between GCS, trajectory, and survival that forms the basis for operative decision-making. Amirjamshidi published the largest series on stab wounds to the brain, which is a distinct entity from gunshot wounds with generally better outcomes due to the absence of cavitation. And vascular injury screening protocols have been developed and refined based on retrospective series demonstrating the incidence of pseudoaneurysm and the risk of fatal delayed hemorrhage without screening.

Let me close with the board pearls you need to have on the tip of your tongue. First, never remove embedded objects in the emergency department. Take the patient to the OR with the object stabilized and immobilized. Second, do not chase deep fragments. Superficial debridement yes, deep retrieval no. The fragments encapsulate and the retrieval attempt causes more injury than the fragments themselves. Third, CT angiography is mandatory for any trajectory near a major vessel. Fourth, screen for traumatic pseudoaneurysm with repeat CTA or DSA at 2 to 3 weeks. Pseudoaneurysms develop in 3 to 5 percent and present with delayed fatal hemorrhage. Fifth, post-traumatic epilepsy occurs in 30 to 50 percent of penetrating brain injuries, the highest rate of any TBI mechanism. Prophylactic antiepileptics prevent early seizures for 7 days but do not prevent late epilepsy. Sixth, achieve watertight dural closure. Use a pericranial graft or dural substitute if needed. Seventh, GCS is the single strongest prognostic factor. GCS 3 to 5 with bilateral fixed dilated pupils and bihemispheric trajectory means near 100 percent mortality. Have the goals-of-care conversation early.

That covers penetrating brain injury. You're ready for this one.
