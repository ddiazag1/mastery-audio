  - q: You see a hypodense area within a hyperdense acute extra-axial collection. Name it and act.
    a: The swirl sign: unretracted, unclotted, fresh liquid blood, meaning active bleeding at the moment of the scan. It predicts rapid expansion and worse outcome. I would take this patient to theatre now rather than observe.
    probe: What is the CT angiography equivalent in an intraparenchymal bleed?
  - q: Describe the empty delta sign and its management implication.
    a: A filling defect within the superior sagittal sinus on post-contrast imaging, where enhancing dura outlines non-enhancing central thrombus. It means dural venous sinus thrombosis, and the treatment is anticoagulation even in the presence of venous haemorrhagic infarction.
    probe: What does the same thrombus look like before contrast?
  - q: A mass has tapering dural enhancement extending from its margin. How confident are you?
    a: That is the dural tail, classic for meningioma and present in a majority, but not pathognomonic. It also occurs with dural metastases, lymphoma and inflammatory disease. I would say classic for but not specific to meningioma.
    probe: What other features would push you toward meningioma?
  - q: How do you decide whether a mass is extra-axial before you offer any differential?
    a: Look for a cerebrospinal fluid cleft, a rim of displaced cortical vessels, buckling of the grey white junction away from the lesion, a broad dural base, and bone reaction such as hyperostosis. That call is the first fork in every tumour differential.
    probe: Why does getting this wrong cost you the whole case?
  - q: Butterfly lesion crossing the corpus callosum. Differential and discriminators.
    a: Glioblastoma, primary CNS lymphoma, and tumefactive demyelination. Lymphoma in the immunocompetent enhances homogeneously, is T2 dark from hypercellularity, restricts diffusion, and melts with steroids, which is exactly why you do not give steroids before biopsy.
    probe: What does the demyelinating lesion look like instead?
  - q: Periventricular hypodensity around dilated ventricles. What is it and what does it change?
    a: Transependymal flow: cerebrospinal fluid forced across the ependyma under pressure. It makes the hydrocephalus acute or decompensated rather than chronic, and argues for urgent diversion.
    probe: What is your most sensitive early sign of hydrocephalus?
  - q: Sagging brainstem, effaced prepontine cistern, diffuse smooth pachymeningeal enhancement. What do you do?
    a: This is intracranial hypotension, not hypertension. The treatment is an epidural blood patch or repair of the leak. Reaching for a shunt here is the trap, because the problem is too little cerebrospinal fluid, not too much.
    probe: Which patients get this after surgery?
  - q: Two adjacent vertebral bodies destroyed with loss of the intervening disc. Infection or tumour?
    a: Infection. Pyogenic organisms have the proteolytic enzymes to destroy disc, whereas metastatic tumour characteristically spares it. Tuberculosis is the classic exception, relatively sparing disc with large paraspinal abscesses and subligamentous spread.
    probe: What would make you say tuberculosis rather than a pyogenic organism?

  - label: Sign to decision
    stem: A 39-year-old woman, six days postpartum, presents with a week of worsening headache, a seizure this morning, and mild left arm weakness. Non-contrast CT shows a right parasagittal area of mixed density and a hyperattenuating superior sagittal sinus.
    q1: What are you looking at, and what single further study do you want?
    a1: The hyperattenuating sinus is the dense triangle or cord sign of acute sinus thrombosis, and the parenchymal abnormality is likely venous haemorrhagic infarction, which does not respect an arterial territory. I want CT or MR venography, and would expect the empty delta sign on post-contrast imaging.
    q2: Venography confirms extensive superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with cortical venous reflux. She has a parenchymal haemorrhage. Do you anticoagulate?
    a2: Yes. Anticoagulation is indicated in cerebral venous sinus thrombosis even in the presence of venous haemorrhage, because the haemorrhage is a consequence of venous outflow obstruction and treating the thrombus treats the cause. I would use heparin and involve haematology given the peripartum state.
    q3: Over twelve hours she becomes drowsy with a fixed dilated right pupil. Now what?
    a3: This is malignant venous infarction with herniation. Secure the airway, hyperosmolar therapy, and proceed to decompressive craniectomy. Decompression for venous infarction has better outcomes than for arterial malignant middle cerebral artery infarction because the parenchyma is often salvageable once pressure is relieved.
    q4: She survives. What do you tell her about future pregnancies?
    a4: She needs a thrombophilia workup and haematology input. Prior cerebral venous thrombosis is not an absolute contraindication to further pregnancy, but it requires planned prophylactic anticoagulation through pregnancy and the puerperium, and I would not give a definitive answer without that workup.

Let's master the classic imaging signs of neurosurgery for the oral boards.

Named signs are examiner currency. They are compact, unambiguous, and they let you demonstrate in four words that you have recognized a pattern which would otherwise take a paragraph to describe. More importantly, most of them carry an immediate management implication, which is what the examiner is really after. Let's work through the ones that actually appear in the examination room, organized by the problem they solve.

Start with trauma and hemorrhage.

The swirl sign describes an area of low attenuation within an otherwise hyperdense acute hematoma on non-contrast CT. That hypodense swirl is unretracted, unclotted, fresh liquid blood, which means the patient is actively bleeding at the moment the scan was taken. This is not a subtle academic point. The swirl sign in an epidural or a subdural hematoma predicts rapid expansion, worse outcome, and it should push you toward emergent surgical evacuation rather than observation. If you see it and say it, follow immediately with your decision to operate.

[[fig:1]]

The spot sign is the analogous finding on CT angiography: a focus of contrast extravasation within an acute intraparenchymal hematoma. It indicates ongoing bleeding and predicts hematoma expansion. Its main use is prognostic and triage oriented, and it argues for aggressive blood pressure control and close interval reimaging.

[[fig:2]]

For chronic subdural collections, know the membrane and septation pattern, and know that a hyperdense layer within a hypodense chronic collection indicates acute on chronic rehemorrhage. Layering of blood with a fluid level within a subdural collection should also prompt you to ask about coagulopathy, because a hematocrit level implies the blood has not clotted.

Now the vascular signs.

The hyperdense middle cerebral artery sign is a bright, dense middle cerebral artery on non-contrast CT representing acute intraluminal thrombus. Its focal variant within the Sylvian fissure is the dot sign, indicating a distal branch occlusion. Recognizing it in a stroke case tells you there is a large vessel occlusion and takes you directly to vascular imaging and thrombectomy triage.

The empty delta sign is a filling defect within the superior sagittal sinus on post contrast imaging, where the enhancing dura outlines a non-enhancing central thrombus, producing a triangle with an empty center. This is dural venous sinus thrombosis. The management implication is anticoagulation, even in the presence of venous hemorrhagic infarction, which is a counterintuitive point examiners love to probe. Recognize also that on non-contrast CT the acutely thrombosed sinus may appear hyperdense, called the dense triangle or cord sign.

[[fig:4]]

[[fig:3]]

The puff of smoke appearance, from the Japanese term moyamoya, describes the hazy network of dilated lenticulostriate and thalamoperforator collaterals on angiography that develops in response to progressive supraclinoid internal carotid stenosis. In children this presents with ischemia, in adults more often with hemorrhage from the fragile collaterals. The management answer is surgical revascularization, direct or indirect.

[[fig:20]]

The ivy sign is the corresponding magnetic resonance finding in moyamoya: leptomeningeal hyperintensity following the sulci on fluid attenuated inversion recovery, reflecting slow flow in pial collaterals.

[[fig:5]]

For aneurysmal disease, know the distribution of blood as a localizing sign. Blood predominantly in the anterior interhemispheric fissure suggests an anterior communicating artery aneurysm. Blood in the Sylvian fissure suggests a middle cerebral artery bifurcation aneurysm. Blood in the perimesencephalic cisterns with a normal angiogram suggests benign perimesencephalic nonaneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, which has an excellent prognosis and does not require repeat angiography in most protocols, though many centers still obtain one repeat study. Isolated fourth ventricular or posterior fossa blood suggests a posterior inferior cerebellar artery aneurysm.

Now the tumor signs, which carry the heaviest examination weight.

The dural tail is a tapering enhancement of the dura extending away from an extra-axial mass. It is classically associated with meningioma and is present in a majority of cases, but it is not pathognomonic and can occur with dural metastases, lymphoma, and inflammatory disease. Say classic for but not specific to meningioma, because that nuance is the distinction between a good and an excellent answer.

[[fig:6]]

The cerebrospinal fluid cleft sign and the presence of a rim of displaced vessels indicate that a mass is extra-axial rather than intra-axial. Other extra-axial indicators include buckling of the grey white junction away from the mass, broad dural base, and bone reaction such as hyperostosis. Determining intra-axial versus extra-axial is the first fork in every tumor differential, so make the call explicitly.

The sunburst or spoke wheel vascular pattern on angiography describes the radial arrangement of feeding vessels in a meningioma, typically supplied by dural branches such as the middle meningeal artery, with a characteristic prolonged vascular blush that persists into the venous phase, sometimes described as the mother in law sign because it comes early and stays late.

The butterfly appearance describes a mass crossing the corpus callosum to involve both hemispheres symmetrically. The differential is short and important: glioblastoma, primary central nervous system lymphoma, and occasionally demyelinating disease such as tumefactive multiple sclerosis. Location plus enhancement pattern plus diffusion will separate them, and lymphoma classically shows homogeneous enhancement with restricted diffusion in an immunocompetent patient, often dramatically steroid responsive, which is why you avoid steroids before biopsy.

[[fig:7]]

For pituitary disease, the snowman or figure of eight appearance describes a macroadenoma that has expanded out of the sella and become waisted at the diaphragma sellae. Know the Knosp grading for cavernous sinus invasion based on the relationship of the tumor to lines drawn through the internal carotid artery.

For posterior fossa pediatric tumors, the toothpaste sign describes ependymoma extruding through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie, a plastic tumor squeezing out of the fourth ventricle, which distinguishes it from the medulloblastoma that fills the fourth ventricle without extruding.

[[fig:8]]

For vestibular schwannoma, the ice cream cone appearance describes the tumor with an intracanalicular component, the cone, and a cerebellopontine angle component, the scoop. Contrast this with meningioma at the same location, which sits broadly on the petrous dura, does not typically expand the internal auditory canal, and shows a dural tail.

[[fig:9]]

Now the hydrocephalus and pressure signs.

Transependymal flow, the periventricular hypodensity on CT or hyperintensity on fluid attenuated inversion recovery, indicates acute or decompensated hydrocephalus with cerebrospinal fluid being forced across the ependyma. Its presence makes the hydrocephalus acute and argues for urgent diversion.

[[fig:11]]

The Mickey Mouse appearance of a dilated third ventricle and the rounding of the frontal horns are supportive signs. The most sensitive early sign of hydrocephalus remains temporal horn dilation.

The hummingbird sign and the morning glory sign describe midbrain atrophy in progressive supranuclear palsy, relevant because normal pressure hydrocephalus mimics and their distinction affects whether you offer a shunt. The disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid space hydrocephalus pattern, with tight high convexity sulci, enlarged Sylvian fissures, and ventriculomegaly, supports a diagnosis of normal pressure hydrocephalus and predicts shunt responsiveness.

[[fig:12]]

The sagging brain appearance, with downward displacement of the brainstem, effacement of the prepontine cistern, diffuse smooth pachymeningeal enhancement, and engorged venous structures, indicates intracranial hypotension. This is critically important because it is the opposite problem from the one you usually treat, and the management is an epidural blood patch or repair of the leak, not diversion. Examiners use this to catch candidates who reflexively reach for a shunt.

[[fig:13]]

Now spine signs.

The empty thecal sac sign describes nerve roots displaced peripherally against the dural margin, seen in arachnoiditis. The inverse, clumping of nerve roots centrally, is also arachnoiditis. Both matter because they predict poor response to further surgery.

[[fig:14]]

The naked facet sign on axial CT indicates facet joint dislocation or subluxation, where the articular surfaces are no longer in apposition. It signals significant ligamentous injury and instability.

[[fig:15]]

The owl eye or snake eye appearance on axial T2 imaging of the cord describes bilateral symmetric anterior horn cell hyperintensity, seen in cord infarct in the anterior spinal artery territory and in some chronic compressive myelopathy.

[[fig:16]]

The pancake like flat enhancement and the absence of disc involvement favor a tumor, whereas involvement of two adjacent vertebral bodies with destruction of the intervening disc strongly favors infection, because pyogenic organisms possess the proteolytic enzymes to destroy disc while metastatic tumor characteristically spares it. Tuberculosis is the classic exception, relatively sparing the disc and producing large paraspinal abscesses with subligamentous spread, sometimes with the gibbus deformity.

[[fig:17]]

Finally, a short list of miscellaneous signs worth having.

The target sign describes a peripheral nerve sheath tumor with central low T2 signal and peripheral high signal, favoring a benign neurofibroma. The split cord and the bony spur of diastematomyelia. The tigroid pattern of metachromatic leukodystrophy. The hot cross bun sign of multiple system atrophy in the pons. The eye of the tiger sign in pantothenate kinase associated neurodegeneration. The cord sign and the dense vessel signs already discussed.

Before adding more signs, fix the habit that makes them useful. A named sign is a compression algorithm: it packages an observation, a pathophysiological claim and a management consequence into a few words. That is why examiners like them and why they are dangerous. The compression only works if the observation is genuinely present, so train yourself to state the finding in plain descriptive language first and only then attach the label. There is a rounded area of low attenuation within the hyperdense extra-axial collection, which is the swirl sign, indicating unclotted actively bleeding blood. The finding is the evidence; the name is the shorthand; the consequence is the answer. Candidates who invert that order, leading with the label, get caught the moment the examiner asks what exactly they are looking at.

Understand too that most of these signs are sensitive or specific but rarely both, and saying so is a strength. The dural tail is present in a majority of meningiomas but also occurs with dural metastases, lymphoma and inflammatory disease. The empty delta sign is quite specific for sinus thrombosis but is present in only a minority of cases and only in a particular time window. The swirl sign is specific for active bleeding but its absence does not reassure. The target sign favours a benign nerve sheath tumour but is neither invariable in neurofibroma nor impossible in a malignant lesion. Offering the sign together with its limitation is what distinguishes a candidate who has read about imaging from one who uses it.

A few more signs are worth carrying, grouped by where they appear. In the sella and suprasellar region, the snowman or figure of eight describes a macroadenoma waisted at the diaphragma sellae as it expands out of the sella, and the Knosp grading describes cavernous sinus invasion by the relationship of tumour to lines drawn through the internal carotid artery. In the pineal region, a mass with a calcification pattern that is engulfed rather than displaced favours a pineal parenchymal tumour over a germ cell tumour. Around the foramen of Monro, a rounded hyperdense lesion on non-contrast computed tomography producing acute obstructive hydrocephalus is a colloid cyst until proven otherwise, and it is the classic cause of sudden deterioration and death from positional obstruction.

In the posterior fossa, remember that the differential of a fourth ventricular mass in a child is driven by its relationship to the floor and the outlets: medulloblastoma arises from the roof and fills the ventricle with restricted diffusion from its high cellularity; ependymoma arises from the floor and extrudes plastically through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie, which is the toothpaste sign; and pilocytic astrocytoma is typically a cerebellar hemispheric cyst with an enhancing mural nodule. That triad, distinguished by origin, diffusion and extrusion, is one of the highest yield discriminations in paediatric neuro-oncology.

In the ventricles and cisterns, the cerebrospinal fluid cleft sign and a rim of displaced cortical vessels establish that a mass is extra-axial rather than intra-axial, and buckling of the grey white junction away from the lesion says the same thing. That determination is the first fork in every tumour differential, so make it explicitly before you offer a diagnosis.

In the spine, add the pancake or flat enhancement of a metastatic deposit that respects the disc, contrasted with the disc destruction of pyogenic infection; the bright vertebra of a haemangioma with its corduroy appearance on sagittal images and polka dot appearance on axial images; and the scalloping of the posterior vertebral bodies seen with dural ectasia in neurofibromatosis type one and in Marfan syndrome. In the craniocervical junction, remember that a pseudomeningocele on myelography indicates root avulsion.

Among the metabolic and degenerative signs, the tigroid or leopard skin pattern of metachromatic leukodystrophy describes perivascular sparing within confluent white matter disease; the hot cross bun sign describes cruciform pontine hyperintensity in multiple system atrophy; and the eye of the tiger sign describes central hyperintensity within a hypointense globus pallidus in pantothenate kinase associated neurodegeneration. These rarely change your operation, but recognising them stops you from operating on something that is not surgical, which is precisely their value.

Finally, know the mimics, because the examiner will sometimes hand you one deliberately. Flow artefact in a dural sinus mimics thrombosis, and the give-away is that it resolves on a different sequence or a delayed acquisition. Arachnoid granulations produce filling defects in the sinuses that are smooth, round and cerebrospinal fluid density, unlike thrombus. Pseudoprogression after chemoradiation mimics tumour growth within the first three to six months and is distinguished by low perfusion and low choline. Post-surgical enhancement along a resection margin mimics residual tumour after about forty eight hours. Radiation necrosis mimics recurrence. And the tumefactive demyelinating plaque with its open ring mimics a high grade glioma. In each case, naming the mimic and saying which study discriminates is the complete answer.

A word on how signs get their names, because it occasionally helps you remember them and it occasionally misleads. Most are culinary or zoological analogies coined by radiologists describing a shape: the hamburger bun, the ice cream cone, the hot cross bun, the hummingbird, the puff of smoke. A few describe a physical process rather than a shape, and those are the more reliable ones because they encode mechanism: the swirl of unclotted blood, the empty delta of an unopacified thrombus, transependymal flow across a pressurised ependyma. When you are under pressure and the name will not come, describe the mechanism instead. The examiner is grading whether you understand what the tissue is doing, not whether you can recall a breakfast pastry.

A closing note on how to deploy these. Never lead with the sign alone. Describe the finding, then name it, then state what it means for management. Saying there is a hypodense area within the hyperdense acute epidural collection, which is the swirl sign, indicating active bleeding, and therefore I would take this patient to the operating room now, is a complete answer. Saying only swirl sign is a party trick. The examiner is grading the chain from observation to decision, and the named sign is only the middle link.

And guard against the reverse error. Do not force a named sign onto a finding that does not fit. If the enhancement is not tapering, it is not a dural tail. Candidates who reach for signs they half remember lose more credibility than candidates who simply describe what they see. Use the ones you know cold, describe the rest, and always close with the management implication.
