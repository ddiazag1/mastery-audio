Let's work through brain metastasis treatment algorithm, one of the highest-yield topics you will face on the oral boards. This comes up constantly because brain metastases are far and away the most common intracranial tumors. They outnumber primary brain tumors by a factor of ten to one. If you remember nothing else from this session, remember that ratio, because it frames everything that follows. Any ring-enhancing lesion you encounter in a patient over fifty should have metastasis at the top of your differential until proven otherwise.

Starting with the foundation. The most common primary site sending metastases to the brain is lung cancer, accounting for forty to fifty percent of all brain metastases. Lung is number one by a wide margin. Breast cancer is second at fifteen to twenty-five percent. Melanoma comes in third at five to twenty percent, and its incidence of brain metastases is climbing as systemic therapies extend survival. Renal cell carcinoma accounts for five to ten percent, and colorectal cancer rounds out the major players at three to five percent. You need these numbers cold. An examiner who asks "what is the most common source of brain metastases" expects you to say lung without hesitation.

Now, where do these metastases land? Eighty percent involve the cerebral hemispheres, and they have a strong predilection for the gray-white matter junction. This is a vascular phenomenon. Tumor emboli traveling through the arterial system lodge where larger caliber cortical vessels branch into smaller medullary arteries, right at that gray-white interface. Fifteen percent of brain metastases occur in the cerebellum, which makes sense given its proportional share of cardiac output relative to brain volume. Only about five percent occur in the brainstem, where they present unique management challenges.

Terminology matters here and the examiners will test you on it. A solitary brain metastasis means there is a single lesion in the brain, but the patient may have systemic disease elsewhere. A single brain metastasis means it is the only known metastatic deposit anywhere in the body, brain or otherwise. Multiple brain metastases means more than one intracranial lesion. These are not interchangeable terms, and confusing them will cost you points.

Certain histologies are notorious for producing hemorrhagic brain metastases. The classic mnemonic is MR CT. M for melanoma, R for renal cell carcinoma, C for choriocarcinoma, and T for thyroid. When you see a hemorrhagic brain lesion in a patient with known cancer, these four should come to mind immediately. Melanoma and renal cell are the two you will encounter most frequently in practice.

Prognostic scoring is essential for treatment planning. The Radiation Therapy Oncology Group developed the recursive partitioning analysis, or RPA, classification system. RPA Class I includes patients under age sixty-five with a Karnofsky Performance Status of seventy or higher, a controlled primary tumor, and no extracranial metastases. These are your best-prognosis patients, with a median survival of 7.1 months. RPA Class III is defined simply by a KPS under seventy, regardless of other factors. These patients have the worst prognosis at a median survival of only 2.3 months. RPA Class II captures everyone else, with a median survival of 4.2 months. Know these numbers. They come up on the boards and they guide your treatment intensity. The Graded Prognostic Assessment, or GPA, is a more modern scoring system that is diagnosis-specific, meaning the prognostic factors are weighted differently depending on whether you are dealing with lung cancer, breast cancer, or melanoma. The molecular GPA goes a step further by incorporating tumor-specific molecular markers such as EGFR mutation status, ALK rearrangement, HER2 amplification, and BRAF mutation. These molecular markers dramatically affect prognosis and treatment selection, and the molecular GPA reflects that reality.

Now let's move to the clinical case. You are presented with a sixty-two-year-old patient with known non-small cell lung cancer. The tumor is EGFR-mutant and has been well controlled on osimertinib. The patient presents with one week of progressive left arm weakness. MRI of the brain with contrast reveals a single 3.5 centimeter right frontal ring-enhancing mass with significant surrounding vasogenic edema and six millimeters of midline shift. There are no other brain lesions. Staging body imaging shows a stable lung primary with no other systemic metastases. So you have a patient with a single, large, symptomatic brain metastasis from a molecular-defined NSCLC, on targeted therapy, with good performance status.

This is where the examiner interrogation begins, and this is where you earn or lose your points.

First question will be about initial management. Start with dexamethasone. The dose for symptomatic vasogenic edema with mass effect is four to ten milligrams every six hours. Some protocols use a loading dose of ten milligrams intravenously followed by four milligrams every six hours. The steroid effect on edema is dramatic and often produces clinical improvement within hours. Regarding seizure prophylaxis, if this patient has had a seizure, start levetiracetam. If the patient has not had a seizure, do not start prophylactic anticonvulsants. This is the same American Academy of Neurology guideline that applies to primary brain tumors. There is no evidence that prophylactic antiepileptic drugs reduce seizure incidence in patients with brain tumors who have not yet seized. The examiner may push you on this. Hold your ground. Do not prophylax.

Now the big question: what is your treatment plan for this lesion? This is a single, large, 3.5 centimeter, symptomatic brain metastasis causing neurological deficit with mass effect. The answer is surgical resection followed by postoperative stereotactic radiosurgery to the resection cavity. This is the current standard of care for large, accessible, symptomatic single brain metastases. Let me walk you through the evidence that built this algorithm.

The landmark trial is Patchell 1990, published in the New England Journal of Medicine. This randomized controlled trial compared surgery plus whole-brain radiation therapy versus whole-brain radiation therapy alone for patients with a single brain metastasis. The surgery group had dramatically better outcomes: median survival of forty weeks versus fifteen weeks, and significantly longer maintenance of functional independence. This trial established that surgery provides a survival benefit for single brain metastases in patients with good performance status. It is one of the most cited trials in neuro-oncology and you must know it.

Then came Patchell 1998, which asked the next logical question: after you resect a single brain metastasis, do you need to add whole-brain radiation? This trial randomized patients who had undergone complete resection of a single brain metastasis to postoperative WBRT versus observation. WBRT reduced the rate of recurrence both at the surgical site and elsewhere in the brain, but it did not improve overall survival. This is a critical nuance. WBRT after surgery reduced recurrence but did not make patients live longer. That set the stage for the question of whether there was a way to reduce local recurrence without subjecting patients to the cognitive toxicity of whole-brain radiation.

The answer came from two pivotal trials. Mahajan 2017, published in The Lancet Oncology, randomized patients who had undergone resection of brain metastases to postoperative stereotactic radiosurgery to the cavity versus observation. Postoperative SRS significantly reduced local recurrence compared to observation alone. This established that treating the resection cavity with focused radiation is beneficial.

Then Brown 2017, the Alliance trial published in JAMA, compared postoperative SRS to the cavity versus postoperative WBRT. This is the practice-changing study. Overall survival was equivalent between the two groups. But here is the key finding: patients who received SRS had significantly better cognitive preservation compared to those who received WBRT. Cognitive function declined less and later in the SRS group. This trial changed the standard of care. Postoperative SRS to the resection cavity replaced postoperative WBRT for patients with limited brain metastases. If the examiner asks you whether you would give WBRT after resection of a single brain metastasis, the answer is no. Postoperative cavity SRS is the standard. Brown 2017 showed equivalent survival with preserved neurocognition. That is a trap question and you need to answer it confidently.

Now let's talk about when SRS alone, without surgery, is appropriate. Stereotactic radiosurgery as a standalone treatment is indicated for lesions three centimeters or smaller, though some centers are treating lesions up to four centimeters. It is appropriate for a limited number of metastases, traditionally one to four, although more recent data supports treating up to ten or even fifteen metastases with SRS if the total tumor volume is manageable. SRS alone is also preferred when lesions are in deep or eloquent locations where surgery carries unacceptable risk, and in patients with good KPS. The dosing follows the RTOG 90-05 dose de-escalation paradigm. For tumors two centimeters or smaller, the dose is twenty to twenty-four Gray in a single fraction. For tumors 2.1 to three centimeters, the dose drops to eighteen Gray. For tumors 3.1 to four centimeters, the dose is fifteen Gray. These numbers are high-yield board material. The dose de-escalation exists because radiation necrosis risk increases with both dose and treatment volume.

What about whole-brain radiation therapy? Its role has declined substantially but it is not dead. WBRT remains appropriate for patients with a large number of brain metastases, generally more than ten to fifteen, where treating each lesion individually with SRS becomes impractical. It is appropriate for patients with poor KPS where the goal is palliation rather than durable control. And it has a role in leptomeningeal disease. The standard dose is thirty Gray in ten fractions, or alternatively 37.5 Gray in fifteen fractions. The major limitation of WBRT is cognitive toxicity. The NRG CC001 trial demonstrated that hippocampal avoidance WBRT combined with memantine significantly reduced cognitive decline compared to standard WBRT. If a patient needs WBRT, hippocampal avoidance plus memantine is the preferred approach based on this trial.

The examiner will almost certainly ask about immunotherapy and targeted therapy. This is a rapidly evolving area and you need to demonstrate current knowledge. For melanoma brain metastases, checkpoint inhibitors have shown remarkable intracranial activity. The CheckMate 204 trial evaluated the combination of ipilimumab and nivolumab for melanoma brain metastases and demonstrated a fifty-five percent intracranial response rate. That is a striking number for a disease that was historically considered a death sentence when it reached the brain. Single-agent pembrolizumab and nivolumab also show intracranial activity, though at lower response rates of roughly twenty to twenty-five percent.

For EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer, and this is directly relevant to our case patient, osimertinib is a third-generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor that crosses the blood-brain barrier effectively. The FLAURA trial demonstrated a twenty-one percent intracranial objective response rate with osimertinib. For ALK-rearranged NSCLC, lorlatinib is the preferred agent given its excellent CNS penetration. For HER2-positive breast cancer, the HER2CLIMB trial showed that the combination of tucatinib plus trastuzumab has meaningful intracranial activity. For BRAF-mutant melanoma, the combination of dabrafenib plus trametinib provides intracranial responses. The key teaching point is that these systemic therapies are increasingly being used upfront, before or alongside stereotactic radiosurgery, rather than as salvage after radiation failure. The treatment algorithm for brain metastases is no longer purely a surgical and radiation oncology discussion. Medical oncology is now a critical partner.

Let's address radioresistant histologies, because this is another common examiner angle. Melanoma and renal cell carcinoma are historically considered resistant to conventional fractionated radiation therapy. However, and this is an important distinction, they respond well to stereotactic radiosurgery. The high single-fraction dose delivered by SRS overcomes the radioresistance that limits conventional fractionated radiation. This is thought to relate to the different mechanisms of cell kill at high single-fraction doses, including vascular endothelial damage and ceramide-mediated apoptosis, rather than relying solely on DNA double-strand breaks. For large radioresistant metastases, surgical resection is also preferred, followed by postoperative SRS.

Now let me walk you through the trap questions that commonly appear. First trap: "The patient has twelve small brain metastases. Would you treat all of them with SRS?" This is an evolving question with no absolute right answer, and the examiner wants to see that you understand the nuance. Traditionally, WBRT was standard for more than three to four brain metastases. However, multiple studies now support SRS for up to ten to fifteen metastases, provided the total tumor volume is manageable. The key variables are total tumor volume, the distribution of lesions, and patient preference. Hippocampal avoidance WBRT plus memantine is a reasonable alternative. The best answer demonstrates that you are aware of the shifting paradigm, that you consider total volume rather than just lesion count, and that you engage in shared decision-making with the patient and radiation oncology.

Second trap: "The MRI shows leptomeningeal enhancement. What now?" This changes everything. Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis carries a grim prognosis, with median survival of six to eight weeks if untreated. Treatment options include intrathecal chemotherapy delivered via an Ommaya reservoir, WBRT for nodular leptomeningeal disease, and targeted or immunotherapy if the tumor molecular profile allows it. The critical point is that leptomeningeal disease shifts the entire treatment paradigm away from focal therapies like surgery and SRS toward systemic or intrathecal approaches.

Third scenario the examiner may raise: the unknown primary. If a brain metastasis is the presenting lesion and there is no known cancer history, you need tissue. The workup includes CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, and PET-CT to search for a primary. But if no primary is identified, surgical resection serves the dual purpose of treating the symptomatic mass and providing tissue for histological and molecular diagnosis. Never assume a diagnosis without histology. The examiner will not accept "it is probably lung" as an answer. Get the tissue.

Complications you must be prepared to discuss include radiation necrosis, which is the major delayed complication of SRS. It occurs in five to twenty-five percent of treated lesions depending on dose and volume, and it typically presents months after treatment. The challenge is that radiation necrosis mimics tumor recurrence on standard MRI. Advanced imaging techniques help distinguish them: perfusion MRI shows decreased cerebral blood volume in necrosis versus increased in recurrence. MR spectroscopy shows elevated lipid and lactate peaks in necrosis. PET imaging shows decreased metabolic activity in necrosis versus increased in recurrence. Treatment of radiation necrosis is dexamethasone as first-line, bevacizumab for steroid-refractory cases, which works by reducing VEGF-mediated permeability, and surgery for refractory cases where the mass effect is unmanageable. Surgical complications include hemorrhage, wound infection, and new neurological deficit, all of which should be discussed during informed consent.

Let me now consolidate the evidence. The trials you must know by name and result for the oral boards are as follows. Patchell 1990: surgery plus WBRT versus WBRT alone for single brain metastasis. Surgery improved median survival from fifteen to forty weeks and improved functional independence. Patchell 1998: postoperative WBRT versus observation after resection. WBRT reduced recurrence but did not improve survival. Andrews and the RTOG 9508 trial: WBRT plus SRS boost versus WBRT alone. The SRS boost improved survival specifically for patients with a single brain metastasis. Mahajan 2017: postoperative SRS to the cavity versus observation. SRS reduced local recurrence. Brown 2017: postoperative SRS versus postoperative WBRT. Equivalent survival but SRS preserved neurocognition. This is the practice-changing trial. NRG CC001: hippocampal avoidance WBRT plus memantine versus standard WBRT. HA-WBRT with memantine reduced cognitive decline. And CheckMate 204: ipilimumab plus nivolumab for melanoma brain metastases, showing a fifty-five percent intracranial response rate.

Closing with the board pearls you should have at instant recall. Know the Patchell trials by number and result. 1990 is the surgery trial showing survival benefit. 1998 is the adjuvant WBRT trial showing reduced recurrence but no survival benefit. SRS dosing follows size: twenty to twenty-four Gray for two centimeters or less, eighteen Gray for 2.1 to three centimeters, fifteen Gray for 3.1 to four centimeters. Postoperative SRS to the resection cavity has replaced WBRT after surgery for limited brain metastases, based on Brown 2017. Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors has fundamentally changed the management of melanoma brain metastases, with combination ipilimumab and nivolumab producing fifty-five percent intracranial response rates. Targeted therapies that cross the blood-brain barrier, including osimertinib for EGFR-mutant NSCLC and lorlatinib for ALK-positive NSCLC, are shifting the treatment paradigm toward earlier systemic therapy. Radioresistant histologies like melanoma and renal cell respond poorly to conventional fractionated radiation but respond well to SRS. Hemorrhagic metastases follow MR CT: melanoma, renal cell, choriocarcinoma, thyroid. Brain metastases are ten times more common than primary brain tumors. And the gray-white junction predilection reflects hematogenous lodging in small-caliber vessels at the cortical-medullary transition.

That covers brain metastasis treatment algorithm. You are ready for this one.
