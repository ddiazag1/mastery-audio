questions:
  - q: Define drug resistance and justify the number.
    a: Failure of two adequate trials of appropriately chosen, tolerated antiseizure medications. The justification is probabilistic: about half achieve freedom on the first drug, roughly another ten percent on the second, and the yield of each subsequent drug falls into the low single digits. Cycling further is a low-yield strategy.
    probe: So what should happen at that point?
    probeA: Referral to a comprehensive epilepsy surgery centre for presurgical evaluation, not a third drug. That is the whole point of the definition: it identifies the moment at which the probability of success from further medication falls below the probability of benefit from evaluation. In a child it is more urgent still, because ongoing seizures cost development that is not recoverable later. In practice this is where the system fails, because the definition is met years before the referral is made.
  - q: Why operate early in a child rather than as a last resort?
    a: Uncontrolled seizures in a developing brain cause developmental arrest and regression, plus injury risk, sudden unexpected death, medication effects on cognition, and educational harm. Meanwhile the young brain reorganises, so function can transfer in ways impossible in an adult. Both arguments point the same way: earlier.
    probe: What does the historical referral delay cost these children?
    probeA: Developmental time that is not recoverable. The historical median delay from meeting the definition of drug resistance to surgery has been many years, often quoted around fifteen to twenty in adult series and still measured in years in children. In a developing brain that means ongoing epileptic encephalopathy, developmental arrest or regression, cumulative medication effects on cognition, lost schooling and social development, accumulating injury risk, and exposure to the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy throughout. It also closes the window in which plasticity would have allowed function such as language to transfer to the other hemisphere. The delay is itself the harm.
  - q: Read me a semiology and localise it.
    a: Epigastric rising sensation, fear, déjà vu, oroalimentary automatisms and contralateral dystonic posturing means mesial temporal. Hypermotor bicycling with nocturnal clustering and brief duration means frontal. Elementary visual phenomena and forced eye deviation means occipital. Painful somatosensory aura with hypersalivation means insular or opercular.
    probe: Why does the insula matter for temporal lobectomy failures?
    probeA: Because the insula is a common site of seizure onset that propagates rapidly to the temporal lobe, so it mimics temporal lobe epilepsy on scalp EEG and on semiology. A patient with insular onset who has a standard anterior temporal lobectomy will often fail, because the generator was never resected. That is one of the main reasons for persistent seizures after an apparently well-selected temporal lobectomy, and it is a major argument for stereo-EEG, which can sample the insula safely in three dimensions whereas subdural grids cannot reach it.
  - q: Stereo-EEG or subdural grids?
    a: Stereo-EEG samples deep and sulcal structures in three dimensions, allows bilateral exploration, has a lower complication profile with about one percent haemorrhage risk, and permits therapeutic thermocoagulation. Grids give contiguous cortical surface coverage and extraoperative stimulation mapping, at the cost of a craniotomy and higher infection and haemorrhage rates.
    probe: What does stereo-EEG require of you before you implant?
    probeA: A specific, testable anatomo-electro-clinical hypothesis before a single electrode is placed. Stereo-EEG samples only where you put it, so unlike a grid it cannot find something you did not suspect. That means I must define, from semiology, scalp video-EEG, MRI, PET and any other functional data, where I think the seizure begins, where it propagates, and which competing hypotheses I need to exclude, and then design an implantation plan that can confirm or refute each one. I also need vascular imaging co-registered for trajectory planning to avoid vessels. Implanting without a hypothesis produces an uninterpretable study.
  - q: Focal cortical dysplasia: classification and imaging.
    a: Blümcke type one is abnormal lamination, type two has dysmorphic neurons subdivided by balloon cells into A and B, type three occurs with another lesion. On MRI look for cortical thickening, grey-white blurring, abnormal T2 and FLAIR signal, abnormal sulcal pattern, and the transmantle sign characteristic of type two B.
    probe: MRI is negative. Does that exclude it?
    probeA: No. MRI-negative focal epilepsy is common and eminently operable; roughly a quarter to a third of focal cortical dysplasia is not visible on standard imaging, and type I dysplasia in particular is frequently occult. What it does is change the pathway: I would first re-image with a dedicated epilepsy protocol at three tesla with thin volumetric sequences reviewed by a neuroradiologist who knows the electroclinical hypothesis, since a substantial proportion of so-called negative scans become positive on targeted re-review. Then I would add functional data with FDG-PET for interictal hypometabolism, ictal SPECT with SISCOM, and magnetoencephalography, and I would have a low threshold for stereo-EEG. Outcomes are somewhat lower than in lesional cases but still good when the data are concordant.
  - q: Which paediatric epilepsies have a specific non-surgical treatment you must not miss?
    a: Glucose transporter type one deficiency responds to the ketogenic diet specifically. Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy responds to pyridoxine. Infantile spasms in tuberous sclerosis respond to vigabatrin. Autoimmune encephalitis responds to immunotherapy. And Dravet syndrome is worsened by sodium channel blockers, which is the classic trap.
    probe: Which drugs specifically must you avoid in Dravet?
    probeA: Sodium channel blockers, because Dravet syndrome is caused by a loss-of-function SCN1A mutation affecting inhibitory interneurons, so blocking sodium channels further impairs inhibition and worsens seizures. Specifically: carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, and lamotrigine, with vigabatrin also generally avoided. The agents that help are valproate and clobazam as first line, with stiripentol, fenfluramine and cannabidiol as add-ons, and the ketogenic diet. Giving carbamazepine to a child with prolonged febrile hemiclonic seizures in the first year of life is the classic examined error.
  - q: Counsel a family about hemispherotomy.
    a: Seizure freedom is high, commonly seventy to eighty percent, best in acquired unilateral pathology such as perinatal infarction and Rasmussen. The child will have a permanent contralateral homonymous hemianopia and lose fine motor control of the contralateral hand, but usually retains or achieves ambulation with a hemiparetic gait, and if operated young can transfer language. It is often a net functional gain because the seizures were preventing development.
    probe: Which pathology gives the least good result and why?
    probeA: Hemimegalencephaly, and diffuse malformations of cortical development generally. Seizure freedom is meaningfully lower than in acquired unilateral pathology such as perinatal middle cerebral artery infarction or Rasmussen encephalitis, because the contralateral hemisphere is frequently abnormal too, even when it looks normal on MRI, so the disconnection does not remove the whole epileptogenic substrate. These children are also the youngest and smallest at operation, with the highest anaesthetic and haemorrhagic risk. I would quote the figure honestly rather than the best-case number, while still recommending surgery, because the alternative is an untreated epileptic encephalopathy.
  - q: What are you most worried about anaesthetically in an infant hemispherotomy?
    a: Blood loss. Circulating volume may be under a litre, so this is a major haemorrhagic undertaking requiring cross-matched blood in theatre, tranexamic acid, cell salvage where feasible, active warming and close communication. Then hypothermia, coagulopathy, transfusion hyperkalaemia, and postoperatively aseptic meningitis and hydrocephalus.
    probe: Does that risk justify waiting until the child is older?
    probeA: No, and this is where the two risks have to be weighed explicitly rather than deferred. Waiting does reduce the anaesthetic and haemorrhagic risk, because the child is larger with a greater circulating volume. But waiting also means continued epileptic encephalopathy in the period of most rapid development, so the developmental cost accrues daily and is largely irreversible, and the window for language to transfer to the remaining hemisphere narrows. In a child with a demonstrable unilateral structural lesion and failed medical therapy, the balance favours operating now at an experienced centre with a paediatric neuroanaesthetist, cross-matched blood, tranexamic acid and cell salvage, rather than accepting months of ongoing regression to obtain a marginally safer operation.
cases:
  - label: Catastrophic infantile epilepsy
    stem: A 7-month-old has had clusters of epileptic spasms since four months, with developmental regression. EEG shows hypsarrhythmia with a right hemispheric predominance. MRI shows an enlarged, dysplastic right hemisphere with abnormal gyral pattern and blurred grey-white differentiation. Vigabatrin and hormonal therapy have failed.
    q1: What is the diagnosis and what does the failed medical therapy tell you?
    a1: Hemimegalencephaly presenting as an epileptic encephalopathy with infantile spasms. Failure of hormonal therapy and vigabatrin in a child with a demonstrable unilateral structural lesion means this is a surgical problem, not a medication problem. Continuing to cycle drugs here would waste the developmental window.
    q2: What operation, and why not a lesser resection?
    a2: Hemispherotomy — a functional disconnection dividing the corpus callosum, internal capsule and corona radiata, temporal stem and fornix, through a lateral periinsular or vertical parasagittal approach. A lesser resection fails because the whole hemisphere is dysplastic and epileptogenic. Modern disconnection avoids the superficial cerebral haemosiderosis and late hydrocephalus of anatomical hemispherectomy.
    q3: The parents ask whether it is safe to operate on a seven-month-old. Answer honestly.
    a3: It carries real risk. Her circulating volume may be under a litre, so this is a major haemorrhagic undertaking needing cross-matched blood, tranexamic acid, active warming, and vigilance for coagulopathy and transfusion hyperkalaemia. But the developmental cost of waiting is severe and ongoing, and the balance favours proceeding now at an experienced centre rather than later.
    q4: What outcome do you predict, and what will she definitely lose?
    a4: She will definitely have a permanent left homonymous hemianopia and lose fine motor control of the left hand. She will most likely walk with a hemiparetic gait, and at this age language can develop in the remaining hemisphere. Seizure freedom is somewhat lower in hemimegalencephaly than in acquired unilateral pathology because contralateral abnormality is common, and I would say that plainly rather than quote the best-case figure.

Let's master pediatric epilepsy surgery for the oral boards.

Pediatric epilepsy surgery differs from adult epilepsy surgery in ways the examiner will probe deliberately. The pathologies are different, the operations are larger, the urgency is greater because of developmental consequences, and the plasticity of the developing brain permits resections that would be unthinkable in an adult. Candidates who simply transplant their adult knowledge will miss all of this. Let's build the differences.

Start with the definition that drives everything. Drug resistant epilepsy, as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy, is failure of adequate trials of two appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications to achieve sustained seizure freedom. The reason this matters is probabilistic: roughly half of patients achieve seizure freedom on the first drug, and a further ten percent or so on the second, but the likelihood of success with each subsequent drug falls into the low single digits. So after two failed appropriate drugs, continuing to cycle medications is a low yield strategy, and the patient should be referred for surgical evaluation.

Now the pediatric imperative, and this is the central conceptual point of the topic. Ongoing uncontrolled seizures in a developing brain are not merely episodic events; they cause developmental arrest and regression, a phenomenon sometimes described as epileptic encephalopathy. Children also face risks of injury, of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, of medication side effects on cognition and behavior, and of the psychosocial and educational consequences of uncontrolled epilepsy. Conversely, the young brain has substantial capacity for functional reorganization, so language and motor functions can transfer to the contralateral hemisphere in ways that do not occur in adults.

Put those together and you get the answer the examiner wants: in children, surgery should be considered early rather than as a last resort, because earlier intervention preserves development and exploits plasticity. The historical pattern of an average delay of many years from onset of drug resistance to surgical referral represents a genuine failure of care, and saying so demonstrates understanding.

Now the presurgical evaluation. The goal is to define the epileptogenic zone, the region whose removal is necessary and sufficient for seizure freedom, and to establish that it can be removed safely. You do this by converging multiple lines of evidence, and you should name them as a hypothesis driven convergence rather than a checklist.

Begin with a detailed seizure semiology, which is the clinical expression of the seizure and often the best localizing information available. Video electroencephalographic monitoring captures habitual seizures with simultaneous electrical and clinical data and is essential. High resolution magnetic resonance with an epilepsy protocol, including thin coronal T2 and fluid attenuated inversion recovery sequences angled perpendicular to the hippocampus and a volumetric T1 for post-processing, is the imaging cornerstone. Add positron emission tomography, which shows interictal hypometabolism in the epileptogenic region, and ictal single photon emission computed tomography with subtraction and coregistration to the magnetic resonance, known as SISCOM, which shows ictal hyperperfusion and is particularly valuable in magnetic resonance negative cases. Magnetoencephalography localizes interictal spikes with good spatial resolution and is used increasingly in children. Neuropsychological assessment establishes a baseline and can lateralize function. Functional magnetic resonance and, less often now, the intracarotid amobarbital test address language lateralization, with the caveat that functional imaging in young children is limited by cooperation.

When the noninvasive data are concordant and point to a resectable lesion in non-eloquent territory, proceed to resection. When the data are discordant, when the magnetic resonance is negative, or when the suspected zone abuts eloquent cortex, proceed to invasive monitoring. Subdural grids and strips provide excellent coverage of the cortical surface and permit functional mapping by stimulation, at the cost of a craniotomy and a higher complication rate including infection and subdural hemorrhage. Stereoelectroencephalography places depth electrodes through small twist drill holes to sample deep and sulcal structures in three dimensions, has a lower complication profile, permits bilateral exploration, and has become the dominant approach in most centers, though it samples points rather than surfaces and requires a well formed anatomo-electro-clinical hypothesis to place the electrodes usefully. Articulating that tradeoff, grids for surface coverage and mapping versus stereoelectroencephalography for deep, bilateral, three dimensional sampling, is a strong answer.

Now the pediatric pathologies, which differ markedly from the adult population where mesial temporal sclerosis dominates.

Focal cortical dysplasia is the single most common pathology in pediatric epilepsy surgery. The Blümcke classification divides it into type one with abnormal cortical lamination, type two with dysmorphic neurons, subdivided into type two A without and type two B with balloon cells, and type three occurring in association with another lesion such as hippocampal sclerosis or a tumor. On magnetic resonance, look for cortical thickening, blurring of the grey white junction, abnormal T2 and fluid attenuated inversion recovery signal, an abnormal sulcal pattern, and the transmantle sign, a tapering band of signal abnormality extending from the cortex toward the ventricle, which is characteristic of type two B. Critically, many focal cortical dysplasias are subtle or invisible on standard imaging, so a negative magnetic resonance does not exclude the diagnosis, and post-processing techniques and repeat imaging at higher field strength with an epilepsy protocol frequently reveal a previously missed lesion. The single strongest predictor of seizure freedom is complete resection of the dysplastic tissue, which is why intraoperative electrocorticography and careful margin definition matter.

Low grade epilepsy associated tumors are the next major group, principally ganglioglioma and dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor. These are typically cortically based, often temporal, frequently calcified or cystic, and they present with long standing seizures rather than mass effect. Outcomes after complete resection are excellent, and the key point is that resection should include the epileptogenic cortex around the lesion, not merely the lesion itself, because lesionectomy alone yields inferior seizure outcomes when there is associated dysplasia, which is the type three association.

Tuberous sclerosis complex, from TSC1 or TSC2 mutations, produces cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas. Children often have multiple tubers, and the surgical challenge is identifying which tuber is epileptogenic, which usually requires invasive monitoring. Know that the subependymal giant cell astrocytoma arises near the foramen of Monro and causes obstructive hydrocephalus, and that mTOR inhibitors such as everolimus are effective both for the tumor and increasingly for seizure control, which is an important non-surgical option to name. Vigabatrin is the drug of choice for infantile spasms in tuberous sclerosis.

Hemispheric pathologies form a distinct category: Rasmussen encephalitis, a progressive unilateral inflammatory encephalitis producing intractable focal seizures, epilepsia partialis continua, progressive hemiparesis, and cognitive decline; Sturge Weber syndrome with a leptomeningeal angioma and facial port wine stain in the trigeminal distribution; hemimegalencephaly; and large perinatal infarcts or porencephaly.

For these, the operation is hemispherectomy or, in modern practice, hemispherotomy, a functional disconnection that achieves the same result as anatomic hemispherectomy with far less tissue removal and far lower rates of the historically devastating complication of superficial cerebral hemosiderosis. Seizure freedom rates are high, commonly in the range of seventy to eighty percent. The counseling point that makes this palatable is the plasticity argument: a child undergoing hemispherotomy for a hemisphere that is already dysfunctional will typically retain or develop ambulation with a hemiparetic gait, will lose fine motor control of the contralateral hand and will have a permanent homonymous hemianopia, and, if operated young enough, can develop or transfer language to the remaining hemisphere. The outcome is often a net functional gain, because the seizures themselves were preventing development.

Hypothalamic hamartoma deserves mention for its distinctive gelastic seizures, with laughter without mirth, precocious puberty, and progressive cognitive and behavioral decline. The lesion itself is intrinsically epileptogenic. It is deep and surgically hazardous, and it is now commonly treated with magnetic resonance guided laser interstitial thermal therapy, which has transformed management of this lesion.

Now the palliative procedures, for patients who are not candidates for resection. Corpus callosotomy, typically anterior two thirds initially with possible completion, is most effective for drop attacks or atonic seizures, dramatically reducing injury, and complications include the acute disconnection syndrome and, with complete section, split brain phenomena. Vagus nerve stimulation reduces seizure frequency in roughly half of patients by half or more, but rarely produces seizure freedom, and side effects include hoarseness, cough, and dyspnea with stimulation. Responsive neurostimulation and deep brain stimulation of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus, and increasingly centromedian thalamic stimulation for generalized epilepsies, are options in older children and adolescents. The ketogenic diet remains genuinely effective, particularly in glucose transporter type one deficiency, where it is the specific treatment, and in pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency and certain epileptic encephalopathies, and you should name it as a legitimate alternative rather than an afterthought.

Laser interstitial thermal therapy deserves specific mention for pediatric practice: it is minimally invasive, appropriate for deep and small targets such as hypothalamic hamartoma, periventricular nodular heterotopia, insular lesions, and mesial temporal structures, and it permits shorter hospitalization, with the tradeoff of no tissue for pathology beyond a small biopsy and somewhat lower seizure freedom rates than open resection for some indications.

Now outcomes and counseling. Seizure freedom, generally reported using the Engel or ILAE classification, is achieved in roughly sixty to eighty percent after resection of a well localized lesion, with the best results in lesional temporal cases and in complete resection of focal cortical dysplasia, and poorer results in magnetic resonance negative and extratemporal cases. The dominant predictors of success are a visible lesion on magnetic resonance, concordance of the noninvasive data, and completeness of resection. Counsel families that medications are typically continued initially and weaned slowly over one to two years if seizure freedom persists, that surgery is not a guarantee, and that even palliative seizure reduction can be life changing for a child with drop attacks or status.

Finally, address the infant specifically, because infants with catastrophic epilepsy such as hemimegalencephaly present the hardest version of this problem. Surgery in infancy carries greater anesthetic and blood loss risk, with the circulating blood volume so small that meticulous hemostasis and proactive transfusion planning are essential, and you should say that explicitly. But the developmental cost of waiting is severe. The balance generally favors proceeding at an experienced center.

Learn to read semiology as localising data, because it is often the best information you have and it costs nothing. A focal aware seizure with an epigastric rising sensation, fear, déjà vu, oroalimentary automatisms and contralateral dystonic posturing points to mesial temporal onset. Hypermotor seizures with bizarre bicycling or thrashing movements, prominent nocturnal clustering and brief duration point to frontal onset. Elementary visual phenomena and forced eye deviation point to occipital onset. Painful or unpleasant somatosensory auras and hypersalivation point to insular or opercular onset, which is important because insular seizures frequently masquerade as temporal and account for a proportion of temporal lobectomy failures. Gelastic seizures with mirthless laughter point to a hypothalamic hamartoma. Epileptic spasms in clusters in infancy point to an epileptic encephalopathy. Describing the semiology and then saying where it localises earns credit before any imaging is discussed.

Know the infantile epileptic encephalopathies and their specific treatments. Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, historically West syndrome, presents with clustered spasms, developmental arrest and hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalography; first line treatment is hormonal therapy with adrenocorticotropic hormone or high dose prednisolone, except in tuberous sclerosis complex where vigabatrin is the drug of choice. Refractory spasms with a demonstrable focal lesion are a surgical indication and should not simply be treated medically indefinitely. Dravet syndrome from SCN1A mutations is worsened by sodium channel blockers such as carbamazepine and phenytoin, which is a classic examination trap, and responds better to valproate, clobazam, stiripentol, fenfluramine and cannabidiol. Lennox Gastaut syndrome with tonic seizures, drop attacks and slow spike and wave is the classic indication for corpus callosotomy or for thalamic neuromodulation.

Add the metabolic and inflammatory causes that must not be missed, because they are treatable without surgery. Glucose transporter type one deficiency responds specifically to the ketogenic diet. Pyridoxine dependent epilepsy responds to pyridoxine and is diagnosed by trial. Autoimmune encephalitis, including anti N methyl D aspartate receptor encephalitis, presents with new refractory status epilepticus and psychiatric features and responds to immunotherapy rather than resection. Rasmussen encephalitis is immune mediated but ultimately requires hemispherotomy in most children, with immunotherapy delaying rather than preventing progression. Naming these before recommending an operation demonstrates the right instincts.

Deepen the invasive monitoring discussion. Stereoelectroencephalography places multiple depth electrodes through twist drill holes using frameless or robotic guidance, samples deep structures, sulcal depths, insula and bilateral hemispheres in three dimensions, carries a haemorrhage risk of roughly one percent per patient and a low infection rate, and permits thermocoagulation through the electrodes as a therapeutic bonus. Its limitation is that it samples points, so it can only test a hypothesis you have already formulated; a poorly conceived implantation yields confident but wrong answers. Subdural grids and strips sample contiguous cortical surface, allow extraoperative functional mapping by stimulation over days, and are better when the question is the precise relationship between a lesion and eloquent cortex, but require a craniotomy and carry higher rates of infection, subdural haemorrhage and raised intracranial pressure. Both approaches also allow cortical stimulation to reproduce the habitual aura, which is powerful confirmatory evidence.

Be concrete about the hemispheric operations. Anatomical hemispherectomy removes the hemisphere and historically caused superficial cerebral haemosiderosis and late hydrocephalus from the large residual cavity. Functional hemispherectomy and modern hemispherotomy achieve complete disconnection with minimal resection, dividing the corpus callosum, the internal capsule and corona radiata, the temporal stem and the fornix, through either a lateral periinsular or a vertical parasagittal approach. Seizure freedom is high, commonly quoted at seventy to eighty percent, and best in acquired unilateral pathology such as perinatal infarction and Rasmussen encephalitis, less good in hemimegalencephaly where contralateral abnormality is common. The counselling is specific and must be given plainly: the child will have a permanent contralateral homonymous hemianopia, will lose fine motor control of the contralateral hand while usually retaining or achieving ambulation with a hemiparetic gait, and, if operated young, can transfer or develop language in the remaining hemisphere.

Address the perioperative realities in small children, because the examiner may test whether you understand the anaesthetic stakes. An infant's circulating blood volume may be under a litre, so a hemispherotomy or a large multilobar resection is a major haemorrhagic undertaking requiring cross matched blood in theatre, tranexamic acid, cell salvage where feasible, active warming, and close communication with anaesthesia. Watch for hypothermia, coagulopathy, hyperkalaemia from transfusion, and aseptic meningitis and postoperative hydrocephalus after disconnection procedures. Say these things aloud; they demonstrate that you have actually done paediatric work rather than read about it.

Finish with the postoperative and long term picture. Continue antiseizure medication initially and consider a slow taper after one to two years of seizure freedom, accepting that a proportion will have recurrence on withdrawal. Monitor development and neuropsychology formally, because cognitive and behavioural gains, and occasionally losses, are as important to families as seizure counts. Report outcomes using the Engel or International League Against Epilepsy classification rather than vague terms. Expect seizure freedom in roughly sixty to eighty percent after resection of a well localised lesion, with the strongest predictors being a visible lesion on magnetic resonance, concordance of the noninvasive data, and completeness of resection, and poorer results in magnetic resonance negative and extratemporal cases. And emphasise once more that even palliative seizure reduction transforms life for a child with daily drop attacks.

To summarize. Two failed appropriate drugs defines drug resistance and should trigger referral. In children, operate early, because seizures arrest development and the young brain reorganizes. Converge semiology, video electroencephalography, magnetic resonance, positron emission tomography, ictal single photon emission computed tomography, and magnetoencephalography into a hypothesis, and use stereoelectroencephalography or grids when the data are discordant or eloquent cortex is involved. Focal cortical dysplasia is the commonest pathology and complete resection is the strongest predictor of seizure freedom. Resect the epileptogenic cortex, not just the lesion. Offer hemispherotomy for hemispheric pathology and counsel the plasticity argument honestly. Use callosotomy for drop attacks, vagus nerve stimulation and neuromodulation for non-resectable cases, laser therapy for deep targets, and the ketogenic diet where specifically indicated.
