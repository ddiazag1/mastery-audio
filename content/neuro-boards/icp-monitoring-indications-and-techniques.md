Let's talk about ICP monitoring indications and techniques. This is one of those foundational critical care topics that the board examiners will absolutely test you on, and they will push deep into the technical details, the physiology, the decision-making, and the complications. You need to own this topic completely. Let's build from the ground up.

We start with the Monro-Kellie doctrine. The cranial vault is a fixed, rigid container. Inside it you have three components: brain parenchyma, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood. The total volume of these three components must remain constant. If one compartment expands, another must decrease in volume to compensate, or intracranial pressure rises. Normal intracranial pressure in an adult is 5 to 15 millimeters of mercury. The Brain Trauma Foundation fourth edition guidelines define the treatment threshold as greater than 22 millimeters of mercury, and that number is important to know precisely because examiners will test it. It was previously 20 in older editions, but the fourth edition moved it to 22 based on observational data suggesting worse outcomes above that threshold.

Now let's talk about the ICP waveform, because this is a favorite board question. The normal ICP waveform has three components. P1 is the percussion wave, which corresponds to arterial pulsation transmitted to the choroid plexus. P2 is the tidal wave, which reflects brain compliance. P3 is the dicrotic wave, which corresponds to aortic valve closure. In a normal, compliant brain, P1 is the tallest peak, followed by P2, followed by P3. The critical finding you need to recognize is when P2 exceeds P1. When the tidal wave becomes taller than the percussion wave, that tells you intracranial compliance is decreased. The brain is operating on the steep portion of the pressure-volume curve, and small additional volume increases will produce large pressure increases. This is a warning sign.

Beyond the individual waveform morphology, you need to understand Lundberg waves, which are patterns of ICP oscillation over time. Lundberg A waves, also called plateau waves, are the most important and most dangerous. These are sustained elevations of ICP to 50 to 100 millimeters of mercury lasting 5 to 20 minutes. They indicate that compensatory mechanisms are exhausted. The pathophysiology involves a cycle of cerebral vasodilation in response to decreased cerebral perfusion pressure, which increases cerebral blood volume, which further raises ICP, which further decreases CPP, creating a positive feedback loop. Plateau waves require immediate treatment. Lundberg B waves are rhythmic oscillations occurring at a frequency of 0.5 to 2 per minute, with ICP amplitudes of 20 to 50 millimeters of mercury. They indicate reduced intracranial compliance and may precede the development of A waves. They are a warning sign that the patient is decompensating. Lundberg C waves are small oscillations at a frequency of 4 to 8 per minute, related to respiratory and arterial pressure variations. C waves are generally not clinically significant.

Now let's go through every type of ICP monitor, because you need to define each one with its advantages and disadvantages.

The external ventricular drain, or EVD, is the gold standard for ICP monitoring. This is the answer the examiner wants to hear first. The EVD is placed into the lateral ventricle, typically the frontal horn. Its unique advantage is that it is both diagnostic and therapeutic. It measures ICP directly from the CSF column, and it allows you to drain cerebrospinal fluid to treat elevated intracranial pressure. You can recalibrate the EVD at any time by rezeroing the transducer at the level of the tragus, which approximates the foramen of Monro. You can also sample CSF through the EVD for laboratory analysis, including cell count, glucose, protein, gram stain, and culture. The disadvantages of the EVD are real: procedural hemorrhage occurs in 1 to 2 percent of placements, catheter malposition occurs in 5 to 10 percent, infection leading to ventriculitis occurs in 5 to 15 percent depending on duration and technique, it requires neurosurgical placement, and it may be technically difficult or impossible to place in patients with small or compressed ventricles.

The second type is the intraparenchymal monitor. The common brands you should know are Codman, Camino, and Pressio. These are fiberoptic or strain-gauge catheters placed directly into the brain parenchyma, typically the right frontal lobe. They are easier to place than an EVD, especially when the ventricles are small or compressed. They have a lower infection risk than EVDs. However, intraparenchymal monitors cannot drain CSF, so they are purely diagnostic with no therapeutic capability. They also cannot be recalibrated after placement. This means they are subject to zero drift over time, typically 1 to 3 millimeters of mercury over 5 days. Despite this limitation, they are reliable for trending, and the measurements correlate well with ventricular pressure in most clinical scenarios.

Subdural and epidural monitors exist but are less accurate than either EVDs or intraparenchymal monitors. They are largely of historical interest and are rarely used in modern neurosurgical practice. The epidural monitor in particular tends to overestimate ICP.

Finally, there are noninvasive methods for estimating ICP. Optic nerve sheath diameter measured by bedside ultrasound is the most commonly referenced. A diameter greater than 5 millimeters correlates with an intracranial pressure greater than 20 millimeters of mercury. Transcranial Doppler can provide a pulsatility index that correlates with elevated ICP. These are useful adjuncts, particularly in settings where invasive monitoring is not immediately available, but they are not substitutes for invasive monitoring and should never be relied upon as the sole means of ICP assessment in a patient who meets indications for invasive monitoring.

Now let's move to the clinical case. A 40-year-old man is brought in after a motor vehicle collision. His GCS is 6. He is intubated in the field. CT of the head shows bifrontal contusions with compressed ventricles, diffuse cerebral edema, and effaced basal cisterns. This patient clearly meets criteria for ICP monitoring: GCS of 8 or less with an abnormal CT. The neurosurgeon places a right frontal EVD. The initial ICP reading is 28 millimeters of mercury. The CSF is bloody, which is expected given the bifrontal contusions. This is your setup for the examiner interrogation.

The examiner will start with EVD placement technique, and you need to know this in exact detail. The entry point is Kocher's point, which is located 1 centimeter anterior to the coronal suture and 2 to 3 centimeters lateral to the midline, at the mid-pupillary line. You prep and drape the area sterilely. You create either a twist drill hole or a formal burr hole. You open the dura with a sharp instrument or cautery. You pass the catheter perpendicular to the skull surface. In the coronal plane, you aim toward the medial canthus of the ipsilateral eye. In the sagittal plane, you aim toward the ipsilateral tragus. The target is the ipsilateral frontal horn of the lateral ventricle. The typical depth from the skull surface is 5 to 7 centimeters. CSF return confirms correct placement. You then tunnel the catheter subcutaneously for at least 5 centimeters from the burr hole, which is a critical step for infection prevention. You secure the catheter, connect it to a closed external drainage system, and level the transducer to the tragus. If there is any concern about catheter position, you obtain a CT scan to confirm placement.

The examiner will then ask about EVD management. You set the drain at a height above the tragus that corresponds to your desired ICP threshold, typically 15 to 20 centimeters of water. When ICP exceeds this height, CSF will drain by gravity. There are two fundamental drainage strategies and you need to understand both. Continuous drainage keeps the ICP lower by allowing constant CSF egress, but you cannot measure true ICP while the drain is open because what you are reading is the drainage pressure, not the actual intracranial pressure. Intermittent drainage involves clamping the drain, measuring the true ICP, then opening the drain for a set period when the ICP exceeds your threshold. This gives you accurate ICP readings but requires more frequent nursing assessment. Most modern protocols use a hybrid approach, and you should be comfortable articulating how your institution manages this.

Zeroing and recalibration is a key nursing and physician responsibility. The transducer must be leveled to the tragus at the start of every nursing shift, with any change in patient head position, and whenever the readings are questioned. This is a major advantage of the EVD over the parenchymal monitor. The EVD can be rezeroed at any time, whereas the parenchymal monitor cannot be recalibrated after insertion.

Now the examiner returns to your case. The ICP is 28. What do you do? You begin Tier 1 management immediately, which we cover in detail in the ICP management topic. Specific to EVD management, you drain CSF. However, you must recognize a practical limitation here. This patient has compressed ventricles on CT. With small, slit-like ventricles, CSF output through the EVD may be minimal because there is very little CSF in the ventricular system to drain. In this scenario, a parenchymal monitor may actually be more appropriate for ICP monitoring, and you should discuss this reasoning with the examiner if the clinical scenario leads there.

The examiner will ask about EVD weaning when the patient improves. When ICP has been controlled and the patient's clinical trajectory is improving, you gradually raise the drain height. A typical protocol is to raise from 15 to 20 to 25 centimeters of water over 24 to 48 hours. If ICP remains controlled at each level, you clamp the drain entirely for 24 hours with frequent ICP checks, typically every 1 to 2 hours. If ICP stays below 20 to 22 during the clamping trial, you remove the EVD. If ICP rises during the trial, you lower the drain back down and continue drainage, then reattempt the weaning trial later.

Now we get into ventriculitis and EVD infection, which is one of the highest-yield topics for the oral boards. The incidence is 5 to 15 percent and increases with duration of catheterization. The risk is highest after day 5 to 7. The diagnosis requires you to integrate multiple data points. Positive CSF cultures are the most important and most definitive finding. Rising CSF white blood cell count, particularly above 100 cells, or a rapidly rising trend from baseline, is concerning. Falling CSF glucose below 50 percent of the serum glucose is a classic finding. Rising CSF protein supports the diagnosis. Clinical signs include fever, altered mental status, and meningismus, but you must recognize that in the critically ill, sedated, or intubated patient, these clinical signs are frequently absent or unreliable.

The examiner will almost certainly complicate this by introducing hemorrhage into the picture, because bloody CSF makes interpretation of the cell count much harder. This is where you need to show sophistication. CSF pleocytosis from hemorrhage alone, so-called chemical or aseptic meningitis, will produce an elevated white blood cell count. The key distinctions are the trend and the cell differential. In infection, the CSF white cell count rises progressively, whereas in chemical ventriculitis from blood breakdown, the WBC count typically stabilizes or decreases over time. Bacterial infection produces a neutrophilic predominance, while chemical ventriculitis tends to show a lymphocytic pleocytosis. You should also trend the CSF white-cell-to-red-cell ratio. A rising ratio suggests a disproportionate increase in white cells beyond what would be expected from hemorrhage alone, pointing toward infection.

The most common organisms causing EVD-related ventriculitis are coagulase-negative staphylococci, particularly Staphylococcus epidermidis, followed by Staphylococcus aureus and gram-negative rods including Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella. Empiric treatment is vancomycin plus cefepime or meropenem to cover both gram-positive and gram-negative organisms, including resistant species commonly found in the ICU environment. For resistant gram-positive infections, intrathecal vancomycin at a dose of 10 to 20 milligrams daily administered through the EVD is a critical adjunct. For resistant gram-negative infections, intrathecal gentamicin or amikacin can be administered. Treatment duration is 10 to 21 days depending on the organism and clinical response. EVD exchange, meaning removal of the infected drain and placement of a new one on the contralateral side, is widely practiced. Whether this truly accelerates clearance of infection is debated, but it is common practice and you should be prepared to discuss it as part of your management plan.

Infection prevention is a major topic and the examiner will test it. Antibiotic-impregnated catheters are one of the strongest interventions. These catheters are coated with minocycline and rifampin or clindamycin and rifampin, and multiple studies and meta-analyses have shown they reduce ventriculitis rates to 1 to 4 percent compared to standard catheters. Strict sterile insertion technique is essential. Subcutaneous tunneling of the catheter for at least 5 centimeters from the burr hole reduces infection by creating a longer barrier against skin flora migration. Minimize the frequency of CSF sampling to reduce system violations. Maintain a closed drainage system at all times. Nursing protocols should minimize disconnections of the drainage system. One important point the examiner may try to trap you on: systemic prophylactic antibiotics during EVD placement. A single perioperative dose of antibiotics at the time of insertion is standard. However, there is no evidence supporting ongoing daily prophylactic antibiotics for the duration of EVD placement. In fact, prolonged prophylactic antibiotics may select for resistant organisms, making subsequent infections harder to treat.

Let me give you three trap questions the examiner might throw at you. First: the EVD is draining clear CSF but the ICP reads zero. What is happening? There are two possibilities you should work through. First, the transducer may be malpositioned. Recheck that the transducer is leveled at the tragus. If it has been moved above the tragus, the readings will be falsely low. Second, and more likely, the drain is open and CSF is freely draining. If you are using continuous drainage, what you are reading is the drainage pressure, not the true intracranial pressure. You must clamp the drain briefly to measure the true ICP.

Second trap question: CSF shows a white blood cell count of 500, but the patient had subarachnoid hemorrhage and the EVD has been in for 10 days. Is this infection or chemical ventriculitis? This is a difficult distinction and the examiner knows it. You need to demonstrate a systematic approach. Trend the CSF white cell count. Is it rising or falling? If it is rising at day 10, that is concerning for infection. Check the CSF glucose and protein trends. A falling glucose, especially below 50 percent of the serum glucose, strongly suggests bacterial infection. Look at the cell differential. A neutrophilic predominance points toward bacterial infection, while a lymphocytic predominance suggests chemical ventriculitis from blood breakdown products. Send gram stain and cultures. The correct answer is that rising WBC with falling glucose strongly suggests infection, and you should treat empirically while cultures are pending.

Third trap question: would you place an ICP monitor in a patient with a GCS of 8 and a normal CT? This is a direct test of whether you know the Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines. The answer is yes, you should consider ICP monitoring if the patient has a GCS of 8 or less with a normal CT and two or more of the following risk factors: age over 40, unilateral or bilateral motor posturing, or systolic blood pressure less than 90 millimeters of mercury. A normal CT alone does not exclude elevated intracranial pressure, and approximately 10 to 15 percent of patients with severe TBI and a normal initial CT will develop intracranial hypertension.

Let's move to the evidence base you need to cite. The Brain Trauma Foundation fourth edition guidelines are your primary reference for ICP monitoring indications, the treatment threshold of 22, and the level of evidence supporting monitoring in severe TBI. The BEST TRIP trial, published by Chesnut in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012, randomized patients in Bolivia to ICP monitoring-based management versus clinical and imaging-based management. There was no outcome difference between the groups. This trial is widely cited and widely debated. The critical nuance you must articulate is that this trial does not mean ICP monitoring is useless. The clinical-imaging group received aggressive empiric treatment that may have effectively treated elevated ICP without a monitor. The trial was conducted in a resource-limited setting with a different patient population and practice environment than most North American trauma centers. The appropriate interpretation is that ICP monitoring is one tool in a broader management strategy, not that we should abandon it. Meta-analyses of antibiotic-impregnated catheters consistently show reduced ventriculitis rates, and Lozier's systematic review of EVD infection rates remains a frequently cited reference for understanding the scope of this complication.

Let's finish with the board pearls you absolutely must have memorized. The EVD is the gold standard for ICP monitoring. It is the only monitor that is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Know Kocher's point landmarks precisely: 1 centimeter anterior to the coronal suture, 2 to 3 centimeters lateral to midline, at the mid-pupillary line. Aim toward the medial canthus in the coronal plane and the tragus in the sagittal plane. Depth is 5 to 7 centimeters. Ventriculitis diagnosis rests on the triad of rising CSF white blood cell count, falling CSF glucose, and positive CSF cultures. Neutrophilic predominance distinguishes bacterial infection from chemical ventriculitis. Antibiotic-impregnated catheters reduce infection rates from 5 to 15 percent down to 1 to 4 percent and should be used whenever available. The ICP treatment threshold per the BTF fourth edition is 22 millimeters of mercury. P2 exceeding P1 on the ICP waveform indicates decreased compliance. Lundberg A waves are plateau waves, they are pathologic, and they require immediate treatment. Normal CT does not rule out elevated ICP in a patient with GCS of 8 or less if risk factors are present. The BEST TRIP trial showed no outcome difference between monitoring-based and clinical-based management, but it does not invalidate the use of ICP monitoring.
