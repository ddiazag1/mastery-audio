Let's master advanced neuroimaging for the oral boards.

Perfusion, spectroscopy, functional imaging, and tractography are where the examiner separates the candidate who orders imaging from the candidate who understands it. These studies come up in three recurring scenarios: stroke triage, distinguishing tumor recurrence from treatment effect, and planning surgery near eloquent cortex. Let's take each technique, establish what it measures, and then attach it to the decisions it actually drives.

Start with perfusion imaging, because it has the clearest management consequences.

Perfusion measures the passage of blood through the capillary bed and yields several parameters you must be able to name. Cerebral blood flow is the volume of blood passing through a given mass of tissue per unit time. Cerebral blood volume is the fraction of a voxel occupied by blood. Mean transit time is the average time for blood to traverse the capillary bed, and time to peak and the derived parameter T max describe the delay in contrast arrival. The governing relationship is that cerebral blood flow equals cerebral blood volume divided by mean transit time.

The techniques differ. Computed tomographic perfusion uses iodinated contrast and is fast, available, and the workhorse of acute stroke triage. Dynamic susceptibility contrast magnetic resonance perfusion uses gadolinium and gives relative cerebral blood volume, which is the key tumor parameter. Arterial spin labeling magnetically labels arterial blood water as an endogenous tracer and requires no contrast at all, which makes it valuable in renal impairment and in children.

Now stroke. The entire modern thrombectomy paradigm rests on the distinction between core and penumbra. The infarct core is tissue already irreversibly injured, identified by markedly reduced cerebral blood flow, conventionally below roughly thirty percent of the contralateral normal side, or by restricted diffusion on magnetic resonance. The penumbra is hypoperfused but still viable tissue, identified by prolonged T max, conventionally greater than six seconds. The mismatch between the two represents salvageable brain. A large penumbra with a small core is the patient who benefits from reperfusion. A large core is the patient in whom reperfusion risks hemorrhagic transformation without functional benefit.

Know why this matters historically. Perfusion selection is what extended the thrombectomy window from six hours out to twenty four hours in appropriately selected patients, based on the DAWN and DEFUSE 3 trials. DAWN used clinical and core mismatch out to twenty four hours, and DEFUSE 3 used perfusion imaging mismatch from six to sixteen hours. Be able to say that selection is now physiologic rather than purely chronologic, which is the conceptual point the examiner wants.

Also know the pitfalls. Perfusion maps are post processed and susceptible to motion and to poor arterial input function selection. A delayed but not critically hypoperfused territory, such as that seen with chronic carotid occlusion and good collateral flow, can mimic penumbra. And the ghost infarct core phenomenon describes overestimation of the core in patients imaged very early, which can inappropriately exclude a patient from treatment.

Perfusion also has a cerebrovascular reserve application. In chronic occlusive disease such as moyamoya or carotid occlusion, a perfusion study performed before and after a vasodilatory challenge with acetazolamide or carbon dioxide assesses whether the vasculature has any remaining capacity to dilate. Exhausted reserve, meaning no augmentation or paradoxical steal, identifies the patient at highest stroke risk and supports revascularization.

Now perfusion in tumors. Relative cerebral blood volume correlates with neovascularity and therefore with tumor grade. High grade gliomas show elevated relative cerebral blood volume in the enhancing tissue. Low grade gliomas show low values. The two highest yield applications are these. First, distinguishing tumor recurrence from radiation necrosis: recurrent tumor shows elevated relative cerebral blood volume, whereas radiation necrosis shows low values because it is a destructive rather than proliferative process. Second, guiding the biopsy target in a heterogeneous glioma: you sample the region of highest relative cerebral blood volume, because that is where the highest grade tissue lives and it protects you against undergrading from sampling error. Say that explicitly when the examiner gives you a non-enhancing or heterogeneously enhancing glioma.

One more nuance worth having: the enhancing rim of a metastasis and the enhancing rim of a glioblastoma both show elevated perfusion, but the peritumoral T2 signal differs. In glioblastoma, the peritumoral region contains infiltrating tumor and therefore shows somewhat elevated relative cerebral blood volume; in metastasis, the peritumoral region is pure vasogenic edema with low relative cerebral blood volume. That can help distinguish a solitary metastasis from a glioblastoma.

Now magnetic resonance spectroscopy. It measures the concentration of metabolites, displayed as peaks along a chemical shift axis in parts per million. Know the major peaks and their positions and meanings.

N acetylaspartate sits at approximately two point zero parts per million and is a neuronal marker, so it decreases with any process that destroys or displaces neurons. Creatine sits at approximately three point zero and is relatively stable, which is why it is used as the internal reference for ratios. Choline sits at approximately three point two and reflects cell membrane turnover, so it increases with proliferation, meaning tumor, and also with demyelination. Lactate sits at approximately one point three as a doublet that inverts on an intermediate echo time acquisition, a diagnostic behavior worth knowing, and indicates anaerobic metabolism. Lipid also sits near one point three and indicates necrosis. Myoinositol sits at approximately three point five and is a glial marker, elevated in low grade gliomas and in gliosis. Alanine sits at approximately one point five and is characteristically elevated in meningiomas.

The tumor pattern is elevated choline, decreased N acetylaspartate, and therefore an elevated choline to N acetylaspartate ratio, with lactate and lipid appearing as the lesion becomes necrotic and higher grade. The abscess pattern, as discussed elsewhere, shows amino acids, acetate, and succinate. Radiation necrosis shows depressed everything, with lipid and lactate but without the choline elevation of recurrent tumor. That is again the recurrence versus necrosis question, and spectroscopy and perfusion should be quoted together.

Understand the limitations so you can bound your answer. Spectroscopy requires a reasonably sized voxel, so it performs poorly for small lesions. It is degraded near bone, air, and blood products because of susceptibility effects, making the skull base and posterior fossa difficult. And it is not specific enough to replace tissue diagnosis. Say that you would use it to support a decision, not to make one.

Now positron emission tomography. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography is limited in the brain because normal cortex is intensely glucose avid, producing high background. Amino acid tracers perform far better for brain tumors, and you should be able to name them: fluoroethyltyrosine, methionine, and fluorodopa. These have low normal brain uptake and are useful for delineating tumor extent, differentiating recurrence from treatment effect, and guiding biopsy. In the lymphoma versus toxoplasmosis question in the immunocompromised patient, lymphoma is metabolically avid and toxoplasmosis is not.

Now functional magnetic resonance imaging. It detects the blood oxygen level dependent signal, an indirect measure of neuronal activity based on the local change in the ratio of oxyhemoglobin to deoxyhemoglobin during activation. Its surgical applications are lateralizing language, which has substantially reduced but not eliminated the use of the intracarotid amobarbital or Wada test, and localizing motor and sensory cortex relative to a lesion.

Be candid about its limitations, because the examiner will probe them. Functional imaging shows correlation, not necessity: a region that activates during a task is not necessarily a region whose removal causes a deficit. Neurovascular uncoupling near a high grade tumor or an arteriovenous malformation can produce false negative activation, meaning eloquent cortex appears silent precisely where you most need to know. Task performance and patient cooperation drive data quality. And spatial accuracy relative to the true cortical margin is imperfect. Therefore the correct examination answer is that functional imaging is a planning adjunct and that intraoperative direct cortical and subcortical stimulation mapping, with awake craniotomy for language, remains the gold standard. Never let functional imaging alone justify a resection margin in eloquent territory.

Now diffusion tensor imaging and tractography. Diffusion in white matter is anisotropic, meaning water moves preferentially along the axis of the fiber tracts. By measuring diffusion in multiple directions, you derive fractional anisotropy, a scalar from zero to one describing how directional the diffusion is, and the principal eigenvector, the dominant direction. Tractography chains these directions together to reconstruct fiber bundles.

Surgically, this lets you visualize the corticospinal tract, the arcuate fasciculus for language, the optic radiations including Meyer loop for temporal lobe surgery, and the superior longitudinal fasciculus. You can classify the relationship between tumor and tract as displaced, infiltrated, or disrupted, which informs the expected deficit and the aggressiveness of resection.

Again, know the limitations and state them. Tractography algorithms struggle at crossing fibers, and a large fraction of white matter voxels contain crossing fibers, so simple tensor based approaches under-resolve them. Peritumoral edema reduces fractional anisotropy and can cause a tract to appear absent when it is merely edematous, a false negative that could lead you to resect through an intact tract. Different algorithms and different thresholds produce visibly different tracts from the same data, which is an uncomfortable but real point. Brain shift during surgery degrades the accuracy of any preoperative dataset once the dura is open and cerebrospinal fluid is lost. The answer, once more, is that tractography guides but subcortical stimulation mapping confirms, and that intraoperative ultrasound or magnetic resonance can help correct for shift.

Be precise about which perfusion technique you are asking for, because they are not interchangeable. Dynamic susceptibility contrast tracks the first pass of a gadolinium bolus through the capillary bed using the signal drop it causes, and it is the standard method for relative cerebral blood volume in tumours. Dynamic contrast enhanced perfusion instead tracks slow accumulation of contrast in the extravascular space and yields permeability measures, principally the transfer constant, which reflects blood brain barrier leakiness and is useful for grading and for treatment response. Arterial spin labelling uses magnetically labelled arterial blood water as an endogenous tracer, needs no contrast at all, and is therefore the technique of choice in renal impairment, in children, and when you need serial studies. Computed tomographic perfusion is fastest and most available and dominates acute stroke triage. If an examiner asks how you would assess a tumour in a patient with a glomerular filtration rate of twenty, the answer is arterial spin labelling, and that is exactly the kind of question this knowledge is for.

Know the specific numbers that drive stroke decisions, because vague answers lose points here. The infarct core is conventionally defined on computed tomographic perfusion as tissue with cerebral blood flow below thirty percent of the contralateral normal side, or on magnetic resonance as the volume of restricted diffusion. The penumbra is defined by a time to maximum, written T max, greater than six seconds. The mismatch volume is the penumbra minus the core, and the mismatch ratio is the penumbral volume divided by the core volume. Selection in the extended window has generally required a core below about seventy millilitres, a mismatch ratio of at least one point eight, and a mismatch volume of at least fifteen millilitres. Quote those as the trial derived thresholds that informed practice rather than as immutable law, and note that more recent work has extended thrombectomy to selected patients with large cores, so the field is moving toward treating rather than excluding.

Have the pitfalls ready, because an examiner who uses perfusion will usually probe whether you trust it blindly. Post processing software differs between vendors and produces different core volumes from identical raw data. A poorly chosen arterial input function distorts every map. Motion degrades everything. Chronic large vessel occlusion with good collaterals produces delayed transit that mimics penumbra without genuine jeopardy. Very early imaging can overestimate the core, the so called ghost infarct core, and exclude a patient who would have benefited. And in the posterior circulation perfusion is far less reliable, so a basilar occlusion is a clinical and angiographic diagnosis rather than a perfusion one.

For spectroscopy, know how the study is actually acquired, since that determines what you can ask of it. Single voxel spectroscopy places one cube, typically one and a half to two centimetres on a side, and gives a clean spectrum from that location. Multi voxel or chemical shift imaging acquires a grid and lets you compare tumour with contralateral normal tissue and map heterogeneity, which is more useful for biopsy targeting. Echo time matters: a short echo time of around thirty milliseconds shows more metabolites including myoinositol and lipids, while a long echo time of around one hundred forty milliseconds gives a cleaner baseline and is where the lactate doublet inverts, which is the behaviour that confirms lactate rather than lipid. Always place a control voxel in normal appearing contralateral tissue and quote ratios rather than absolute values.

For functional imaging, be able to describe the paradigms rather than just the principle. Motor mapping uses finger tapping, foot movement or tongue movement against rest. Language mapping uses verb generation, word generation to letters, sentence completion or semantic decision tasks, and lateralisation is expressed as a laterality index comparing activation in homologous regions. Know that a patient who cannot perform the task reliably, whether from aphasia, paresis, youth or confusion, will produce uninterpretable data, and that this is common precisely in the patients you most want to map. Then close with the necessary caveat: activation demonstrates participation, not indispensability, and neurovascular uncoupling near a high grade glioma or an arteriovenous malformation can silence genuinely eloquent cortex. Preoperative mapping plans the approach; intraoperative direct cortical and subcortical stimulation, with an awake craniotomy for language, establishes the resection margin.

For tractography, know the quantitative outputs and the qualitative trap. Fractional anisotropy runs from zero, meaning fully isotropic diffusion, to one, meaning diffusion entirely along one axis; mean diffusivity describes overall magnitude. Peritumoural oedema lowers fractional anisotropy and can make an intact tract disappear from the reconstruction, which is a false negative that could lead you to resect through it. Simple tensor models cannot resolve crossing fibres, which occupy a large fraction of white matter voxels, so higher order models such as constrained spherical deconvolution and probabilistic tracking perform better but are less widely deployed. Different algorithms and thresholds yield visibly different tracts from identical data. And once the dura is open and cerebrospinal fluid is lost, brain shift degrades the accuracy of any preoperative dataset, which is the argument for intraoperative ultrasound, intraoperative magnetic resonance, or simply for trusting stimulation over navigation.

Finally, place these tools in a decision framework rather than listing them. Order an advanced sequence only when it will change what you do next, and say which decision it will change. Perfusion in acute stroke changes whether you take the patient for thrombectomy. Perfusion and spectroscopy in a previously treated glioma change whether you reoperate or continue surveillance. Perfusion in a heterogeneous non-enhancing glioma changes where you put the biopsy needle. Functional imaging and tractography change your trajectory and your counselling about expected deficit. Cerebrovascular reserve testing changes whether you revascularise. If you cannot name the decision, you do not need the scan, and saying so demonstrates better judgement than ordering everything.

To summarize how to deploy all of this. For acute stroke, perfusion defines core and penumbra, and mismatch extends the thrombectomy window to twenty four hours in selected patients. For chronic occlusive disease, perfusion with a vasodilatory challenge defines cerebrovascular reserve. For tumor grading and biopsy targeting, relative cerebral blood volume points to the highest grade tissue. For recurrence versus radiation necrosis, elevated relative cerebral blood volume and elevated choline favor recurrence, whereas low perfusion and low choline with lipid and lactate favor necrosis, supported by amino acid positron emission tomography. For eloquent surgery, functional imaging and tractography plan the approach and direct stimulation mapping confirms it. Order each study because it will change a decision, say what decision it will change, and always state the limitation before the examiner does.
