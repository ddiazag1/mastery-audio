  - q: What is first-line medical therapy, and what must you check first in some patients?
    a: Carbamazepine. In patients of Asian descent, HLA-B*15:02 testing should be performed before starting it, because carriers are at high risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis. That pharmacogenomic point is specifically examinable.
    probe: What is the second-line agent?
    probeA: Oxcarbazepine, which has similar efficacy to carbamazepine with a better side-effect and interaction profile, though with a higher risk of hyponatraemia, which must be monitored. Beyond that, second-line and add-on agents include lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin or pregabalin, and phenytoin, with botulinum toxin type A having reasonable randomised evidence as an adjunct. The practically important point is that only carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine have strong evidence as first-line, and the others are used when those fail or are not tolerated. Failure of adequate doses of carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine is what should trigger a surgical referral, not an indefinite sequence of add-ons.
  - q: Quote the durability of microvascular decompression.
    a: Seventy percent pain-free at ten years, which establishes it as the durable gold standard surgical treatment. No ablative procedure matches that durability, which is the central argument for offering MVD to a fit patient with a demonstrated neurovascular conflict.
    probe: What is the trade-off against the ablative options?
    probeA: Durability against invasiveness and against numbness. Microvascular decompression is the most durable, at around seventy percent pain-free at ten years, and it is non-destructive, so it does not cause the facial numbness that the ablative procedures rely on and therefore carries essentially no risk of anaesthesia dolorosa. Its cost is that it is a posterior fossa craniotomy under general anaesthesia, with risks of hearing loss, cerebrospinal fluid leak, meningitis, cerebellar injury and a small mortality. The ablative procedures are quicker, can be done under sedation, and suit the frail or those with limited life expectancy, but they are less durable, they all produce some sensory loss, and they carry the risk of corneal anaesthesia, masseter weakness and anaesthesia dolorosa. So the decision turns on the patient's fitness and life expectancy far more than on the severity of the pain.
  - q: Which vessel is usually responsible, and where?
    a: The superior cerebellar artery in the majority, compressing the nerve at the root entry zone, where central myelin transitions to peripheral — the region most vulnerable to demyelination from pulsatile compression. The anterior inferior cerebellar artery and veins account for most of the remainder.
    probe: What imaging would you obtain to demonstrate it?
    probeA: High-resolution MRI of the posterior fossa with a heavily T2-weighted steady-state sequence such as CISS or FIESTA, which gives excellent contrast between cerebrospinal fluid, the nerve and vessels at submillimetre resolution, combined with time-of-flight MR angiography and post-contrast T1 for the venous anatomy. Ideally these are fused so I can see the vessel, the nerve and their relationship in three dimensions. I would also look for evidence of atrophy or distortion of the nerve at the compression point, which is a better predictor of surgical success than simple contact, since vascular contact alone is common in asymptomatic people. And equally important, the study is excluding secondary causes: a cerebellopontine angle tumour, a vascular malformation, and demyelinating plaques in the pons.
  - q: Classical or secondary? How do you tell and why does it matter?
    a: Imaging. Classical trigeminal neuralgia has neurovascular compression; secondary has a structural cause — most importantly multiple sclerosis, a cerebellopontine angle tumour, or a vascular malformation. It matters because the treatment differs entirely, and MVD will not help a demyelinating plaque in the pons.
    probe: What clinical features suggest a secondary cause?
    probeA: Objective sensory loss in the trigeminal territory on examination, which is the single most important one, because classical trigeminal neuralgia should have a normal examination. Bilateral symptoms. Younger age at onset, under about forty. Involvement of the first division in isolation. Constant background pain between paroxysms rather than complete freedom. Associated cranial nerve deficits, particularly hearing loss or facial weakness suggesting a cerebellopontine angle lesion. Other neurological symptoms or signs suggesting multiple sclerosis. And failure to respond to carbamazepine, which is unusual in classical disease. Any of those mandates careful MRI and a considered search for a structural or demyelinating cause.
  - q: Young patient with bilateral symptoms and sensory loss. What are you thinking?
    a: Multiple sclerosis until proven otherwise. Bilateral disease, young age, objective sensory loss and atypical features all point away from classical trigeminal neuralgia. I would obtain dedicated MRI looking for demyelinating plaques including in the pons at the trigeminal root entry zone.
    probe: How would you treat that patient?
    probeA: Medically first and for longer, because microvascular decompression does not treat a demyelinating plaque and the results in multiple sclerosis are considerably worse. I would optimise antiepileptic therapy, and liaise with neurology about whether disease-modifying therapy is optimised, since the trigeminal pain may improve with better disease control. When medical therapy fails, I would favour an ablative procedure, usually percutaneous radiofrequency thermocoagulation, balloon compression or glycerol rhizolysis, or stereotactic radiosurgery, accepting that recurrence is commoner and that repeat procedures are frequently needed. Microvascular decompression is reserved for the multiple sclerosis patient in whom imaging demonstrates genuine neurovascular conflict in addition to the plaque, and even then I would counsel a lower expectation of success.
  - q: Compare the ablative options.
    a: Percutaneous procedures — radiofrequency thermocoagulation, balloon compression, glycerol rhizolysis — give rapid relief with lower durability and cause some sensory loss. Stereotactic radiosurgery is non-invasive but has a latency of weeks to months and lower long-term pain-free rates. All are preferable to MVD in the frail or those with limited life expectancy.
    probe: Which carries the risk of anaesthesia dolorosa?
    probeA: All of the destructive ones, because they all work by injuring the nerve, and anaesthesia dolorosa is a complication of deafferentation. The risk is highest with radiofrequency thermocoagulation, which is the most precisely destructive and produces the densest numbness, and it rises with the degree of sensory loss achieved, which is why the lesion is titrated to hypoalgesia rather than dense anaesthesia. Glycerol rhizolysis and balloon compression carry a lower but real risk. Stereotactic radiosurgery carries the lowest, with troublesome numbness in a minority and anaesthesia dolorosa rare. Microvascular decompression, being non-destructive, essentially does not cause it. That gradient is exactly why I would not offer a destructive procedure to a fit forty-year-old with a demonstrated vascular conflict.
  - q: What is anaesthesia dolorosa?
    a: Painful deafferentation in a numb territory following an ablative procedure — a devastating and largely untreatable complication. It is the strongest argument against aggressive ablation in a young patient, and a reason to prefer microvascular decompression where the patient is fit for it.
    probe: How would you manage it if it occurred?
    probeA: Poorly, and I would say that honestly, because it is largely untreatable and the first duty is to avoid causing it. Management is medical and multidisciplinary rather than surgical: tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline or nortriptyline, serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, gabapentinoids, and in some cases topical agents. Referral to a specialist pain service is essential, with psychological support and cognitive behavioural therapy, because the condition carries a high burden of depression and a recognised suicide risk. Further ablation is contraindicated: destroying more nerve makes deafferentation pain worse, not better. Neuromodulation, including motor cortex stimulation and in selected cases deep brain stimulation, has been tried with modest and inconsistent results and would be a specialist referral rather than something I would offer routinely.
  - q: Consent a patient for microvascular decompression.
    a: Hearing loss from eighth nerve manipulation, which is why intraoperative brainstem auditory evoked potentials are used; facial numbness and weakness; cerebrospinal fluid leak; meningitis; cerebellar injury or haematoma; recurrence in a minority over years; and the usual anaesthetic risks. Mortality is low but not zero.
    probe: Which complication would you monitor for intraoperatively?
    probeA: Brainstem auditory evoked potentials, continuously, because hearing loss is the commonest serious complication of microvascular decompression and it is usually caused by traction on the eighth nerve complex or by compromise of the labyrinthine artery during cerebellar retraction. A prolongation of wave V latency or a fall in its amplitude gives me real-time warning to release retraction before the injury becomes permanent. I would also monitor facial nerve electromyography, and in the same operation I would minimise fixed retraction in favour of gravity and dynamic retraction, avoid excessive cerebrospinal fluid drainage, and use a Teflon or similar implant placed so that it separates the vessel from the nerve without itself compressing it, since a poorly placed implant is a recognised cause of recurrence and of new symptoms.
cases:
  - label: Lancinating facial pain
    stem: A 54-year-old woman describes eighteen months of electric shock-like pain in the right cheek and upper lip, lasting seconds, triggered by chewing, cold wind and touching her face while applying make-up. She is pain-free between attacks. Neurological examination including facial sensation is normal. Carbamazepine gave good relief initially but she now has dizziness and hyponatraemia on 1,000 milligrams daily.
    q1: Characterise the pain and say what the normal examination tells you.
    a1: Classical trigeminal neuralgia in the V2 distribution: paroxysmal, lancinating, seconds in duration, trigger-evoked, with complete freedom between attacks. A normal examination — specifically normal facial sensation — argues for classical rather than secondary trigeminal neuralgia, where objective sensory loss would point to a structural cause.
    p1: She has hyponatraemia on carbamazepine. Would switching to oxcarbazepine help?
    pa1: Probably not for that specific problem, and it is worth knowing why. Hyponatraemia is a class effect mediated by an antidiuretic-like action, and oxcarbazepine causes it more frequently than carbamazepine, not less, so switching for that reason is likely to make it worse. Where oxcarbazepine does help is with other carbamazepine intolerances: it has fewer drug interactions, no autoinduction, and less risk of the idiosyncratic haematological and hepatic reactions. Her dizziness may well improve on oxcarbazepine. So the honest answer is that switching is reasonable for the dizziness but not for the sodium, and in practice her intolerance at a therapeutic dose is exactly the situation in which surgical referral is appropriate rather than another drug trial.
    q2: What imaging and what are you looking for?
    a2: High-resolution MRI with heavily T2-weighted steady-state sequences through the cerebellopontine angle, to demonstrate neurovascular conflict at the root entry zone, most often from the superior cerebellar artery. Equally important, I am excluding secondary causes: a cerebellopontine angle tumour, a vascular malformation, and demyelinating plaques of multiple sclerosis.
    p2: Contact between a vessel and the nerve is common in normal people. So how much does it prove?
    pa2: Not much on its own, and this is the key nuance. Simple neurovascular contact is found in a substantial proportion of asymptomatic nerves, including on the unaffected side of patients with unilateral trigeminal neuralgia, so its presence is weakly discriminating. What carries predictive weight is contact with morphological change: distortion, indentation, displacement or atrophy of the nerve at the root entry zone, which is much more strongly associated with symptomatic disease and predicts a good response to decompression. So I would report not merely whether a vessel touches the nerve, but whether it deforms it, which vessel it is, and where along the nerve the conflict lies. A patient with classic symptoms and contact without deformity is a less certain surgical candidate, and I would say so before offering the operation.
    q3: Imaging shows clear superior cerebellar artery compression at the root entry zone. She is fit and 54. What do you recommend?
    a3: Microvascular decompression. She has failed medical therapy through intolerance rather than lack of efficacy, which is a legitimate indication, and she has a demonstrated conflict. MVD gives seventy percent pain-free rates at ten years — no ablative procedure matches that durability, and at fifty-four with a long life expectancy durability matters.
    p3: What recurrence rate do you quote, and what do you do about it?
    pa3: Roughly thirty percent over ten years, which is the corollary of the seventy percent pain-free figure, with most recurrences occurring in the first two years and the annual rate then falling to around one to two percent. I would tell her that plainly so that a recurrence is not experienced as a failure of the operation or of her. If it recurs, the options are to retry medical therapy, which sometimes works again at a lower dose, repeat microvascular decompression, which is reasonable if imaging shows a new or displaced conflict or a poorly placed implant and has good results in selected patients, or an ablative procedure. Repeat decompression is technically harder because of arachnoid scarring and carries higher rates of hearing loss, so it is a decision to be made carefully rather than automatically.
    q4: She asks why not just have the needle procedure her friend had. Answer her.
    a4: Percutaneous ablative procedures — radiofrequency, balloon compression, glycerol — give rapid relief with a shorter recovery and are excellent options for frail patients or those with limited life expectancy. But they are less durable and they work by injuring the nerve, so they cause facial numbness and carry a risk of anaesthesia dolorosa, painful deafferentation in a numb face, which is devastating and largely untreatable. In a fit fifty-four-year-old I would rather decompress the nerve than damage it.
    p4: Her friend did well. How do you avoid sounding dismissive of that?
    pa4: By acknowledging it as true rather than arguing with it. I would say that her friend's result is exactly what a percutaneous procedure can deliver, that it is a good operation and I offer it regularly, and that the question is not which operation is better in the abstract but which is better for her. Then I would give the reason in terms of her own circumstances: she is fifty-four and fit, so she has perhaps thirty years in which durability matters, and she has normal facial sensation now, which the needle procedures work by taking away. I would make clear that if she preferred a shorter recovery and was willing to accept numbness and a higher recurrence rate, a percutaneous procedure would be a reasonable choice and I would support it. Presenting it as her decision informed by the trade-offs, rather than as my verdict on her friend's treatment, is what keeps the conversation useful.

Let's work through trigeminal neuralgia from presentation to treatment algorithm. This is a bread-and-butter functional neurosurgery topic that shows up on the oral boards consistently, and you need to be able to walk through the entire decision tree without hesitation.

Starting with the foundation. Trigeminal neuralgia is defined as paroxysmal, lancinating, electric shock-like pain in the distribution of one or more divisions of cranial nerve five. The incidence is four to thirteen per one hundred thousand per year, with a female predominance of roughly one and a half to one. It favors the right side over the left at about a sixty to forty ratio. The second and third divisions, V2 and V3, are the most commonly affected. V1 involvement alone is uncommon, and when you see isolated V1 pain, you need to broaden your differential because that pattern should make you think about other diagnoses, including cluster headache, trochlear headache, or a structural lesion in the superior orbital fissure or cavernous sinus.

Now let's distinguish the two main clinical types, because this distinction drives every treatment decision you will make. Type 1, the classic form, presents as predominantly episodic sharp, shooting, or stabbing pain with clear pain-free intervals between attacks. These episodes are triggered by innocuous stimuli: light touch to the face, chewing, talking, cold wind, brushing the teeth. There is typically a refractory period after each attack during which another paroxysm cannot be provoked. This is the pattern that responds best to treatment across the board, both medical and surgical. Type 2, the atypical form, features a constant aching or burning background pain with superimposed sharp episodes layered on top. There are fewer pain-free intervals, and sometimes no truly pain-free periods at all. This pattern responds less well to every treatment modality, and you must counsel patients accordingly before offering any intervention.

The etiology breaks down as follows. Neurovascular compression accounts for eighty to ninety percent of cases. The most common offending vessel is the superior cerebellar artery compressing the trigeminal nerve at the root entry zone, which is the transition zone where central myelin produced by oligodendrocytes meets peripheral myelin produced by Schwann cells. This is the Obersteiner-Redlich zone. The vulnerability at this location is due to the relative paucity of myelin at the transition point. The anterior inferior cerebellar artery and small veins can also be the offending structure, though less commonly. Multiple sclerosis accounts for two to four percent of cases, where a demyelinating plaque at or near the root entry zone produces the same pathophysiology through a different mechanism. Tumors cause two to five percent: think epidermoid cysts, meningiomas, and schwannomas. The remainder are classified as idiopathic when no structural cause is identified.

For imaging, you need an MRI with thin-cut CISS or FIESTA sequences through the posterior fossa. These heavily T2-weighted, high-resolution sequences give you exquisite contrast between cerebrospinal fluid, the cisternal segment of the trigeminal nerve, and surrounding vascular structures. You are looking for neurovascular contact or compression at the root entry zone, and you must also exclude a tumor or demyelinating plaques suggesting multiple sclerosis.

Moving into the clinical case. A fifty-eight-year-old woman presents with a two-year history of right-sided electric shock-like pain in the cheek and jaw, localizing to the V2 and V3 distribution. The pain is triggered by chewing and brushing her teeth. She has clear pain-free intervals between episodes. She was initially controlled on carbamazepine two hundred milligrams three times daily, but despite dose escalation to twelve hundred milligrams per day, she now has breakthrough pain. Her neurological examination is entirely normal, with no facial numbness, no motor weakness, no other cranial nerve deficits. MRI demonstrates a superior cerebellar artery loop contacting the right trigeminal nerve at the root entry zone. There are no demyelinating plaques.

Now let's move into the examiner interrogation, because this is where the oral boards live.

First, confirm the diagnosis. This is classic trigeminal neuralgia, Type 1. The features that lock this in are the episodic nature of the pain, the trigger zones, the V2 and V3 distribution, the pain-free intervals, and the identification of neurovascular compression on imaging. Critically, her neurological examination is normal. This matters because any trigeminal sensory deficit, facial numbness, or weakness of mastication should immediately raise your suspicion for a tumor compressing or infiltrating the nerve, or for multiple sclerosis. A normal exam in the setting of classic symptoms and imaging confirmation of vascular compression is the textbook setup.

Next, walk through the medical management. The first-line agent is carbamazepine. You start at one hundred milligrams twice daily and titrate upward to a target range of two hundred to four hundred milligrams three times daily. The maximum dose is twelve hundred milligrams per day. The initial response rate is seventy to eighty percent, which is actually so reliable that some clinicians consider response to carbamazepine as nearly diagnostic. The alternative first-line agent is oxcarbazepine, which has equivalent efficacy but is better tolerated with fewer drug-drug interactions because it does not induce the cytochrome P450 system the way carbamazepine does. Oxcarbazepine dosing is three hundred to six hundred milligrams twice daily.

For monitoring, both drugs can cause hyponatremia, and this is actually worse with oxcarbazepine than with carbamazepine, so check sodium levels. With carbamazepine specifically, you need a baseline CBC and periodic monitoring because aplastic anemia, while rare, is a real risk. In patients of Asian descent, HLA-B star fifteen oh two testing should be performed before starting carbamazepine because carriers are at high risk for Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis. You also need liver function tests because carbamazepine is hepatotoxic. Second-line agents include baclofen, which works well as an add-on to carbamazepine, lamotrigine, and gabapentin or pregabalin, though the evidence for these latter agents is weaker.

This patient has failed adequate medical therapy. She is on maximum-dose carbamazepine with breakthrough pain. This establishes the surgical indication. The two indications for surgery in trigeminal neuralgia are medication failure and intolerable medication side effects. She meets the first criterion.

Now walk through the three surgical categories, because the examiner will want you to compare them head to head.

Category one: microvascular decompression, the Jannetta procedure. This is performed through a retrosigmoid craniotomy. You open behind the sigmoid sinus, retract the cerebellum superolaterally, and follow the superior petrosal vein complex to identify cranial nerve five at the root entry zone. You identify the offending vessel, most commonly the superior cerebellar artery, and you interpose a small piece of shredded Teflon felt between the vessel and the nerve. The key principle is that you transpose the vessel away from the nerve; you do not mobilize or manipulate the nerve itself. The results of MVD are the best of any intervention for trigeminal neuralgia. Initial pain relief is ninety to ninety-five percent. The ten-year pain-free rate is seventy to eighty percent. It has the lowest recurrence rate of any procedure. It preserves facial sensation because you are not destroying nerve fibers, you are removing the compressive pathology. For these reasons, MVD is the preferred procedure in younger, medically fit patients with identified vascular compression. Complications include hearing loss in one to two percent, which occurs from traction on cranial nerve eight or compromise of the labyrinthine artery, facial numbness which is rare with proper technique, CSF leak, aseptic meningitis from Teflon granuloma formation, hemorrhage, other cranial nerve injury, and very rarely cerebellar injury from retraction. The most dreaded complication across all trigeminal neuralgia procedures is anesthesia dolorosa, which I will come back to because it is a trap question.

Category two: stereotactic radiosurgery, specifically Gamma Knife. You target the root entry zone of cranial nerve five with a single fraction of seventy to ninety Gray delivered to a four-millimeter isocenter. This is completely non-invasive. Pain relief occurs in eighty to ninety percent of patients, but the onset is delayed by weeks to months, which is important for patient counseling. Long-term recurrence is twenty to thirty percent at five years, which is higher than MVD. Facial numbness develops in ten to thirty percent of patients, and interestingly, the development of some degree of numbness actually correlates with better and more durable pain relief. Gamma Knife is best suited for elderly patients, those who are medically unfit for general anesthesia and craniotomy, patients on anticoagulation, those who refuse open surgery, and patients with multiple sclerosis-associated trigeminal neuralgia.

Category three: percutaneous procedures. All three percutaneous approaches access the Gasserian ganglion through the foramen ovale under fluoroscopic guidance. They share the advantage of being performed under brief general anesthesia or sedation, being quick, and providing immediate pain relief. They share the disadvantage of intentional partial sensory loss: numbness is expected and actually desired as it indicates an adequate lesion.

First, percutaneous balloon compression, the Mullan technique. You pass a Fogarty catheter through the foramen ovale into Meckel's cave and inflate the balloon for one to two minutes. The pear-shaped balloon compresses the large myelinated fibers that mediate the triggered pain while relatively sparing the small unmyelinated fibers. This makes balloon compression particularly effective for V1 distribution pain because it has a lower risk of corneal numbness compared to the other percutaneous approaches, and it is well-suited for elderly patients.

Second, glycerol rhizotomy. You inject zero point two to zero point four milliliters of anhydrous glycerol into Meckel's cave after confirming position with contrast cisternography. The glycerol is neurolytic and selectively damages the myelinated fibers. This is the least predictable of the three percutaneous procedures in terms of degree and duration of pain relief.

Third, radiofrequency thermocoagulation. This is the most selective of the percutaneous approaches. You advance a temperature-controlled electrode through the foramen ovale and position it adjacent to the specific division of the trigeminal nerve you want to lesion. The patient is briefly awakened during the procedure to confirm the division being stimulated, then re-sedated while you create a thermal lesion at sixty to eighty degrees Celsius for sixty to ninety seconds. The ability to selectively target a single division is the major advantage of this technique.

All percutaneous procedures have initial pain relief rates around ninety percent, but recurrence rates are twenty to fifty percent at five years, which is meaningfully higher than MVD. The best candidates for percutaneous procedures are elderly patients, those with significant medical comorbidities precluding craniotomy, and patients who want a quick procedure and accept the trade-off of numbness and higher recurrence.

Now synthesize all of this into the treatment algorithm, because this is what the examiner really wants to hear. For a young, healthy patient with Type 1 trigeminal neuralgia and identified vascular compression on MRI, MVD is the answer. It offers the best long-term cure rate and the lowest recurrence, and it preserves sensation. This patient in our case, a fifty-eight-year-old with medication-refractory Type 1 TN and SCA compression confirmed on imaging, is an ideal MVD candidate.

For an elderly patient or one who is medically unfit for craniotomy, you offer either a percutaneous procedure, which provides immediate relief, or Gamma Knife, which is non-invasive but has delayed onset. The choice between these two depends on how urgently the patient needs relief and their ability to tolerate the waiting period for radiosurgery to take effect.

For MS-associated trigeminal neuralgia, the algorithm shifts. MVD is less effective because the underlying pathology is demyelination at the root entry zone, not vascular compression. Pain-free rates after MVD in MS patients drop to fifty to sixty percent compared to ninety percent or higher for classic TN. Gamma Knife and percutaneous procedures are reasonable first-line surgical options in this population, though some surgeons do advocate for MVD in MS patients when MRI demonstrates clear vascular compression in addition to the demyelinating plaque.

For Type 2 TN, atypical trigeminal neuralgia, all procedures have lower success rates. You must counsel patients that their constant background pain component is less likely to resolve regardless of the intervention chosen. Setting expectations is critical.

Now let's hit the trap questions, because these will separate the candidates who pass from those who don't.

Trap one. A thirty-year-old presents with bilateral trigeminal neuralgia. What should you consider? The answer is multiple sclerosis, full stop. Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia in a young patient is MS until proven otherwise. You get an MRI of the brain with contrast and the spinal cord looking for demyelinating plaques. You should also consider other secondary causes of bilateral facial pain, but MS is the diagnosis that the examiner wants you to say immediately and without hesitation.

Trap two. You perform MVD and find no vascular compression at the root entry zone. What do you do? This happens in five to ten percent of MVD cases. Your options are to perform an internal neurolysis or a partial sensory rhizotomy of the nerve, or to close without further intervention and reconsider the diagnosis and alternative treatments postoperatively. Document your findings meticulously. Some surgeons advocate for placing Teflon along the nerve even without an identified compressive vessel, as there may have been venous compression that was relieved when CSF was drained during the approach. The critical point is that you should not panic and you should not make reckless decisions intraoperatively. Either perform a controlled rhizotomy if you are comfortable with the technique, or close and regroup.

Trap three. A patient develops constant burning facial pain and numbness after a percutaneous procedure. What is this? This is anesthesia dolorosa, a deafferentation pain syndrome. The patient has constant, burning, often agonizing pain in an area that is objectively numb on examination. It is a devastating complication that is extremely difficult to treat. There is no reliable intervention for established anesthesia dolorosa. This complication is the reason why sensation-preserving MVD is preferred over destructive percutaneous procedures in young patients. Any procedure that intentionally destroys trigeminal nerve fibers carries the risk of anesthesia dolorosa, and patients must be counseled about this before undergoing a percutaneous procedure.

Let me round out the complications by procedure to make sure you have this organized in your mind. MVD carries risks of hearing loss, CSF leak, cranial nerve palsy, cerebellar injury from retraction, and aseptic meningitis. Gamma Knife can cause delayed facial numbness and rare radiation-related complications. Percutaneous procedures carry expected facial numbness, corneal numbness and keratitis when V1 is treated, masseter weakness from motor root injury, anesthesia dolorosa, carotid puncture during needle placement, and very rarely intracranial hemorrhage.

Turning to the evidence that supports this approach. The landmark long-term MVD outcomes data comes from the Barker 1996 series out of Johns Hopkins, which is Peter Jannetta's institutional series. That study demonstrated seventy percent pain-free rates at ten years following MVD, establishing it as the durable gold standard surgical treatment. The Gamma Knife literature is anchored by the Regis series, which established the dosing parameters and long-term outcomes for radiosurgical treatment. Kanpolat published the largest radiofrequency rhizotomy series, validating the technique and defining the expected outcomes and complication rates. The American Academy of Neurology and the European Federation of Neurological Societies have published joint guidelines on the management of trigeminal neuralgia that support carbamazepine as first-line medical therapy with strong evidence and recommend surgical intervention for medically refractory cases. The Burchiel classification of facial pain is the framework you should use when categorizing patients with facial pain syndromes. It distinguishes Type 1 TN, Type 2 TN, trigeminal neuropathic pain, trigeminal deafferentation pain, symptomatic TN from MS or tumor, postherpetic neuralgia, and atypical facial pain. Knowing this classification demonstrates to the examiner that you think systematically about facial pain.

Closing with the board pearls you need to have locked in. Carbamazepine is the first-line medical treatment for trigeminal neuralgia, and response to carbamazepine is nearly diagnostic. MVD is the definitive surgical cure with the highest long-term pain-free rate and the lowest recurrence. Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia in a young patient equals multiple sclerosis until proven otherwise. Never miss a tumor on MRI, which is why you get thin-cut posterior fossa sequences on every patient. The Obersteiner-Redlich zone, the transition between central and peripheral myelin, is the site of vulnerability. The superior cerebellar artery is the most common offending vessel. Anesthesia dolorosa is the complication you fear most and the reason you prefer MVD over destructive procedures in young patients. Type 2 TN responds less well to everything, and you must set expectations. HLA-B star fifteen oh two testing before carbamazepine in patients of Asian descent. And in MS-associated TN, the pathology is demyelination, not compression, which is why MVD is less effective and radiosurgery or percutaneous procedures are preferred.
