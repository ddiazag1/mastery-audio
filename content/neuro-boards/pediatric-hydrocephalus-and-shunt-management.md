Let's master pediatric hydrocephalus and shunt management for the oral boards.

No topic in pediatric neurosurgery generates more examination questions or more real clinical work. The examiner will almost certainly give you a child with a shunt and a headache, and the entire case will turn on whether you approach shunt malfunction systematically and whether you know the failure modes. Let's build the physiology, the etiologies, and then the algorithm that must be automatic.

Start with cerebrospinal fluid physiology. Production is roughly twenty milliliters per hour, or about five hundred milliliters per day in an adult, predominantly by the choroid plexus, with a meaningful extrachoroidal contribution. Total volume is roughly one hundred fifty milliliters in an adult, far less in an infant, meaning the fluid turns over several times daily. The classical circulation runs from the lateral ventricles through the foramina of Monro to the third ventricle, through the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, out the foramina of Luschka and Magendie into the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed at the arachnoid granulations into the dural venous sinuses, with additional absorption through lymphatic pathways along cranial nerve sheaths, which is increasingly recognized. Importantly, production is largely independent of intracranial pressure, which is why hydrocephalus does not self correct.

Now the classification. Communicating hydrocephalus means cerebrospinal fluid can exit the ventricular system but is inadequately absorbed or the flow is obstructed in the subarachnoid space, as after hemorrhage or meningitis. Non-communicating or obstructive hydrocephalus means flow is blocked within the ventricular system, as in aqueductal stenosis or a posterior fossa mass. This distinction determines whether endoscopic third ventriculostomy is an option, so make it explicitly.

Know the localization rule from the ventricular pattern. Dilated lateral ventricles with a normal third and fourth ventricle means the block is at the foramina of Monro. Dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth ventricle localizes to the aqueduct, which is the classic aqueductal stenosis pattern. Dilatation of all four ventricles means a communicating process or an obstruction at the fourth ventricular outlets. The examiner will show you a scan and expect you to localize in one sentence.

Now the pediatric etiologies by age. In the neonate, the leading causes are post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus of prematurity following germinal matrix and intraventricular hemorrhage, congenital aqueductal stenosis, myelomeningocele with Chiari two malformation, Dandy Walker malformation, and congenital infection. In infants and children, add post-infectious hydrocephalus following meningitis, tumors particularly in the posterior fossa, and arachnoid cysts. Know that X linked hydrocephalus from an L1CAM mutation classically features aqueductal stenosis with adducted thumbs.

Now the clinical presentation, which differs fundamentally with age because of the open fontanelle. In an infant with open sutures, the skull expands, so you see an accelerating head circumference crossing percentile lines, a full and tense fontanelle, split sutures, dilated scalp veins, a setting sun sign from upgaze paresis due to pressure on the tectum, irritability, poor feeding, vomiting, and increased tone. Serial head circumference measurement plotted on a growth chart is your single most valuable tool, and you should say that. In an older child with fused sutures, the presentation is classical raised intracranial pressure: headache that is worse in the morning and on lying flat, vomiting, papilledema, sixth nerve palsy as a false localizing sign, diplopia, declining school performance, and lethargy.

For imaging, in infants with an open fontanelle, ultrasound is the first line, radiation free and repeatable at the bedside. Beyond that, use rapid sequence or limited magnetic resonance protocols specifically designed to avoid radiation and sedation, which have largely replaced serial computed tomography for shunt surveillance in children. This is an important answer: a child with a shunt may have dozens of imaging studies over a lifetime, and cumulative radiation is a genuine harm, so say that you would use rapid magnetic resonance sequences where available. Full magnetic resonance is used to define the anatomy, identify the obstruction, and evaluate for tumor, and cine phase contrast imaging can demonstrate aqueductal flow.

Now treatment. The two options are cerebrospinal fluid shunting and endoscopic third ventriculostomy.

Endoscopic third ventriculostomy creates a stoma in the floor of the third ventricle between the infundibular recess and the mamillary bodies, allowing cerebrospinal fluid to bypass an obstruction into the interpeduncular cistern. Its enormous advantage is that it avoids hardware entirely, eliminating shunt infection and shunt dependence. It requires a communicating pathway distally, so it works for obstructive hydrocephalus and fails in communicating hydrocephalus with impaired absorption.

Patient selection is predicted by the Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy Success Score, which assigns points based on age, etiology, and whether there is a previous shunt. The key principles are that success improves with older age, is poor in infants under six months and particularly under one month, and is best for aqueductal stenosis and tectal tumors while being poor for post-hemorrhagic and post-infectious hydrocephalus. Adding choroid plexus cauterization improves success in infants, an approach developed in resource limited settings and now used more widely. Complications include injury to the basilar artery or its perforators, which can be catastrophic, injury to the hypothalamus and fornix, memory disturbance, diabetes insipidus, bleeding, and late stoma closure, which can present with sudden deterioration and even death, so families must be counseled that late failure can be abrupt.

Shunting, most commonly ventriculoperitoneal, remains the workhorse. Alternatives when the peritoneum is unusable include ventriculoatrial and ventriculopleural shunts, with the gallbladder and other sites as salvage options. Valves come as fixed pressure, programmable, and with antisiphon or gravitational devices to combat overdrainage in the upright position. Programmable valves allow noninvasive pressure adjustment, at the cost of expense and the need to check and reset the setting after exposure to strong magnetic fields, which is an important practical point with magnetic resonance imaging.

Now the central examination scenario: the child with a shunt who presents unwell. Assume shunt malfunction until proven otherwise. This is the sentence to say first, because shunt failure is common and deterioration can be rapid, particularly in a shunt dependent child with slit ventricles.

Work it systematically. History: what are the child's baseline and their previous malfunction symptoms, because families are often exquisitely accurate and a parent saying this is how it looked last time is powerful data. When was the shunt placed and revised, what type of valve and setting, and what is the shunt series history. Examination: level of consciousness, fontanelle in an infant, head circumference, fundoscopy for papilledema, eye movements for sixth nerve palsy and upgaze paresis, and palpation of the shunt tract for the reservoir, for disconnection, and for fluid tracking along the tubing. Pumping the reservoir is traditional but unreliable, and you should say so rather than rely on it.

Investigations: a shunt series of plain radiographs covering skull, neck, chest, and abdomen to look for disconnection, fracture, migration, and the position of the distal catheter, and cranial imaging with rapid sequence magnetic resonance or ultrasound compared against the child's own baseline scan. Comparison with a prior study is essential, because absolute ventricular size is unhelpful: a child can be in florid shunt failure with barely changed ventricles if the ventricles are non-compliant, which is the slit ventricle situation. Unchanged ventricles do not exclude malfunction, and saying that explicitly is a strong answer.

If imaging is equivocal and suspicion remains, options include a shunt tap to measure opening pressure, assess flow, and obtain fluid for cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain, and culture; a radionuclide shunt patency study; and ultimately surgical exploration. A shunt tap is also the definitive diagnostic step when infection is suspected. Never let a normal looking scan overrule a convincing clinical picture; explore the shunt.

Know the failure modes and their typical timing. Proximal catheter obstruction by choroid plexus or debris is the most common cause overall. Distal obstruction from a pseudocyst, adhesions, or omental wrapping. Disconnection or fracture of the tubing, which occurs with growth and with calcification and tethering of the catheter, most often at the connector or in the neck. Migration and displacement, again with growth, and this is why distal catheter length must accommodate growth. Valve failure. Overdrainage, producing low pressure headaches, subdural hygromas and hematomas, slit ventricle syndrome, and in infants premature closure of sutures and craniosynostosis. Loculated or isolated compartments, particularly a trapped fourth ventricle after post-hemorrhagic or post-infectious hydrocephalus, requiring dedicated drainage.

Shunt infection deserves its own treatment. It occurs in roughly five to ten percent of procedures, most commonly within the first few months after surgery, with the dominant organisms being skin flora, chiefly coagulase negative staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus, with gram negatives more common in the presence of abdominal pathology or in neonates. Risk is higher in neonates, premature infants, and after myelomeningocele repair. Presentation may be fever, shunt tract erythema and tenderness, abdominal pain and peritonitis, malfunction, or simply an unwell child. Diagnosis rests on culture of fluid obtained from the shunt, since peripheral blood cultures are frequently negative. Standard management is complete removal of the hardware with externalization via an external ventricular drain, culture directed intravenous antibiotics, with vancomycin plus a gram negative agent as empiric therapy pending culture, and reimplantation of a new shunt after the cerebrospinal fluid is sterile. Attempting to treat an infected shunt with antibiotics alone while leaving hardware in place has a high failure rate. Prevention bundles, standardizing skin preparation, double gloving, limiting operating room traffic, minimizing shunt handling, and using antibiotic impregnated catheters, have demonstrably reduced infection rates and are worth naming.

Two further syndromes to know. Slit ventricle syndrome describes a shunt dependent child with chronically overdrained, small, stiff ventricles who develops intermittent severe headaches with a shunt that intermittently obstructs because the ventricular walls collapse onto the catheter. Management includes valve upgrading to a higher pressure or programmable valve, antisiphon devices, and in refractory cases subtemporal decompression or cranial expansion. Shunt dependency and the risk of rapid decompensation should be communicated to every family.

Finally, address the lifetime perspective. A child shunted in infancy faces a substantial cumulative revision burden, with a large fraction requiring revision within the first two years and many requiring multiple revisions over decades. Outcomes depend heavily on the underlying etiology and on associated brain injury rather than on the hydrocephalus alone. Transition to adult care, documentation of hardware type and settings, and giving the family a shunt card with the valve type and setting are practical answers that demonstrate real world competence.

Know the hardware, because the examiner may ask you to choose a valve and justify it. A shunt has three components: a proximal ventricular catheter, a valve, and a distal catheter. Differential pressure valves open at a fixed pressure, classified as low, medium or high, and their weakness is siphoning: when the patient stands, the hydrostatic column in the distal catheter generates negative pressure and drives overdrainage. Antisiphon devices and gravitational units counteract this by adding resistance in the upright position. Programmable valves allow the opening pressure to be adjusted noninvasively with an external magnet, which avoids reoperation for pressure adjustment but costs more and, critically, may be reset by strong magnetic fields, so the setting must be checked and documented after every magnetic resonance scan. Flow regulated valves aim to hold flow constant across a range of pressures. Antibiotic impregnated and silver coated catheters reduce infection in several trials and registries and are widely adopted.

Add the details of the operation, because they map directly onto the complications. Position supine with the head turned, plan the burr hole site and mark the distal tunnelling course. The commonest ventricular entry points are Kocher point, roughly eleven centimetres posterior to the nasion and three centimetres lateral to the midline, aiming for the ipsilateral frontal horn along a trajectory perpendicular to the skull toward the medial canthus and the tragus; and Frazier or parieto-occipital entry for an occipital approach. Minimise hardware handling and skin contact, use a strict no touch technique, limit theatre traffic, and give perioperative antibiotics. Verify cerebrospinal fluid flow, tunnel the distal catheter subcutaneously, and place a generous length in the peritoneum to accommodate somatic growth, because a catheter that becomes too short with growth is a predictable late failure. Image based or ultrasound guided or endoscopically guided placement improves catheter position, and a well placed catheter tip away from the choroid plexus is the single best predictor of proximal longevity.

Be thorough about the alternatives to the peritoneum. Ventriculoatrial shunts are used when the abdomen is unusable, and carry risks of catheter migration with growth, thrombosis, pulmonary hypertension and shunt nephritis. Ventriculopleural shunts risk effusion and are generally avoided under about age seven because of limited pleural absorptive capacity. Gallbladder and ureteric shunts are salvage options. In post-haemorrhagic hydrocephalus of prematurity, temporising measures precede definitive shunting because the infant is too small and the cerebrospinal fluid protein too high: serial lumbar punctures, a ventricular access device or reservoir, a ventriculosubgaleal shunt, or an external ventricular drain, with conversion to a permanent shunt once weight and fluid characteristics allow, conventionally around one and a half to two kilograms.

Expand the malfunction workup. The clinical picture varies with age and with the child's own baseline, and the single best historical question is whether this looks like previous malfunctions to the family. Examine for level of consciousness, fontanelle tension, head circumference trajectory, papilloedema, sixth nerve palsy, upgaze paresis producing the setting sun sign, and swelling or tracking of fluid along the shunt tract. Obtain a shunt series of plain films covering skull, neck, chest and abdomen to detect disconnection, fracture, migration, coiling and distal catheter position, and obtain cranial imaging compared against the child's own baseline, using rapid sequence magnetic resonance or ultrasound in preference to repeated computed tomography because cumulative radiation across a lifetime of shunt imaging is a genuine harm. Say explicitly that unchanged ventricular size does not exclude malfunction, because non-compliant slit ventricles may not dilate. If suspicion persists despite equivocal imaging, proceed to a shunt tap to measure opening pressure, assess flow and sample fluid, to a radionuclide patency study, or directly to surgical exploration. Exploring a working shunt is a far smaller error than observing a failing one.

Give infection the depth it deserves. Incidence is roughly five to ten percent per procedure, concentrated in the first few months after surgery, and is higher in neonates, in premature infants, after myelomeningocele repair, and with repeated revisions. The organisms are predominantly skin flora, chiefly coagulase negative staphylococci and Staphylococcus aureus, with gram negatives and Propionibacterium in specific contexts. Presentation may be fever, shunt tract erythema, abdominal pain and pseudocyst, malfunction, or simply an unwell child; in ventriculoatrial shunts, chronic bacteraemia can produce shunt nephritis. Diagnosis rests on culture of cerebrospinal fluid obtained from the shunt, since peripheral blood cultures are often negative. Standard management is complete hardware removal with externalisation, culture directed intravenous antibiotics with empiric vancomycin plus a gram negative agent, and reimplantation once the fluid is sterile. Antibiotics alone with retained hardware fail frequently. Prevention bundles standardising skin preparation, double gloving, restricting theatre traffic, minimising handling and using impregnated catheters have measurably reduced rates.

Cover the pressure related syndromes. Overdrainage produces postural headache, subdural hygroma and haematoma, slit ventricles, and in infants premature suture fusion and secondary craniosynostosis with microcephaly. Slit ventricle syndrome describes intermittent severe headache in a shunt dependent child with small stiff ventricles whose walls intermittently occlude the catheter; management escalates from valve upgrade and antisiphon devices through subtemporal decompression to cranial expansion. A trapped or isolated fourth ventricle follows post-haemorrhagic or post-infectious hydrocephalus when the aqueduct and outlets both occlude, presents with posterior fossa signs, and requires a dedicated fourth ventricular catheter or endoscopic aqueductoplasty. Loculated multicompartment hydrocephalus after ventriculitis may need endoscopic fenestration to create a single communicating compartment rather than multiple shunts.

Close with the lifetime view, because it is what families most need. Roughly half of shunts placed in infancy fail within two years, and most shunted children undergo multiple revisions over decades. Outcome is determined far more by the underlying aetiology and any associated brain injury than by the hydrocephalus itself. Give every family a shunt card recording the valve type, setting and date, teach them the warning signs, arrange structured transition to adult services, and document the hardware clearly, because the adult neurosurgeon meeting this patient in thirty years will depend on that record.

To summarize. Localize the obstruction from the ventricular pattern. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy for obstructive hydrocephalus in older children, poor under six months, augmented by choroid plexus cauterization in infants. Shunt otherwise. In any shunted child who is unwell, assume malfunction, take the family's history seriously, obtain a shunt series and imaging compared against the child's own baseline, remember that unchanged ventricles do not exclude failure, and tap or explore when suspicion persists. Treat infection by removing the hardware, not by antibiotics alone. And counsel every family that shunts fail and that late failure can be sudden.
