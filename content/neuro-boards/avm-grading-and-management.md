Welcome to this viva-style teaching session on arteriovenous malformation grading and management. This is one of the bread-and-butter vascular topics for the oral boards, and it consistently appears because it tests your ability to integrate anatomy, pathophysiology, grading systems, treatment algorithms, and landmark trial data into a coherent management plan. Let's build this from the ground up.

An arteriovenous malformation is a congenital vascular lesion consisting of a tangle of abnormal vessels, called the nidus, that directly shunt blood from arterial feeders to draining veins without an intervening capillary bed. This is the fundamental pathologic feature. The absence of a capillary network means there is no resistance bed, so the draining veins are exposed to arterial pressures. Over time, this produces arterialization of the veins, flow-related aneurysms on feeding arteries, and progressive recruitment of additional feeders. The nidus itself is a meshwork of dysplastic vessels with thin, fragile walls, and this is what makes the lesion dangerous.

The angioarchitecture of an AVM has three critical components that you must evaluate on every angiogram. First, the feeding arteries. These can be pial, meaning they arise from cortical branches, or they can be direct perforating feeders from deep vessels like lenticulostriates or choroidal arteries. The distinction matters because perforating feeders are difficult to access surgically and increase operative risk. Second, the nidus. You need to characterize its size, its morphology, whether it is compact or diffuse, and whether it contains intranidal aneurysms. Intranidal aneurysms are weak points that are associated with a higher hemorrhage risk. Third, the draining veins. You must determine whether drainage is superficial, meaning into cortical veins and then into a dural sinus, or deep, meaning into the internal cerebral veins, basal veins of Rosenthal, or the vein of Galen. Deep venous drainage is associated with higher surgical morbidity and is a key grading variable.

Now, the natural history. The overall annual hemorrhage rate for an unruptured AVM is approximately two to four percent per year. Once an AVM has bled, the annual re-hemorrhage rate increases, with estimates ranging from six to eighteen percent in the first year after rupture, then settling back toward baseline. Over a lifetime, the cumulative risk is substantial, especially for a young patient. Features associated with a higher hemorrhage risk include prior hemorrhage, deep venous drainage, deep or infratentorial location, associated aneurysms (both intranidal and flow-related on feeding pedicles), and single draining vein with venous outflow restriction.

Presentation patterns. AVMs present in several ways. Hemorrhage accounts for roughly fifty percent of presentations and is the most dangerous. Seizures account for about twenty to twenty-five percent. Headache, often migrainous, accounts for about fifteen percent. Progressive neurological deficit from vascular steal is less common but real, particularly with large, high-flow lesions. Some AVMs are discovered incidentally on imaging obtained for unrelated reasons, and this is increasingly common in the era of widespread MRI use.

Let's now define the grading systems precisely, because the examiner will test every detail. The Spetzler-Martin grading system assigns points based on three variables. Size of the nidus: less than three centimeters is one point, three to six centimeters is two points, and greater than six centimeters is three points. Eloquence of adjacent brain: non-eloquent cortex is zero points, eloquent cortex is one point. Eloquent regions include sensorimotor cortex, language areas, visual cortex, hypothalamus, thalamus, internal capsule, brainstem, cerebellar peduncles, and deep cerebellar nuclei. Venous drainage pattern: exclusively superficial drainage is zero points, any deep venous drainage is one point. The total score ranges from one to five. A Grade I AVM is small, in non-eloquent cortex, with superficial drainage only. A Grade V is large, in eloquent cortex, with deep drainage. Grade VI was later added by Spetzler to denote an inoperable lesion, essentially any AVM where surgical cure would produce unacceptable morbidity. In practice, Grade I and II AVMs have excellent surgical outcomes with morbidity and mortality rates of zero to three percent. Grade III is the controversial middle ground. Grade IV and V AVMs carry surgical risks that generally exceed the natural history risk.

The supplementary Lawton-Young grading system was developed precisely to help with Grade III decision-making. It assigns points based on three additional variables. Patient age: less than twenty years is one point, twenty to forty years is two points, and greater than forty years is three points. Hemorrhage presentation: yes, the AVM has ruptured, is one point; no hemorrhage is zero points. Nidus diffuseness: compact is one point, diffuse is two points. A compact nidus has clear angiographic margins and a definable surgical plane. A diffuse nidus has indistinct margins, interposed normal brain, and is harder to resect completely. The supplementary score ranges from two to six. When you combine the Spetzler-Martin score with the supplementary score, the combined score ranges from three to eleven. Lower combined scores favor surgical treatment, and higher combined scores favor conservative management. For Grade III AVMs specifically, a supplementary score of two or three, meaning a young patient, with hemorrhage, and a compact nidus, makes surgery reasonable. A supplementary score of five or six tips the balance toward observation or radiosurgery.

Moving to the clinical case. A twenty-eight-year-old right-handed man presents to the emergency department after a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure at work. He has no prior seizure history, no significant past medical history, and takes no medications. His neurological examination is non-focal. A CT head is unremarkable. An MRI of the brain with and without contrast reveals a three-centimeter tangle of flow voids in the left temporal lobe, lateral and posterior to the temporal horn. There is no evidence of hemorrhage on susceptibility-weighted imaging. The lesion enhances avidly and has prominent draining veins coursing superficially toward the transverse sinus. There are no associated aneurysms seen on MRA. The surrounding brain appears normal without gliosis. His seizure is controlled with levetiracetam, and he is neurologically intact on your evaluation. You are consulted for further management.

Now let's enter the examiner interrogation portion. The examiner begins.

"Doctor, tell me your initial workup for this patient."

Your workup starts with the MRI you already have, which gives you structural information about the nidus location, size, and relationship to eloquent structures. You need a formal cerebral angiogram, a six-vessel digital subtraction angiography, which is the gold standard for AVM evaluation. DSA will define the feeding arteries, the nidus angioarchitecture, the draining veins, the transit time, and any associated aneurysms. You also need functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging tractography to map the relationship of the nidus to language cortex and the optic radiations, given the left temporal location in a right-handed patient.

"You get the angiogram. There are two feeding pedicles from the left middle cerebral artery, one from a temporal branch of the left posterior cerebral artery. Drainage is entirely superficial into a cortical vein that empties into the transverse sinus. The nidus is compact, measuring three centimeters. No aneurysms. Grade this AVM for me."

Spetzler-Martin grading: size is three centimeters, which is at the boundary, so that is one point since the nidus is not greater than three centimeters. Actually, let me be precise. Three centimeters falls into the less-than-three-centimeter category only if it is strictly less than three. At exactly three centimeters, it could be scored as one or two depending on how you measure. For the boards, I would call this one point if it is three centimeters or less, two points if the maximum dimension crosses three centimeters. Let me score it as one point, since the nidus measures three centimeters. The left temporal lobe in a right-handed patient contains Wernicke's area, so this is eloquent cortex, one point. Drainage is exclusively superficial, zero points. Spetzler-Martin Grade II. For the supplementary Lawton-Young score: age twenty-eight, that falls in the twenty-to-forty range, two points. No hemorrhage, zero points. Compact nidus, one point. Supplementary score is three. Combined score is five, which favors intervention.

"Would you treat this AVM?"

Yes. This is a Spetzler-Martin Grade II AVM with a favorable supplementary score of three in a young patient with a long life expectancy and cumulative hemorrhage risk. The surgical outcomes for Grade II AVMs are excellent, with complete obliteration rates exceeding ninety-five percent and major morbidity rates of zero to three percent in experienced hands. I would recommend microsurgical resection.

"Would you embolize before surgery?"

Preoperative embolization can be a useful adjunct for Grade II and III AVMs. The goal is to reduce flow, occlude deep feeding pedicles that are difficult to reach early in surgery, and make the resection safer. In this case, the PCA feeder is the one I would consider targeting with embolization because the temporal branch from the PCA may be deeper and harder to access surgically compared to the MCA pedicles. I would use embolization selectively, not to attempt a cure, but as a surgical adjunct. I would schedule surgery within one to two days of embolization to minimize recanalization and inflammatory response.

"What embolic agents would you use?"

The two main agents are Onyx, which is an ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer, and n-butyl cyanoacrylate, or NBCA. Onyx has the advantage of being non-adhesive and allowing a slow, controlled injection with deeper nidus penetration. The disadvantage is that Onyx creates a hard cast that can make surgical dissection more difficult. NBCA polymerizes on contact with blood, giving faster occlusion, but it is adhesive and requires more operator experience to control. For a preoperative adjunct, either is acceptable. I tend to prefer Onyx for its controlled delivery, but I defer to the neurointerventionalist's preference and experience.

"Let's say you decide to take this to surgery. Walk me through your approach."

I position the patient supine with the head turned to the right, resting on a horseshoe or in pins. I plan a left temporal craniotomy centered over the nidus using neuronavigation. I administer dexamethasone, mannitol if needed for relaxation, and ensure antiepileptic levels are therapeutic. After opening the dura, I identify the cortical draining vein early but I do not sacrifice it. This is critical. You must preserve the draining vein until the very end of the resection. Premature sacrifice of the draining vein will cause the AVM to swell, hemorrhage, and become unmanageable. I then circumferentially dissect around the nidus, starting at the surface and working toward the depth. I identify and coagulate feeding arteries as I encounter them, always distinguishing feeders from en passage arteries. En passage arteries supply normal brain beyond the AVM and must not be sacrificed. I use a combination of bipolar coagulation and microsurgical clips on the feeders. As I progressively devascularize the nidus, it will begin to shrink and become easier to mobilize. Once all feeders are divided, the nidus is supplied only through retrograde flow from the draining vein. At that point, I coagulate and divide the draining vein and remove the specimen. I obtain an intraoperative DSA or indocyanine green angiography to confirm complete resection. I achieve meticulous hemostasis and close.

"What is normal perfusion pressure breakthrough, and are you worried about it?"

Normal perfusion pressure breakthrough is a controversial but testable concept. The theory is that brain parenchyma surrounding a high-flow AVM has been chronically hypoperfused due to vascular steal. The small vessels in this region have lost their capacity for autoregulation. When the AVM is resected and normal perfusion pressure is restored to these territories, the impaired vessels cannot constrict, leading to hyperemia, edema, and potentially hemorrhage in the surrounding brain. The classic presentation is diffuse edema or hemorrhage in the resection bed, not at the resection site itself but in surrounding previously hypoperfused brain. In practice, this is rare and some authorities question whether it is a real phenomenon or simply incomplete resection or venous infarction. Regardless, the board answer is that you should be aware of it, maintain strict blood pressure control postoperatively with a systolic target below one hundred and forty, avoid hypertension, and keep the patient in the ICU with an arterial line for continuous monitoring.

"Here is a trap question. Your colleague shows you a forty-five-year-old patient with an incidentally discovered unruptured three-centimeter AVM in the right frontal lobe. He says, based on the ARUBA trial, he plans to just observe this patient. What do you think?"

I would respectfully disagree with a blanket application of ARUBA to this case. The ARUBA trial, A Randomized Trial of Unruptured Brain AVMs, randomized two hundred and twenty-six patients with unruptured AVMs to medical management alone versus interventional therapy. At a mean follow-up of thirty-three months, the intervention group had a significantly higher rate of stroke or death, with a risk of thirty percent versus ten percent in the medical management group. However, ARUBA has critical limitations that prevent it from being the final word. First, the follow-up of thirty-three months is absurdly short for a disease with a cumulative lifetime risk. A twenty-five-year-old with an AVM has decades of exposure to hemorrhage risk. Second, the interventional arm lumped together surgery, embolization, and radiosurgery, and a significant proportion of patients received embolization alone, which is not curative for most AVMs. Third, the trial included Grade IV and V AVMs, which most surgeons would not operate on anyway, inflating the complication rate in the intervention arm. Fourth, there was significant crossover from the medical arm to the intervention arm. Fifth, many of the participating centers were low-volume. The key takeaway from ARUBA is not that you should never treat unruptured AVMs. The takeaway is that treatment should be thoughtful, selective, and performed at high-volume centers. For Spetzler-Martin Grade I and II unruptured AVMs in young patients, microsurgical resection remains the standard of care, and the cure is complete and immediate.

"Another question. You have a patient with a Spetzler-Martin Grade III AVM. Would you operate?"

Grade III is exactly where the supplementary Lawton-Young grading becomes essential. I would not make a treatment decision based on the Spetzler-Martin grade alone. A Grade III AVM can score three points in many different configurations. A small AVM in eloquent cortex with deep drainage has a very different risk profile than a medium AVM in non-eloquent cortex with superficial drainage. I need to apply the supplementary grading. If this is a young patient, say under twenty, who presented with hemorrhage and has a compact nidus, their supplementary score is two, and the combined score is five. I would recommend surgery. If this is a fifty-year-old who was found incidentally and has a diffuse nidus, their supplementary score is five, combined score is eight, and I would lean toward observation or radiosurgery for a small lesion, or observation alone for a larger one.

"Tell me about radiosurgery for AVMs."

Stereotactic radiosurgery, whether Gamma Knife, CyberKnife, or linear accelerator-based, is an option for AVMs that are less than three centimeters in maximum diameter, particularly when they are located in deep or eloquent locations where surgery carries high risk. The typical marginal dose is twenty to twenty-five Gray delivered in a single fraction. The obliteration rate is approximately eighty percent at three years for appropriately sized lesions. For AVMs less than three centimeters, obliteration rates can reach eighty to ninety percent. For AVMs larger than three centimeters, rates drop significantly. The critical caveat is the latency period. During the two to three years between treatment and obliteration, the patient remains at risk for hemorrhage, and that annual risk does not decrease during the latency period. This means radiosurgery does not protect the patient in the near term. Adverse radiation effects, including radiation necrosis, occur in two to four percent of patients. Repeat radiosurgery can be considered for partial responders after three years. Radiosurgery is generally not the first-line treatment for Grade I or II AVMs where surgery offers an immediate cure with low morbidity.

"During embolization of a feeding pedicle, the patient develops a new neurological deficit. What happened and what do you do?"

The most likely etiology is either occlusion of an en passage vessel, reflux of embolic material into a normal branch, or hemorrhage from venous outflow obstruction if embolic material migrated through the nidus into a draining vein. I immediately stop the embolization procedure. I obtain a control angiographic run to evaluate the situation. If there is vessel occlusion without hemorrhage, I consider whether intraarterial thrombolysis or mechanical retrieval is appropriate, though this is limited if the occlusion is from Onyx or NBCA rather than thrombus. I get an immediate CT to rule out hemorrhage. If there is hemorrhage, the management depends on the size and clinical status. A small, contained hemorrhage may be managed conservatively with blood pressure control and ICU monitoring. A large, expanding hemorrhage with clinical deterioration requires emergent surgical evacuation.

"What if you resect the AVM and the postoperative angiogram shows a small residual nidus?"

This is a critical situation. Any residual AVM carries continued hemorrhage risk, and some data suggest that a partially treated AVM may have a higher hemorrhage risk than an untreated one due to alterations in flow dynamics. If a small residual nidus is identified on intraoperative or immediate postoperative angiography, I return to the operating room for re-resection if the residual is surgically accessible and the patient is clinically stable. If it is not accessible or the patient's clinical status does not permit re-operation, I plan for staged treatment with either delayed surgical re-resection or targeted radiosurgery to the residual nidus. The goal of AVM surgery is always complete obliteration. A subtotal resection is not a success.

Now let's review the evidence in more detail. The ARUBA trial, published in the Lancet in 2014 by Mohr and colleagues, remains the most cited and most controversial trial in AVM management. As I discussed, the thirty-three-month follow-up showed a three-fold higher event rate in the intervention group. The longer-term follow-up data published subsequently showed that the medical management group's event rate continued to climb over time, narrowing the gap, which supports the argument that the initial results reflected early procedural risk without capturing the long-term benefit of AVM cure. The BARBADOS observational extension continues to follow this cohort, and early data suggest convergence of outcomes at longer follow-up. The Spetzler and Ponce classification, published in 2011, simplified the original five-tier system into a three-tier system: Class A includes Grades I and II, recommended for surgery; Class B includes Grade III, requiring further stratification; and Class C includes Grades IV and V, recommended for observation. Spetzler's own published outcomes data for Grade I and II AVMs show surgical morbidity and mortality rates of zero percent and two point nine percent, respectively. For Grade III, morbidity was seven point one percent. For Grades IV and V, combined permanent morbidity exceeded thirty percent. Radiosurgery outcomes data from the Pittsburgh series, the largest single-center experience, demonstrate obliteration rates of seventy-eight to eighty percent at three years for AVMs less than three centimeters treated with marginal doses of twenty Gray or higher. The Karlsson and Lax dose-response models confirm that higher marginal doses achieve higher obliteration rates but at the cost of increased radiation-related complications.

Board pearls. Here are the rapid-fire high-yield points you need locked in. The annual hemorrhage rate for an unruptured AVM is two to four percent per year. After a hemorrhage, the first-year re-hemorrhage rate is six to eighteen percent. The Spetzler-Martin grade is the sum of size plus eloquence plus drainage, scored one to five. The supplementary Lawton-Young grade adds age plus hemorrhage history plus nidus diffuseness, scored two to six. Grade I and II AVMs should be offered microsurgical resection. Grade III AVMs require supplementary grading to guide decision-making. Grade IV and V AVMs are generally observed. Radiosurgery works for lesions less than three centimeters at a marginal dose of twenty to twenty-five Gray with an eighty percent obliteration rate at three years. The latency period is two to three years during which the patient remains at full hemorrhage risk. Embolization alone is rarely curative and serves primarily as a presurgical adjunct. Never sacrifice the draining vein until the nidus is completely devascularized. Normal perfusion pressure breakthrough is managed with strict postoperative blood pressure control. Any residual AVM on postoperative angiography requires re-treatment. ARUBA showed higher event rates with intervention at thirty-three months but has critical limitations that do not justify withholding treatment from low-grade unruptured AVMs in young patients.

Three things that will fail you. First, saying you would observe a Spetzler-Martin Grade I or II AVM in a young patient because of ARUBA. That shows you do not understand the trial's limitations or the natural history data. Second, saying you would operate on a Grade IV or V AVM without a compelling indication like progressive deficit or recurrent hemorrhage refractory to conservative management. That shows poor judgment. Third, failing to mention the supplementary grading system when presented with a Grade III AVM. That is the entire point of Lawton-Young, and the examiner is waiting for you to bring it up.

Never say "I would just watch this AVM" for a low-grade lesion in a young patient. Never say "embolization can cure this AVM" unless you are describing a very rare, small, single-pedicle lesion with a favorable anatomy. Never say "I would take the draining vein early to reduce bleeding." That will kill your patient and your score.

That covers AVM grading and management. You have the pathophysiology, the grading systems, the treatment algorithm, the evidence, and the traps. You're ready for this one.
