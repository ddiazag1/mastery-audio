Let's master cervical disc herniation management for the oral boards.

Cervical disc herniation is one of the most commonly tested spine topics on the ABNS oral examination. The examiner expects you to localize the lesion from history and exam alone, select appropriate imaging, articulate surgical indications with precision, choose the correct operative approach, and handle complications without hesitation. This is a topic where fluency and confidence will carry you. Let's build this systematically.

Start with the anatomy of the cervical disc. The cervical intervertebral disc has two main components: the annulus fibrosus, which is a concentric ring of fibrocartilaginous lamellae that contains the central nucleus pulposus, which is a gelatinous proteoglycan-rich structure responsible for axial load distribution. The annulus is thinnest posteriorly, which is why the majority of herniations occur in the posterolateral direction. Unique to the cervical spine are the uncovertebral joints, also called the joints of Luschka, which are small synovial articulations located at the posterolateral margins of the vertebral bodies from C3 to C7. These joints form the anteromedial border of the neural foramen and are critical because osteophytic hypertrophy of the uncovertebral joints is one of the most common causes of foraminal stenosis and cervical radiculopathy. The posterior longitudinal ligament runs along the posterior aspect of the vertebral bodies within the spinal canal and provides a partial barrier to central disc herniation, though it is weakest laterally, which again favors posterolateral herniation.

Now the localization. This is where the examiner separates the prepared candidate from the unprepared one. You must know the dermatomal and myotomal patterns for each cervical root cold. Remember that in the cervical spine, the nerve root exits above its numbered pedicle, so a C6-7 disc herniation compresses the C7 nerve root.

The C5 nerve root. Motor: deltoid and biceps. Reflex: biceps. Sensory: lateral arm, the regimental badge area. A C5 radiculopathy produces shoulder abduction and elbow flexion weakness with a diminished biceps reflex.

The C6 nerve root. Motor: wrist extensors and brachioradialis. Reflex: brachioradialis. Sensory: lateral forearm, thumb, and index finger. This is the most commonly affected root. The patient has weak wrist extension, a diminished brachioradialis reflex, and thumb numbness.

The C7 nerve root. Motor: triceps, wrist flexors, and finger extensors. Reflex: triceps. Sensory: middle finger. The second most commonly affected root. Triceps weakness, diminished triceps reflex, middle finger numbness.

The C8 nerve root. Motor: finger flexors, grip strength, and hand intrinsics. Reflex: no reliable isolated reflex. Sensory: medial forearm, ring finger, and small finger. Presents with grip weakness and fine motor dysfunction.

The T1 nerve root. Motor: hand intrinsics, thenar and hypothenar musculature. Reflex: none. Sensory: medial arm. T1 radiculopathy is rare from disc herniation. When you see hand intrinsic wasting, consider ulnar neuropathy, Pancoast tumor, and thoracic outlet syndrome before attributing it to a T1 disc.

Now understand the distinction between hard disc and soft disc. A soft disc herniation is an acute extrusion of nucleus pulposus through a torn annulus. It tends to occur in younger patients, presents acutely, and is often lateralized. A hard disc refers to osteophytic spondylotic change, typically uncovertebral or facet joint hypertrophy, that narrows the foramen or canal chronically. Hard discs present more insidiously and are often associated with multilevel spondylosis. This distinction matters surgically because a soft disc can often be addressed with a targeted posterior foraminotomy, whereas a hard disc with anterior osteophyte compression generally requires an anterior approach for direct decompression and removal of the offending osteophyte.

The location of the herniation also matters. A central disc herniation compresses the spinal cord and produces myelopathy. A paracentral disc herniation, which is the most common type, compresses the exiting nerve root and may also cause some cord compression depending on size. A foraminal disc herniation compresses the nerve root within the foramen and produces pure radiculopathy without myelopathy.

The natural history of cervical radiculopathy is favorable. Seventy-five to ninety percent of patients with cervical radiculopathy from disc herniation will improve with conservative management alone, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, activity modification, and cervical epidural steroid injections. This is an important number for the boards because it frames your surgical indications. You do not operate on everyone with a cervical disc herniation. You operate on the subset that fails conservative care or has progressive neurological deficit.

Now the myelopathy signs. When the disc herniates centrally and compresses the spinal cord, you see upper motor neuron findings below the level of the lesion and potentially lower motor neuron findings at the level. The classic findings include hyperreflexia in the lower extremities, the Hoffmann sign, which is involuntary flexion of the thumb and index finger when you flick the distal phalanx of the middle finger, a positive Babinski sign, gait ataxia with a wide-based shuffling gait, and myelopathy hand, which is characterized by loss of power grip, difficulty with fine motor tasks such as buttoning a shirt, and the finger escape sign where the ulnar digits spontaneously abduct when the patient tries to hold the fingers together in extension. Lhermitte sign, an electric shock sensation radiating down the spine with neck flexion, is another myelopathic sign. The key point about cervical myelopathy is that it rarely improves spontaneously and typically follows a stepwise deterioration. This makes myelopathy a surgical indication even when mild.

Moving to the clinical case.

You are consulted on a forty-eight-year-old right-hand-dominant man who presents with three months of progressive right arm pain in a C7 distribution, radiating from the neck into the posterior arm and into the middle finger. He was initially managed with physical therapy for six weeks and a course of oral naproxen without improvement. Over the past two weeks he has developed right triceps weakness. On exam, his triceps strength is four out of five on the right, his triceps reflex is diminished on the right compared to the left, and he has decreased sensation to light touch over the dorsum of the right hand and the middle finger. His Hoffmann sign is negative bilaterally. Gait is normal. MRI of the cervical spine shows a right paracentral disc herniation at C6-7 compressing the right C7 nerve root with mild thecal sac effacement but no cord signal change.

This patient has a right C7 radiculopathy from a C6-7 disc herniation with a progressive motor deficit that has failed adequate conservative care. He is a surgical candidate. Let's go through the examiner interrogation.

Examiner: "Walk me through your history and exam for the boards."

Your answer: On history, I ask about onset, duration, and dermatomal distribution of pain, any inciting event, and progression. I specifically screen for myelopathic symptoms: gait difficulty, balance problems, hand clumsiness, difficulty with buttons, and urinary urgency. I confirm prior treatments were given an adequate trial. On exam, I test each myotome individually, check reflexes including biceps, brachioradialis, triceps, patellar, and Achilles, test dermatomal sensation, perform Hoffmann and Babinski testing, assess gait, and perform the Spurling maneuver, which reproduces radicular pain with ipsilateral head rotation, lateral flexion, and axial compression. A positive Spurling test is highly specific for cervical radiculopathy.

Examiner: "What imaging do you obtain?"

Your answer: MRI of the cervical spine without contrast is the gold standard initial study. It demonstrates the disc herniation, the degree of neural compression, cord signal change suggesting myelomalacia, and it evaluates for other pathology. If the patient cannot undergo MRI due to a pacemaker or other contraindication, or if there is a contrast allergy precluding gadolinium-enhanced studies, I obtain a CT myelogram, which provides excellent spatial resolution and is actually superior to MRI for delineating bony anatomy and differentiating osteophyte from soft disc. CT myelogram remains the gold standard for bony detail. I also obtain flexion-extension lateral cervical radiographs to evaluate for dynamic instability, defined as greater than 3.5 millimeters of translation or greater than eleven degrees of angular change between adjacent segments on the lateral view using White and Panjabi criteria. Standing anteroposterior and lateral cervical spine films evaluate overall alignment, disc height, and the presence of spondylosis.

Examiner: "What are your surgical indications for cervical disc herniation?"

Your answer: There are four primary surgical indications. First, progressive motor deficit, as in this patient with worsening triceps weakness. A patient who is getting weaker needs surgery and should not continue to wait. Second, myelopathy, even if mild. Cervical spondylotic myelopathy does not reliably improve without surgery and typically follows a stepwise decline. The examiner expects you to operate on myelopathy. Third, intractable radicular pain that has failed an adequate trial of conservative management, which is generally defined as six to twelve weeks of appropriate nonsurgical treatment. Fourth, significant functional limitation that prevents the patient from performing their daily activities or occupation despite conservative care.

Examiner: "You decide to proceed with surgery. What operation do you perform?"

Your answer: For this patient with a single-level right paracentral C6-7 disc herniation, the workhorse operation is anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, or ACDF. This is the Smith-Robinson approach. I perform a transverse skin incision on the left side of the neck at the C6-7 level. I approach from the left because the recurrent laryngeal nerve on the left has a consistent course in the tracheoesophageal groove, ascending from the aortic arch to the larynx. On the right side, the recurrent laryngeal nerve has a more variable and nonrecurrent course in up to one percent of patients, where it comes directly off the vagus nerve and crosses the operative field, placing it at higher risk of injury. For this reason, many spine surgeons prefer a left-sided approach for primary ACDF.

I develop the plane between the carotid sheath laterally and the trachea and esophagus medially. I confirm the correct level with intraoperative fluoroscopy. I place self-retaining retractors and perform a complete discectomy, removing the annulus, nucleus, and any osteophytic material compressing the nerve root. I decompress the neural foramen bilaterally using a Kerrison rongeur or a high-speed burr. I prepare the endplates by removing the cartilaginous endplate down to bleeding subchondral bone, taking care not to violate the bony endplate, which would promote cage subsidence. I select an appropriately sized interbody cage, typically PEEK or titanium, packed with demineralized bone matrix or local autograft. I then apply an anterior cervical plate with fixed or variable angle screws into the C6 and C7 vertebral bodies. Single-level ACDF fusion rates exceed ninety-five percent in the literature.

Examiner: "When would you choose a posterior approach instead?"

Your answer: I would choose a posterior cervical foraminotomy, also called a keyhole foraminotomy, for a lateral or foraminal soft disc herniation causing pure radiculopathy without instability and without significant anterior compression. The advantage is motion preservation because you are not fusing the segment. This approach works best for soft discs, not hard discs with ventral osteophytic compression, because a posterior approach does not directly address anterior pathology. For multilevel disease with myelopathy, I would choose a posterior approach if there are three or more levels involved. The options include posterior cervical laminectomy with lateral mass fusion using screws and rods, or cervical laminoplasty, which is an expansile procedure that opens the canal without fusion and preserves some motion. The critical caveat with posterior approaches for myelopathy is that cervical lordosis must be preserved. If the patient has a kyphotic cervical spine, a posterior decompression alone will fail because the cord will remain draped over the anterior pathology. In that scenario, you must perform an anterior approach, either multilevel ACDF or corpectomy with fusion, to directly decompress the cord and restore lordosis.

Examiner: "What about cervical disc replacement? Would you consider arthroplasty for this patient?"

Your answer: Yes, this patient is a candidate for cervical disc arthroplasty. The indications for single-level cervical disc replacement include symptomatic single-level or two-level disc herniation or spondylosis causing radiculopathy or myelopathy in a patient with no significant facet arthrosis at the index level, no instability, no ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, and preserved segmental motion. The advantages of arthroplasty over ACDF are motion preservation at the operated segment and a theoretical reduction in adjacent segment disease. The Prestige ST trial, published with seven-year follow-up data, demonstrated that cervical disc replacement was non-inferior to ACDF for overall success and superior on some secondary endpoints including neurological success and the rate of subsequent surgery at the index level and adjacent levels. The Mobi-C trial, which was the first FDA IDE trial to study two-level arthroplasty, showed that two-level disc replacement was statistically superior to two-level ACDF at seven years for overall success, with lower rates of adjacent segment disease and reoperation. Contraindications to arthroplasty include significant facet arthrosis at the index level, instability, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, severe osteoporosis, active infection, and metabolic bone disease.

Examiner, and this is a trap question: "The patient actually has a central disc herniation with myelopathy at C3-4, C4-5, C5-6, and C6-7. What approach do you take?"

Your answer: This is a four-level myelopathy case, and this is where the anterior versus posterior debate becomes critical. Both approaches are viable and the decision depends on alignment, the nature of the compressive pathology, and the patient's overall condition. If the patient has preserved cervical lordosis, a posterior approach is reasonable: either a C3 to C7 laminectomy with lateral mass fusion, or a C3 to C6 or C7 laminoplasty. The advantage of the posterior approach for multilevel disease is that it is a single-stage operation with reliable decompression across many levels and lower rates of dysphagia compared with multilevel anterior surgery. An anterior approach for four levels could be a four-level ACDF, though this has higher rates of pseudarthrosis at roughly fifteen to twenty percent, or anterior corpectomy, either a two-level corpectomy of C4 and C6 with strut grafting or a combined corpectomy and discectomy construct. However, multilevel anterior constructs have higher complication rates including graft dislodgment and pseudarthrosis. Here is the critical point: if the patient has cervical kyphosis, a posterior-only approach is contraindicated because the cord will remain compressed against the anterior pathology even after posterior decompression. In a kyphotic spine, you must include an anterior component, either standalone or as part of a combined anterior-posterior approach with anterior decompression and reconstruction followed by posterior instrumented fusion for supplemental stability.

Examiner, another trap: "You perform a posterior cervical foraminotomy for a lateral soft disc. Would you fuse that level?"

Your answer: No. A posterior cervical foraminotomy does not create instability and does not require fusion, provided you remove less than fifty percent of the facet joint. The biomechanical literature demonstrates that removing more than fifty percent of the facet compromises segmental stability and would necessitate fusion. As long as I respect that boundary, this is a motion-preserving procedure and adding a fusion would be unnecessary and counterproductive.

Examiner: "Your ACDF patient develops dysphagia on postoperative day one. How do you manage this?"

Your answer: Dysphagia after ACDF is very common, reported in up to fifty percent of patients in the early postoperative period when you specifically ask about swallowing difficulty. It is typically caused by retraction of the esophagus and prevertebral soft tissue swelling. Most cases are self-limited and resolve within six to twelve weeks. My initial management is a soft diet, upright positioning, and observation. If dysphagia is mild and improving, I continue conservative management. If it is severe, persistent, or worsening, I obtain a speech and swallow evaluation and consider a modified barium swallow study. However, and this is the critical point for the boards, if the patient develops severe dysphagia, odynophagia, or subcutaneous crepitus within the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours, or if there is any concern for esophageal perforation, this is an emergent situation. I obtain a CT of the neck with oral contrast or a Gastrografin swallow study immediately. Esophageal perforation after ACDF, though rare with an incidence of roughly 0.2 to 0.5 percent, is a life-threatening complication that requires emergent surgical repair, drainage, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and NPO status with parenteral nutrition. Early recognition is essential because delayed diagnosis dramatically worsens outcomes.

Examiner: "What are the major complications of ACDF you would discuss with the patient?"

Your answer: Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, presenting as hoarseness, occurs in two to five percent of cases and is more common after right-sided approaches. Most are neuropraxic and recover within six to twelve weeks. Esophageal injury, as I just discussed. Vertebral artery injury is rare but catastrophic, occurring if dissection strays lateral to the longus colli or drilling extends too far laterally. Management is direct pressure, packing, and emergent angiography. Adjacent segment disease occurs at 2.9 percent per year symptomatically based on Hilibrand's data, with twenty-five percent of patients requiring reoperation within ten years. Pseudarthrosis occurs in roughly five percent of single-level ACDF and fifteen to twenty percent for three or more levels, with smoking being the single biggest modifiable risk factor. C5 palsy is a unique complication presenting as deltoid and biceps weakness twenty-four to seventy-two hours postoperatively. The incidence is roughly five percent after multilevel posterior decompressions and two to three percent after multilevel ACDF. The mechanism likely involves tethering of the C5 nerve root, which has the shortest intradural course, combined with posterior cord drift after decompression. Most patients recover over three to six months.

Now let's review the evidence.

The Prestige ST trial was an FDA IDE randomized controlled trial comparing the Prestige ST cervical disc to ACDF for single-level disease. At seven-year follow-up, arthroplasty was non-inferior for overall success and superior on secondary endpoints including neurological success and reoperation rates at both the index and adjacent levels.

The Mobi-C trial was the first FDA IDE trial to evaluate two-level cervical disc replacement versus two-level ACDF. At seven years, arthroplasty was statistically superior for overall success with significantly lower rates of subsequent surgery. This provided level-one evidence supporting two-level disc replacement.

The Hilibrand data from 2002 analyzed 374 patients after ACDF and found symptomatic adjacent segment disease at 2.9 percent per year, with twenty-five percent eventually requiring reoperation. This remains the strongest argument for motion-preserving alternatives.

Wang and colleagues demonstrated in meta-analyses that single-level ACDF with plate fixation achieves fusion rates above ninety-five percent, while two-level ACDF achieves approximately ninety percent. The addition of a plate significantly improves fusion over stand-alone grafting.

Posterior cervical foraminotomy achieves ninety to ninety-five percent good or excellent outcomes for lateral soft disc herniations, with reoperation rates of five to seven percent at five years, comparable to ACDF but with motion preservation.

Now for the board pearls.

Memorize the level-specific patterns. C5 is deltoid, biceps, lateral arm. C6 is wrist extension, brachioradialis, thumb. C7 is triceps, wrist flexion, middle finger. C8 is grip, hand intrinsics, ring and small fingers. You will be asked to localize the lesion from the clinical description alone, and you must do it instantly without hesitation.

Never say you would operate on a patient with cervical radiculopathy who has not had an adequate trial of conservative management unless there is a progressive motor deficit or myelopathy. The examiner is testing whether you understand that seventy-five to ninety percent improve without surgery.

Never say you would perform a posterior-only approach for cervical myelopathy in a kyphotic spine. This is a classic trap. The cord remains draped over the anterior pathology, and the decompression fails.

Never say you would fuse a posterior foraminotomy if less than fifty percent of the facet was removed. This shows you do not understand the biomechanics.

If asked about a right-sided versus left-sided approach for ACDF, always mention the recurrent laryngeal nerve. The left side is preferred because the nerve has a predictable course in the tracheoesophageal groove. On the right, the nerve may be nonrecurrent in up to one percent of patients, crossing directly through the operative field.

If asked about postoperative dysphagia, always separate benign retraction-related dysphagia from esophageal perforation. The examiner wants to hear that you would emergently work up severe or early-onset dysphagia to rule out perforation.

Always mention the Hilibrand 2.9 percent per year adjacent segment disease rate when discussing ACDF. This demonstrates evidence-based practice and frames the rationale for disc replacement.

The three things that will fail you on this topic. One: inability to rapidly localize a cervical radiculopathy to the correct level based on the clinical presentation. If you cannot tell the examiner that triceps weakness with a diminished triceps reflex and middle finger numbness is a C7 root without pausing, you are not prepared. Two: not knowing when to choose anterior versus posterior, especially in the multilevel myelopathy patient with kyphosis. This is one of the most commonly tested decision points on the spine section. Three: not recognizing esophageal perforation as an emergent complication of ACDF. If the examiner describes severe early dysphagia with crepitus and you say "observe with a soft diet," you have failed the question.

That covers cervical disc herniation management. You're ready for this one.
