cases:
  - label: The head shape referral
    stem: A 3-month-old is referred with an abnormal head shape noticed since birth. The head is long and narrow with frontal bossing and a prominent occiput. There is a palpable ridge along the midline vertex. Cephalic index is 70. Development is normal and the fontanelle is soft.
    q1: Diagnose it and say what you would image.
    a1: Sagittal synostosis producing scaphocephaly, the commonest single-suture synostosis, with a cephalic index of seventy well below the normal seventy six to eighty one. I would image with three-dimensional CT using a dose-reduction protocol, or ultrasound of the sutures, which is radiation-free and excellent under about six months in experienced hands.
    q2: He is three months old. What are his options and which would you recommend?
    a2: At three months he is just within the window for endoscopic strip craniectomy with postoperative helmet moulding, which relies on rapid brain growth to remodel. The alternative is open total or subtotal vault remodelling from around six months. I would favour endoscopic now, provided the family can commit to helmet wear and close follow-up for six to twelve months.
    q3: The family cannot reliably attend for helmet fitting and live four hours away. Does that change your recommendation?
    a3: Yes, materially. Endoscopic surgery without helmet compliance gives a poor result, so the operation depends on a social factor, not just an anatomical one. I would recommend waiting and performing open vault remodelling at six to twelve months, which achieves correction in a single controlled procedure without requiring a helmet.
    q4: You proceed with open remodelling. What do you warn the parents about for the first week?
    a4: Significant blood loss with a likely transfusion, which I would have planned for with tranexamic acid and cross-matched blood. Marked periorbital swelling that routinely closes the eyes for several days, which distresses parents unless forewarned. Plus infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, seroma, and the possibility of persistent bony defects requiring secondary grafting, with relapse and reoperation being much more likely in syndromic than nonsyndromic disease.

Let's master craniosynostosis for the oral boards.

This topic is highly testable because it is deterministic. Each suture, when fused, produces a predictable head shape governed by a single rule, and once you know the rule you can derive every answer rather than memorizing a table. Layer onto that the syndromic associations, the question of intracranial pressure, and the timing of repair, and you have a complete case. Let's build it.

Start with normal skull development. The calvarium develops by intramembranous ossification, and the bones are separated by sutures that function as growth centers, permitting the skull to expand in response to the growing brain. The major sutures are the metopic, between the frontal bones; the coronal, between the frontal and parietal bones; the sagittal, between the two parietal bones; and the lambdoid, between the parietal and occipital bones. Normal closure timing matters: the metopic suture closes early and physiologically, typically between three and nine months, whereas the others remain patent for years, with the sagittal and coronal fusing in adulthood. The anterior fontanelle closes at roughly nine to eighteen months and the posterior fontanelle at roughly two to three months.

Now the governing principle, which is Virchow law. When a suture fuses prematurely, growth is restricted perpendicular to that suture, and compensatory growth occurs parallel to it. That single sentence lets you derive every deformity. Apply it.

Sagittal synostosis is the most common, accounting for roughly forty to sixty percent of nonsyndromic cases, with a male predominance. The sagittal suture runs anteroposteriorly, so growth is restricted transversely and compensates anteroposteriorly, producing a long, narrow head. This is scaphocephaly, or dolichocephaly, with frontal bossing, an occipital prominence sometimes called a bullet occiput, and a palpable sagittal ridge. The cephalic index, which is the biparietal width divided by the anteroposterior length times one hundred, is reduced below roughly seventy five.

Coronal synostosis fuses a suture running transversely, so growth is restricted anteroposteriorly and compensates transversely. Unilateral coronal synostosis produces anterior plagiocephaly, with ipsilateral flattening of the forehead, ipsilateral elevation and recession of the supraorbital rim producing the harlequin eye deformity on plain radiographs, ipsilateral widening of the palpebral fissure, and deviation of the nasal root toward the affected side with the chin pointing away. Bilateral coronal synostosis produces brachycephaly, a short, wide, tall skull, frequently with a turricephalic or towering component, and it is the pattern most associated with syndromic disease.

Metopic synostosis restricts transverse growth of the forehead, producing trigonocephaly: a triangular, keel shaped forehead with a palpable midline ridge, bitemporal narrowing, and hypotelorism, meaning decreased interorbital distance. The hypotelorism is the discriminating feature. Metopic synostosis has been rising in reported incidence.

Lambdoid synostosis is rare, accounting for only a few percent, and produces posterior plagiocephaly. It is the one that must be distinguished from the far more common deformational or positional plagiocephaly, and that distinction is the highest yield differential in the topic.

Here is how to make it. In true lambdoid synostosis, the ipsilateral occiput is flattened, the ipsilateral ear is displaced posteriorly and inferiorly, there is ipsilateral mastoid bulging, and the head shape viewed from above forms a trapezoid. In deformational plagiocephaly, the ipsilateral occiput is flattened but the ipsilateral ear is displaced anteriorly, there is ipsilateral frontal bossing, no mastoid bulge, and the head viewed from above forms a parallelogram. So: ear forward with frontal bossing and a parallelogram means positional; ear back and down with mastoid bulge and a trapezoid means lambdoid synostosis. Deformational plagiocephaly became far more common after the Back to Sleep campaign, is managed with repositioning, tummy time, treatment of any torticollis with physical therapy, and helmet orthosis if needed, and requires no surgery. Do not operate on positional plagiocephaly.

Now the evaluation. Take a history covering pregnancy and birth, head shape at birth and its evolution, developmental milestones, family history, and symptoms of raised pressure such as irritability, vomiting, poor feeding, and developmental regression. Examine the head shape from multiple perspectives, particularly from above, which is the most informative view. Palpate the sutures for ridging and the fontanelles. Measure the head circumference and plot it. Examine for dysmorphic features, syndactyly, and limb anomalies, which point to a syndrome. Perform a fundoscopic examination for papilledema, recognizing that papilledema is insensitive in infants.

For imaging, the study of choice is computed tomography with three dimensional reconstruction, which directly demonstrates the fused suture and the skull morphology, and can show the copper beaten or beaten silver appearance of the inner table suggesting chronically raised pressure. Because these are children, dose reduction protocols matter and you should mention them. Ultrasound of the sutures is an excellent radiation free screening tool in infants under roughly six to eight months in experienced hands. Magnetic resonance is indicated when intracranial anomalies, hydrocephalus, or Chiari malformation are suspected, particularly in syndromic cases. Genetic testing is warranted when a syndrome is suspected.

Now the syndromes, which you must be able to name with their distinguishing features. Most involve mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor genes.

Crouzon syndrome, from FGFR2 mutations, features bicoronal synostosis, midface hypoplasia, proptosis from shallow orbits, and normal hands and feet. Normal extremities is the discriminator.

Apert syndrome, also from FGFR2 mutations, features bicoronal synostosis, midface hypoplasia, and symmetric complex syndactyly of the hands and feet, classically a mitten hand. It is associated with a higher rate of intellectual disability and of central nervous system anomalies including corpus callosum abnormalities.

Pfeiffer syndrome features craniosynostosis with broad, medially deviated thumbs and great toes. Type two, with the cloverleaf skull deformity or kleeblattschädel, is the severe form with a poor prognosis.

Muenke syndrome, from a specific FGFR3 mutation, features unilateral or bilateral coronal synostosis with variable expression and sensorineural hearing loss.

Saethre Chotzen syndrome, from TWIST1 mutations, features coronal synostosis, a low frontal hairline, ptosis, and ear anomalies.

Carpenter syndrome features multiple suture synostosis with polysyndactyly and obesity.

Know the key syndromic associations beyond the skull: raised intracranial pressure, which is far more common in multisuture and syndromic disease with rates reported above forty percent; hydrocephalus; Chiari one malformation, especially in Crouzon; obstructive sleep apnea from midface hypoplasia, which can itself cause raised pressure and cor pulmonale and requires sleep study evaluation; proptosis with risk of corneal exposure and even globe luxation, which is an emergency; and hearing and visual impairment requiring surveillance.

Now the question of intracranial pressure, because the examiner will ask why we operate. In single suture nonsyndromic synostosis, raised intracranial pressure is uncommon, reported in the range of roughly four to fifteen percent, so the primary indication is largely morphologic and developmental. In multisuture and syndromic synostosis, raised pressure is common and the indication is functional. Do not overstate the pressure argument for a simple sagittal synostosis; the honest framing is correction of deformity, normalization of skull growth, avoidance of progressive deformity, and the psychosocial consequences of an abnormal head shape, with pressure being a genuine but less frequent concern. That nuanced answer scores better than a dogmatic one.

Now the surgical options and timing. Two broad strategies exist.

Minimally invasive strip craniectomy, either endoscopic or open, removes the fused suture through small incisions and relies on postoperative helmet orthosis to mold the growing skull. Its requirements are early age, generally under three to four months and ideally around two to three months, because it depends on rapid brain growth to achieve the remodeling. Its advantages are shorter operative time, dramatically less blood loss and lower transfusion rates, shorter hospital stay, and lower cost. Its disadvantages are the narrow age window, the requirement for helmet compliance for many months, and the need for close follow up. Spring assisted cranioplasty is a related approach using implanted distraction springs, requiring a second procedure for spring removal.

Open calvarial vault remodeling, performed at roughly six to twelve months, involves removal and reshaping of the affected bone with fixation using resorbable plates. For anterior deformities such as unicoronal and metopic synostosis, the standard is fronto-orbital advancement and remodeling, in which the supraorbital bar is removed, reshaped, and advanced. Its advantages are immediate and controlled correction, no helmet requirement, and applicability to later presentation. Its disadvantages are longer operative time, substantially greater blood loss, and higher transfusion requirement.

Posterior cranial vault distraction osteogenesis has become important for syndromic multisuture cases, achieving the largest volume expansion with gradual distraction, and it is often performed before anterior work in a staged plan.

Blood loss is the dominant perioperative risk and you must address it proactively: type and cross before incision, use tranexamic acid, which has good evidence for reducing transfusion in this population, employ cell salvage, consider preoperative erythropoietin in selected cases, maintain meticulous hemostasis, and have the anesthesia team prepared for rapid transfusion. Also watch for venous air embolism, given the head up position and open venous sinuses, and for the risk of dural tear and sagittal sinus injury during strip craniectomy over the sagittal suture, which is the specific technical hazard to mention.

Other complications include infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, hardware issues, persistent bony defects requiring secondary grafting, undercorrection or relapse requiring reoperation, particularly in syndromic patients who may need multiple procedures across childhood, and rarely visual loss.

Finally, articulate the multidisciplinary craniofacial team: neurosurgery, plastic and craniofacial surgery, genetics, ophthalmology, otolaryngology, sleep medicine, audiology, speech, orthodontics, and developmental pediatrics, with long term follow up through skeletal maturity.

Quantify the deformity, because numbers beat adjectives. The cephalic index is the biparietal width divided by the maximal anteroposterior length times one hundred; normal is roughly seventy six to eighty one, scaphocephaly falls below about seventy five, and brachycephaly rises above about eighty five. For deformational plagiocephaly, the cranial vault asymmetry index compares the two oblique diagonals, with values above roughly three point five percent considered significant and used to decide on helmet therapy. Recording these at presentation and at follow up gives you an objective measure of correction and of relapse.

Understand the intracranial pressure question more deeply, because it justifies the operation. Raised pressure is uncommon in single suture nonsyndromic disease, in the region of roughly four to fifteen percent, but rises steeply with the number of fused sutures, exceeding forty percent in multisuture and syndromic cases. The mechanisms are multiple and worth naming: a cranial volume that cannot accommodate brain growth, associated hydrocephalus, venous outflow obstruction from jugular foramen stenosis which is particularly characteristic of Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndromes, and obstructive sleep apnoea from midface hypoplasia producing intermittent hypercapnia and raised pressure. Detection is difficult: papilloedema is insensitive in infants, the copper beaten or beaten silver appearance of the inner table on computed tomography is suggestive but nonspecific, and optical coherence tomography of the retinal nerve fibre layer and, in selected cases, invasive intracranial pressure monitoring are used in specialist centres. Treating the sleep apnoea and the hydrocephalus is part of treating the pressure, not a separate issue.

Give the surgical options more anatomical specificity. For sagittal synostosis, endoscopic strip craniectomy removes a strip of the fused suture through two small incisions before three to four months and relies on helmet moulding for six to twelve months; spring assisted cranioplasty inserts distraction springs and requires a second procedure for removal; and open total or subtotal cranial vault remodelling, in variants such as the pi procedure or the modified Melbourne technique, is used from around six months. For unicoronal and metopic synostosis, the standard is fronto-orbital advancement and remodelling, in which the supraorbital bar is removed, reshaped by barrel stave osteotomies or greenstick bending, advanced and fixed with resorbable plates, with the frontal bone reshaped and often switched side to side. For syndromic multisuture disease, posterior cranial vault distraction osteogenesis achieves the largest volume expansion by gradual distraction at roughly one millimetre per day after a latency period, and is frequently performed first in a staged plan before anterior work and before later midface advancement by Le Fort three or monobloc distraction in childhood or adolescence.

Make blood loss a central part of the answer, because it is the dominant perioperative risk in an infant whose total circulating volume may be only seven hundred millilitres. State that you would type and cross match before incision, use tranexamic acid, which has good evidence for reducing transfusion in craniofacial surgery, employ cell salvage, consider preoperative erythropoietin and iron in selected cases, maintain meticulous haemostasis with bone wax and bipolar, communicate continuously with anaesthesia, and monitor for coagulopathy from dilution and for hyperkalaemia and citrate toxicity with large volume transfusion. Name venous air embolism explicitly as a risk given the head up position and open diploic veins and open sinuses, with end tidal carbon dioxide monitoring and precordial Doppler as detection strategies, and flooding the field and lowering the head as the response.

List the complications properly. Intraoperatively: haemorrhage, venous air embolism, dural tear, superior sagittal sinus injury during strip craniectomy over the sagittal suture, and globe or orbital injury during fronto-orbital work. Postoperatively: infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, seroma, periorbital swelling that routinely closes the eyes for several days and must be explained to parents in advance, hardware complications, and persistent bony defects requiring secondary grafting. In the longer term: undercorrection, relapse and the need for reoperation, which is common in syndromic patients and may involve several procedures across childhood. Quote overall reoperation rates as low in nonsyndromic single suture disease and substantially higher, often a third or more, in syndromic multisuture disease.

Address outcomes and the reason for operating beyond the cosmetic. Neurodevelopmental outcome in nonsyndromic single suture synostosis is generally good, though subtle deficits in language, visuospatial processing and executive function are described and are not reliably abolished by surgery, so avoid promising cognitive benefit. The defensible indications are normalisation of skull shape and growth, prevention of progressive deformity, protection of vision and of the globe in syndromic proptosis, treatment of raised intracranial pressure where present, and the genuine psychosocial consequences of an abnormal head shape. That honest framing scores better than an overstated pressure argument.

Finally, describe the team and the follow up. Craniofacial care is delivered by neurosurgery and plastic or craniofacial surgery operating together, with genetics, ophthalmology for visual surveillance and papilloedema, otolaryngology and sleep medicine for airway and apnoea, audiology, speech and language therapy, orthodontics, dentistry, psychology and developmental paediatrics. Follow up continues to skeletal maturity, with serial head circumference, photographs and anthropometry, periodic ophthalmological review, and imaging only when clinically indicated to limit radiation.

To summarize. Virchow law derives every head shape: restricted growth perpendicular to the fused suture with compensation parallel to it. Sagittal gives scaphocephaly, bicoronal gives brachycephaly, unicoronal gives anterior plagiocephaly with a harlequin eye, metopic gives trigonocephaly with hypotelorism, and lambdoid gives posterior plagiocephaly with the ear back and a trapezoid head, distinguishing it from positional plagiocephaly with the ear forward and a parallelogram. Image with three dimensional computed tomography. Suspect a syndrome with multisuture disease and check the hands. Raised pressure is uncommon in single suture disease and common in syndromic disease. Operate endoscopically before three to four months with a helmet, or perform open vault remodeling at six to twelve months. Plan aggressively for blood loss.
