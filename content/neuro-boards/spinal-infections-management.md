questions:
  - q: How do you distinguish spinal infection from metastatic disease on imaging?
    a: By the disc. Pyogenic infection involves two adjacent vertebral bodies and destroys the intervening disc, because bacteria possess proteolytic enzymes. Metastatic tumour characteristically spares the disc. Tuberculosis is the exception, relatively sparing disc with large paraspinal abscesses and subligamentous spread.
    probe: What imaging do you order and with what?
    probeA: MRI of the whole spine with gadolinium. Whole spine because non-contiguous multifocal infection occurs in a meaningful minority and missing a second site is an avoidable error, and with contrast because it distinguishes a drainable abscess, which enhances peripherally, from phlegmon, which enhances homogeneously, and defines the extent of epidural involvement. If MRI is contraindicated I would use CT with contrast plus nuclear imaging, accepting that both are inferior. I would add CT for the bony detail when planning surgery, and plain standing radiographs to assess alignment and deformity, which MRI performed supine does not show.
  - q: What is your diagnostic priority before antibiotics?
    a: An organism. Blood cultures, and if negative, image-guided biopsy of the disc or vertebral body. Starting empirical antibiotics before obtaining tissue in a stable patient compromises the yield and commits them to prolonged broad-spectrum therapy without a target.
    probe: When would you give antibiotics before you have an organism?
    probeA: When the patient is septic and unstable, when there is meningitis, or when there is a rapidly progressive neurological deficit that will not wait for a biopsy. In those situations I take blood cultures and start empirical therapy immediately, because the risk of delay outweighs the loss of culture yield. I would also start empirically if the patient has already had a positive blood culture with a plausible organism, since I then have my target. Outside those, in the stable neurologically intact patient, I hold antibiotics until tissue is obtained, and if the patient has already been started elsewhere I would discuss an antibiotic holiday with infectious diseases before biopsy to improve the yield.
  - q: Who does not need surgery?
    a: The neurologically intact patient without instability, without a significant epidural collection causing compression, and without failure of medical therapy. Most vertebral osteomyelitis is treated medically, and the default is antibiotics with an organism, not an operation.
    probe: So what are the surgical indications?
    probeA: A neurological deficit from epidural compression, which is the one that forces my hand most urgently. Spinal instability or progressive deformity. Failure of medical therapy, meaning ongoing sepsis, rising inflammatory markers or radiological progression despite appropriate antibiotics. The need for tissue when image-guided biopsy has failed, typically after two attempts. And intractable pain that prevents mobilisation. Of those, deficit and instability are the two that are non-negotiable; the others are judgement calls made jointly with infectious diseases.
  - q: State your surgical indications.
    a: Neurological deficit from epidural compression, spinal instability or progressive deformity, failure of medical therapy with ongoing sepsis or progression, the need for tissue when biopsy has failed, and intractable pain. Deficit and instability are the two that force your hand.
    probe: Would you instrument an infected spine?
    probeA: Yes, when instability or deformity requires it, and I would say that confidently because it reverses older teaching. Titanium has a relatively low biofilm affinity, stability itself promotes eradication and fusion by eliminating the micromotion that perpetuates infection, and the long-term morbidity of leaving a patient with an unstable or progressively kyphotic spine substantially exceeds the risk attributable to the metal. The conditions are thorough debridement, culture-directed antibiotics of adequate duration, and joint management with infectious diseases. What I would avoid is placing instrumentation into an undrained abscess without debridement, or placing it and then giving an inadequate antibiotic course.
  - q: Do you put metal into an infected field?
    a: Yes, when instability demands it. Modern practice accepts instrumentation in the presence of infection, because stability promotes fusion and eradication, and titanium has a relatively low biofilm affinity. Deformity and instability cause more long-term harm than the hardware does, provided the infection is treated adequately.
    probe: What would change your mind?
    probeA: A highly resistant or difficult organism, particularly fungal infection, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, or a resistant Gram-negative, where eradication in the presence of hardware is much less likely. Failure of a previous debridement with retained hardware. An immunocompromised host in whom I have little margin. And the situation where stability can be achieved without instrumentation in the infected field, for example with an orthosis, or by instrumenting outside the infected segments. I would also reconsider if the patient's life expectancy is short enough that fusion is not the goal. In those cases I would favour debridement with external or remote fixation, or staged reconstruction once the infection is controlled.
  - q: Summarise OVIVA and what it changed.
    a: OVIVA, New England Journal of Medicine 2019, randomised over a thousand patients with complex bone and joint infection, including vertebral osteomyelitis, and showed oral antibiotics were non-inferior to intravenous when switched within the first week. It has changed practice toward early oral transition in selected patients. Do not confuse it with POET, which asked the same question in endocarditis.
    probe: Which patients would you not transition?
    probeA: Patients with an undrained collection or abscess, because source control has to come first and no antibiotic route compensates for that. Patients with concomitant endovascular infection, particularly endocarditis or an infected graft, where the evidence and the guidance differ. Those without an identified organism, or whose organism has no high-bioavailability oral option with adequate bone penetration. Patients who are not improving clinically or whose inflammatory markers are not falling. Those with malabsorption, vomiting, or significant doubt about adherence. And I would be cautious in the profoundly immunocompromised. The decision is made jointly with infectious diseases rather than by me alone.
  - q: Who is a candidate for oral transition, and when?
    a: Patients improving clinically with inflammatory markers trending down, with a known organism sensitive to a high-bioavailability oral agent with good bone penetration, source control achieved, and no endovascular infection or undrained collection. On OVIVA the switch was made within the first week, so I would not insist on a fixed two-week intravenous course before transitioning. Infectious diseases should be jointly involved in that decision.
    probe: How long is the total course?
    probeA: Six to twelve weeks for pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis, with six weeks being the usual minimum and longer courses for undrained collections, epidural abscess, implant-associated infection or a slow clinical response. Tuberculous spondylitis is entirely different: nine to twelve months of multi-drug therapy, with an intensive four-drug phase of rifampicin, isoniazid, ethambutol and pyrazinamide for two months followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for the remainder, with pyridoxine alongside isoniazid. Fungal infection is longer still. I would follow CRP serially as the more responsive marker, repeat imaging at the end of treatment, and make the stopping decision with infectious diseases rather than to a fixed calendar.
  - q: An epidural abscess with no deficit. Do you operate?
    a: Not automatically. A neurologically intact patient with an identified organism can be managed medically with very close observation and serial imaging, but the threshold for surgery is low because deterioration can be rapid and recovery after established deficit is poor. I would not manage this on a general ward without immediate access to theatre.
    probe: What would make you operate immediately?
    probeA: Any neurological deficit attributable to the abscess, and particularly a progressive one, because recovery after an established deficit is poor and the window between weakness and paralysis can close in hours. Rapid clinical deterioration. Sepsis with a compressive collection that is not responding. Significant cord compression with early myelopathic signs even if the power is preserved. Failure of medical therapy with radiological progression. And a cervical or thoracic location with a large ventral collection, where the cord has little room and the consequences of waiting are greatest. The preoperative neurological status is the strongest predictor of the postoperative one, so the patient who arrives able to walk and is operated on promptly does far better than the one who arrives paraplegic.

Welcome to this oral board preparation session on spinal infections management. This is a topic where the examiners expect precision, urgency, and a clear decision-making framework. You will be tested on your ability to differentiate the three major spinal infection entities, identify surgical emergencies, and articulate evidence-based antibiotic protocols. Let's build this from the ground up.

We begin with the foundation. There are three principal entities you must command: vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, and spinal epidural abscess. These entities frequently coexist, but you must understand each one independently before you can manage them together.

Vertebral osteomyelitis is infection of the vertebral body itself. The most common mechanism is hematogenous spread. Bacteria reach the vertebral body through the arterial blood supply, which preferentially feeds the endplates. This is why early vertebral osteomyelitis typically begins at the endplates and then extends into the vertebral body proper. The lumbar spine is the most common location for pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis, followed by the thoracic spine and then the cervical spine. Direct inoculation from spinal procedures and contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infections are less common routes but remain important in the postoperative patient population.

Discitis is infection of the intervertebral disc space. In adults, the disc is relatively avascular, so primary hematogenous discitis is less common than in children, whose discs retain a blood supply. In adults, discitis most often occurs as an extension from vertebral osteomyelitis through the infected endplate or as a postoperative complication following discectomy or other disc space procedures. Postoperative discitis typically presents two to four weeks after surgery with recurrent back pain disproportionate to the expected postoperative course, and the key distinguishing feature on MRI is enhancement of the disc space and adjacent endplates.

Spinal epidural abscess is infection within the epidural space. This is the entity that demands the most urgency. The infection can arrive hematogenously or by direct extension from adjacent vertebral osteomyelitis or discitis. Spinal epidural abscess can be ventral or dorsal relative to the thecal sac, and this distinction matters for surgical planning. The classic clinical triad is fever, spinal pain, and neurological deficit, but recognize that this full triad is present in a minority of patients at initial presentation. The clinical progression follows four well-described stages: stage one is back pain, stage two is radiculopathy, stage three is weakness, and stage four is paralysis. This is a medical emergency. Neurological deterioration can be rapid and irreversible. Once a patient reaches stage four paralysis, recovery after surgical decompression is poor, particularly if paralysis has been present for more than twenty-four to forty-eight hours.

Now let's talk organisms. Staphylococcus aureus is the dominant pathogen across all three entities, accounting for sixty to seventy percent of pyogenic spinal infections. MRSA is increasingly common, particularly in IV drug users and patients with healthcare exposure, which directly impacts your empiric antibiotic selection. Streptococcus species are the second most common group. Gram-negative organisms, particularly Escherichia coli, are seen when the source is a genitourinary tract infection. Pseudomonas aeruginosa has a classic association with IV drug use. In immunocompromised patients, consider fungal organisms including Aspergillus and Candida species.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis deserves its own discussion. Tuberculous spondylitis, known as Pott disease, has a distinct presentation and management paradigm. TB has a strong predilection for the thoracic spine. The pathology involves anterior vertebral body destruction leading to progressive kyphotic deformity, the so-called gibbus deformity. A hallmark feature is the cold abscess, a paraspinal collection that forms without the acute inflammatory signs you would expect in pyogenic infection. On imaging, you will see paraspinal calcifications in chronic cases. An important distinguishing feature from pyogenic infection is that in TB spondylitis, the disc space is relatively preserved early in the disease course because TB destroys bone but initially spares the disc, whereas pyogenic infection characteristically involves the disc space early.

Risk factors that cross all three entities include diabetes mellitus, intravenous drug use, immunosuppression from any cause including HIV and transplant recipients on immunosuppressive medications, recent spinal procedures, distant source infections such as endocarditis or urinary tract infection, chronic renal failure particularly patients on hemodialysis with vascular access, chronic liver disease, and indwelling catheters or central lines. On boards, the patient profile will often include multiple risk factors to signal the diagnosis. The combination of IV drug use and diabetes in particular should immediately raise your suspicion for spinal infection when the patient presents with axial pain and fever.

Let's move into the clinical case. A fifty-five-year-old man with a history of poorly controlled diabetes and active intravenous drug use presents with three weeks of progressively worsening thoracic back pain and intermittent fevers. Over the past two days, he has developed bilateral lower extremity weakness graded at four out of five on manual muscle testing, and he reports difficulty initiating urination consistent with early urinary retention. His laboratory workup shows a white blood cell count of eighteen thousand, erythrocyte sedimentation rate of ninety-five, and C-reactive protein of twelve. MRI of the thoracic spine with gadolinium contrast demonstrates T8 and T9 vertebral body destruction with disc space enhancement and a dorsal epidural collection extending from T7 to T10 that shows rim enhancement and is compressing the thoracic spinal cord.

This case is designed to test your ability to recognize a surgical emergency and execute a systematic management plan. Notice the key features the examiners have loaded into this vignette: the IV drug use and diabetes as risk factors, the subacute presentation with three weeks of symptoms suggesting a smoldering infection that has now declared itself with cord compression, the thoracic location where the spinal canal is narrowest and tolerance for compression is lowest, and the neurological deficit with myelopathic features including bilateral weakness and bladder dysfunction. Every one of these elements is intentional and should trigger your decision-making cascade. Let's now move into examiner interrogation, which is where boards are won or lost.

The examiner asks you what urgent workup you want. You need blood cultures drawn from two separate sites before any antibiotics are administered. Blood cultures are positive in fifty to seventy percent of spinal infections, and they can spare the patient a biopsy procedure. You need a complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein. In this patient, ESR and CRP are already markedly elevated, which is consistent with active infection. Both markers are elevated in over ninety percent of spinal infections, making them highly sensitive screening tools. The gold standard imaging study is MRI of the entire spine with gadolinium contrast. You image the entire spine because multifocal infection occurs in a meaningful percentage of cases and noncontiguous skip lesions are well described, particularly in the setting of hematogenous seeding. On MRI, the epidural abscess appears T1 hypointense, T2 hyperintense, and demonstrates rim enhancement with contrast administration, distinguishing a drainable abscess from phlegmonous tissue. Given this patient's IV drug use, you must obtain a transthoracic echocardiogram, and if negative but clinical suspicion remains, follow with a transesophageal echocardiogram. Endocarditis is a common source infection in IV drug users and changes your long-term management.

The examiner now asks about your management plan. This patient needs emergent surgery. You do not wait. He has a progressive neurological deficit with bilateral lower extremity weakness and urinary retention in the setting of a compressive epidural abscess. Time to surgery directly correlates with neurological outcome. You obtain blood cultures immediately and take the patient to the operating room. You do not delay surgery to identify the organism. Intraoperative cultures will be obtained during the procedure, and these cultures are often more reliable than blood cultures for directing targeted therapy.

What is your surgical approach? The abscess is dorsal, so you approach posteriorly. You perform a laminectomy spanning the extent of the abscess, in this case T7 through T10, to achieve adequate decompression and drainage. You send intraoperative cultures for aerobic, anaerobic, fungal, and acid-fast bacillus analysis. You send tissue for pathology to evaluate for granulomatous inflammation, which would suggest tuberculosis, and to rule out neoplasm, because metastatic disease can mimic infection on imaging. You irrigate copiously with antibiotic-impregnated saline. Now, the examiner will probe further. There is significant vertebral body destruction at T8 and T9. Does this patient need more than just a laminectomy? Yes. Vertebral body destruction creates anterior column instability, and combined with the posterior element removal from laminectomy, you have created a circumferential destabilization. This patient will need anterior column reconstruction, which can be performed as a staged procedure or in the same sitting depending on the patient's physiologic reserve. Posterior instrumented fusion with pedicle screws spanning from healthy vertebral levels above and below the infected segment provides immediate stability.

The examiner will then challenge you on placing hardware in an infected field. This is a classic trap question and you must answer with confidence. Modern evidence supports placing instrumentation in the setting of active spinal infection. Multiple case series, including the work by Patel and colleagues, demonstrate acceptable infection clearance rates with hardware in place when combined with appropriate antibiotic therapy. Titanium implants are preferred because titanium develops a biofilm-resistant oxide layer. The rationale is straightforward: fixation is required for spinal stability, and without it, progressive deformity worsens both neurological and clinical outcomes. The infection is treated through the hardware with prolonged targeted antibiotic therapy. You do not leave a patient with an unstable spine simply because there is active infection.

When is medical management alone appropriate? The examiner will ask this to test your understanding of the full spectrum. Nonoperative management with antibiotics alone is reasonable when the patient has no neurological deficit, no significant spinal instability, the abscess is small, a causative organism has been identified through blood cultures or CT-guided needle biopsy, and the patient is reliable for close clinical follow-up with serial neurological examinations. In clinically stable patients without a known organism, you perform a CT-guided needle biopsy of the involved vertebral body or disc space to obtain tissue for culture before starting empiric antibiotics. The diagnostic yield of CT-guided biopsy ranges from fifty to seventy percent, and it is lower if the patient has already received antibiotics, which is why obtaining cultures before antibiotics is emphasized. If the initial biopsy is negative and there is no identified organism from blood cultures, a repeat biopsy should be considered. In patients who are medically managed, you must perform serial neurological examinations, ideally every few hours in the acute phase, because neurological deterioration can occur rapidly and converts a medical patient into an emergent surgical patient.

Let's discuss antibiotic protocols in detail. Empiric therapy should cover the most likely organisms. You start with vancomycin to cover MRSA plus either ceftriaxone or meropenem for gram-negative coverage. Once culture and sensitivity data return, you narrow to targeted therapy. For vertebral osteomyelitis and discitis, the standard duration is six to eight weeks of intravenous antibiotics at minimum. However, the OVIVA trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2019, was a landmark randomized controlled trial that demonstrated non-inferiority of oral compared with intravenous antibiotics for bone and joint infections including vertebral osteomyelitis, with randomization within the first week of treatment. Do not cite POET here: POET asked the same question in infective endocarditis. This trial has changed practice, and many infectious disease specialists now transition patients to high-bioavailability oral antibiotics early, within the first one to two weeks, provided source control is achieved, the patient is clinically improving and inflammatory markers are trending down. For spinal epidural abscess, the standard remains six to eight weeks of intravenous therapy, with the decision to transition to oral antibiotics guided by clinical response. For Pott disease, the treatment protocol is fundamentally different. TB spondylitis requires nine to twelve months of multi-drug therapy consisting of rifampin, isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide. The standard regimen mirrors pulmonary TB treatment with an intensive four-drug phase of all four agents for two months followed by a continuation phase of rifampin and isoniazid for the remaining seven to ten months. Pyridoxine, vitamin B6, should be co-administered with isoniazid to prevent peripheral neuropathy. Drug resistance testing is essential, as multidrug-resistant TB changes the entire treatment algorithm and requires infectious disease consultation.

Follow inflammatory markers serially throughout treatment. CRP normalizes faster, typically within two weeks of effective therapy, making it a more responsive marker for acute treatment response. ESR normalizes more slowly, over six to eight weeks. The examiner may present you with a scenario where CRP has normalized at four weeks but ESR remains elevated and ask whether you stop antibiotics. The answer is no. You complete the full six to eight week course minimum regardless of CRP normalization. ESR is a slower-trending marker and its persistent elevation alone does not necessarily indicate treatment failure. However, failure of both CRP and ESR to normalize should raise concern for inadequate treatment, undrained collection, or an unrecognized secondary source. In that scenario, consider repeat MRI, recognizing that MRI enhancement can persist even with successful treatment. Post-treatment MRI must be correlated clinically rather than interpreted in isolation.

Now let's address Pott disease management specifically, because the examiner may pivot to this. Pott disease is fundamentally a medical disease. Anti-tubercular chemotherapy is the primary treatment, and the majority of patients respond without surgical intervention. Surgical indications for Pott disease include progressive neurological deficit, progressive kyphotic deformity, large paraspinal abscess not responding to medical therapy, and diagnostic uncertainty where tissue is needed to confirm the diagnosis. The surgical approach for Pott disease is typically anterior debridement of the destroyed vertebral body followed by anterior column reconstruction with a structural strut graft, either autograft from the iliac crest or fibula, or a titanium mesh cage. Posterior instrumentation may be added for additional stability, particularly in cases with significant kyphotic deformity requiring correction.

Let's address additional trap questions the examiner may deploy. The examiner asks whether you would delay surgery to get blood cultures and identify the organism before operating. In this patient with progressive neurological deficit, absolutely not. You draw blood cultures and take the patient to the operating room simultaneously. Neurological deficit with a compressive epidural abscess is a surgical emergency, and delaying for culture identification is not appropriate. Intraoperative cultures provide the same information without the dangerous delay. In a patient without neurological deficit and without instability, the calculus is different, and you can afford to pursue tissue diagnosis before starting antibiotics. This distinction between the emergent surgical patient and the stable medical patient is one of the most important decision points on the oral boards for this topic.

The examiner may also ask about the abscess size threshold for intervention. While there is no absolute cutoff that mandates surgery in the absence of neurological deficit, epidural abscess collections greater than two and a half centimeters in anteroposterior diameter or those spanning more than three vertebral segments are generally considered large enough to warrant surgical drainage rather than medical management alone, given the higher failure rate of antibiotics-only treatment in these cases. Phlegmon without a drainable liquid collection is more amenable to medical management, and distinguishing phlegmon from a drainable abscess on MRI is clinically important. Rim enhancement with a central area of restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging favors a drainable abscess.

Another common probing question involves complications. The most devastating complication is progressive neurological deficit despite surgery, which is almost always a function of delayed surgical intervention. This reinforces why time to surgery is the critical variable. Other complications include hardware failure, persistent or recurrent infection requiring reoperation, progressive spinal deformity particularly when anterior column reconstruction is inadequate, wound dehiscence in malnourished or immunocompromised patients, cerebrospinal fluid leak from dural violation during decompression, and systemic medical complications including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and ongoing sepsis. Postoperative management should include infectious disease consultation for prolonged antibiotic management, nutritional optimization given the catabolic state of sepsis, and close monitoring for recurrence with serial inflammatory markers and clinical examinations.

Let's consolidate the evidence base. The OVIVA trial, published in 2019, is the highest-impact trial for antibiotic management of bone and joint infections and demonstrated that oral antibiotics are non-inferior to continued intravenous antibiotics when the switch is made within the first week. The Darouiche meta-analysis and systematic reviews on spinal epidural abscess outcomes have established that preoperative neurological status is the strongest predictor of postoperative neurological recovery, reinforcing the urgency of early surgical decompression. The Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines authored by Berbari and colleagues, published in 2015, provide the current standard recommendations for diagnosis and management of native vertebral osteomyelitis, including recommendations on imaging, biopsy, and antibiotic duration. The Patel series and similar retrospective case series on instrumentation in infected spines provide the evidence base for the safety and efficacy of hardware placement in the setting of active infection. Additionally, be aware of the work by Rezai and colleagues demonstrating that prolonged time to surgical decompression beyond twenty-four hours in patients with epidural abscess and neurological deficit is associated with significantly worse neurological outcomes. This data point reinforces the surgical urgency in every case presentation where the examiner gives you a patient with progressive neurological deficit. The literature consistently shows that patients who arrive at surgery with the ability to ambulate have far better outcomes than those who arrive paraplegic, and the window between ambulatory weakness and complete paralysis can close in a matter of hours.

Let's close with the board pearls that you should have at the tip of your tongue walking into the examination room. Staphylococcus aureus is the dominant organism in spinal infections, period. It accounts for sixty to seventy percent of cases. When you hear IV drug user with back pain and fever, your differential must include spinal epidural abscess until proven otherwise. Spinal epidural abscess is a surgical emergency when neurological deficit is present. Do not delay surgery to identify the organism in a patient with progressive neurological deficit. Draw blood cultures and go directly to the operating room. Hardware can be placed in the setting of active infection. The evidence supports this, and the examiner is testing whether you will leave a patient with an unstable spine out of an unfounded fear of seeding implants. The modern approach is to stabilize the spine and treat the infection through the hardware with appropriate antibiotics. For antibiotic duration, remember six to eight weeks for pyogenic infections and nine to twelve months for tuberculosis. Follow CRP and ESR serially, but CRP is the more responsive acute marker. The OVIVA trial supports early oral transition for vertebral osteomyelitis in selected patients; POET is the corresponding endocarditis trial and the two are easily confused. For Pott disease, remember the thoracic predilection, the cold abscess, the gibbus deformity, the relative disc preservation, and that medical management is the primary treatment. Finally, always image the entire spine. Noncontiguous multifocal infection is a well-documented phenomenon, and missing a second site of involvement is an avoidable error that the examiners will not forgive.

One more tactical point for the examination itself. When the examiner presents a spinal infection case, structure your response systematically. Start with the diagnosis, then the workup, then the decision between surgical and medical management with clear articulation of the indications driving that decision, then the specific surgical plan if operative, then the antibiotic plan, and finally the follow-up strategy. This organized approach demonstrates command of the topic and prevents you from getting lost in the details under pressure.
