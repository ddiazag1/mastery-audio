Let's talk about status epilepticus in neurosurgical patients. This is a topic the board examiners love because it sits right at the intersection of critical care pharmacology, neurophysiology, and operative decision-making. You need to know the definitions cold, the drug doses by heart, and the neurosurgery-specific nuances that separate your answer from a neurologist's answer. Let's get into it.

Start with the definition, because the old definition has been retired. The classic teaching was that status epilepticus meant a seizure lasting thirty minutes or more. That is outdated. The current operational definition, based on the Trinka 2015 consensus statement published in Epilepsia, defines convulsive status epilepticus as a seizure lasting greater than five minutes, or two or more discrete seizures without return to neurologic baseline between them. The five-minute threshold is called time point t1. This is the point at which a seizure is unlikely to stop on its own, and treatment should begin. The second critical time point is t2, which for convulsive status epilepticus is thirty minutes. This is the point at which irreversible neuronal injury may begin. You need to know both time points. t1 equals five minutes, the trigger to treat. t2 equals thirty minutes, the threshold for permanent injury. For nonconvulsive status epilepticus, the time points are different. t1 is ten minutes and t2 is indeterminate, but neuronal injury still occurs, just on a longer and less predictable timeline. The examiners want to hear you articulate this updated framework, not the old thirty-minute definition.

Now let's classify status epilepticus, because the type determines your diagnostic approach. Convulsive status epilepticus is what everyone pictures: tonic-clonic motor activity, obvious and dramatic. But nonconvulsive status epilepticus, or NCSE, is far more relevant to your neurosurgical practice because it is extraordinarily common in the neuro ICU and nearly invisible without the right monitoring. Studies using continuous EEG in comatose neurosurgical patients consistently show that twenty to thirty percent of these patients have subclinical electrographic seizures. Let me say that again. Up to one in three of your comatose neurosurgical ICU patients may be seizing and you will not know it unless you put them on continuous EEG. This is not an academic curiosity. Nonconvulsive seizures cause secondary neuronal injury, worsen cerebral edema, increase metabolic demand in already compromised brain, and independently predict worse outcomes. If an examiner gives you a post-craniotomy patient who is not waking up as expected, one of the first things out of your mouth should be continuous EEG to rule out nonconvulsive status epilepticus.

Moving to etiology, and this is where you show you are a neurosurgeon and not just reciting a medicine algorithm. The causes of status epilepticus in neurosurgical patients are specific and you need to rattle them off systematically. Post-craniotomy cortical irritation is the most straightforward. Any time you manipulate cortex, especially eloquent cortex, you create an irritable focus. This is why seizure prophylaxis exists in the perioperative period. Hemorrhage is the next category, and you should think of this in layers. Subdural hematoma, particularly acute subdural with cortical compression. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, where seizures occur in six to eighteen percent of patients and are an independent predictor of poor outcome. Intracerebral hemorrhage, especially lobar hemorrhage that involves cortex. Tumors are a major cause, and the examiner may test your knowledge of which tumors are most epileptogenic. Low-grade gliomas are the highest risk, with seizure rates of sixty to ninety percent, because they grow slowly in cortex and create chronic irritative foci. Cortical metastases are also high risk, particularly melanoma metastases. High-grade gliomas cause seizures in thirty to fifty percent of patients but are somewhat less epileptogenic than low-grade tumors despite being more aggressive. Infection is the next category. Brain abscess causes seizures in twenty-five to fifty percent of cases. Encephalitis, whether viral like HSV or autoimmune, can present as status epilepticus and the examiner may try to lead you down a purely surgical path when the answer is actually immunotherapy or acyclovir.

Here is the category that catches neurosurgeons off guard on boards: metabolic causes. Hyponatremia is the big one, and it is incredibly common in our patient population. Post-transsphenoidal patients develop SIADH, and the resulting hyponatremia can trigger seizures when sodium drops below one hundred twenty milliequivalents per liter, sometimes even higher if the drop is rapid. Post-subarachnoid hemorrhage patients develop cerebral salt wasting, which also causes hyponatremia but through renal sodium loss rather than water retention. The critical teaching point here is that hyponatremia-induced seizures will not stop with antiepileptic drugs alone. You must correct the sodium. Give hypertonic saline, three percent, as a 100 mL bolus over ten minutes, repeatable up to three times, targeting a sodium rise of four to six milliequivalents per liter acutely. Do not exceed ten to twelve milliequivalents per liter correction in twenty-four hours to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. The antiepileptics are adjunctive here; the sodium correction is the treatment. Drug withdrawal is another common trigger, particularly alcohol withdrawal and benzodiazepine withdrawal in your trauma population. And do not forget contrast agents. Iodinated contrast and gadolinium, particularly when the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, can be directly neurotoxic and epileptogenic. This is relevant after endovascular procedures or when contrast leaks through a disrupted barrier in tumor or infection cases.

Now the treatment protocol. You must know this as a timed, stepwise algorithm with specific drug doses. The examiners will walk you through a clinical scenario minute by minute and expect you to give exact doses at each stage.

Zero to five minutes. This is the stabilization phase. Airway, breathing, circulation. Establish two large-bore IVs. Check a point-of-care glucose immediately because hypoglycemia causes seizures and is instantly reversible with dextrose. If glucose is below sixty milligrams per deciliter, give fifty milliliters of D50W intravenously. Place the patient on a monitor with pulse oximetry. Position them to prevent aspiration. Draw labs including a comprehensive metabolic panel, magnesium, calcium, phosphorus, antiepileptic drug levels if applicable, and a toxicology screen. Now give your first-line agent: lorazepam, dosed at 0.1 milligrams per kilogram IV, with a maximum single dose of 4 milligrams. You can repeat this once after five minutes if the seizure continues. Lorazepam is preferred over diazepam because it has a longer duration of action in the brain, roughly twelve to twenty-four hours versus diazepam's fifteen to thirty minutes of effective anticonvulsant activity despite diazepam's longer plasma half-life. If you do not have IV access, midazolam 10 milligrams intramuscularly is an acceptable alternative, as shown in the RAMPART trial.

Five to twenty minutes. The seizure has not stopped with benzodiazepines. You now have established status epilepticus, and you move to your second-line agent. You have three options, and thanks to the ESETT trial, we know they are equally effective. Let me give you each one with full dosing, then we will talk about how to choose.

Option one: fosphenytoin, dosed at 20 milligrams phenytoin equivalents per kilogram IV. The advantage of fosphenytoin over phenytoin is that it can be infused faster, up to 150 milligrams PE per minute compared to phenytoin's maximum of 50 milligrams per minute. Fosphenytoin also causes less hypotension and does not carry the risk of purple glove syndrome. Purple glove syndrome is a limb-threatening complication of IV phenytoin caused by the highly alkaline pH of the phenytoin preparation, which causes tissue necrosis if it extravasates. It presents as progressive edema, discoloration, and pain in the limb distal to the IV site and can require fasciotomy. Fosphenytoin is a prodrug that is converted to phenytoin and does not carry this risk. Monitor the cardiac rhythm during infusion regardless of which formulation you use, because both can cause bradycardia and hypotension through sodium channel blockade in the heart.

Option two: levetiracetam, dosed at 60 milligrams per kilogram IV, with a maximum dose of 4500 milligrams. This is the dose validated in the ESETT trial. Many practitioners underdose levetiracetam in status epilepticus because they are used to the maintenance dose of 500 to 1500 milligrams twice daily. The loading dose for status epilepticus is much higher. Levetiracetam has a favorable side effect profile: no cardiac toxicity, no hepatotoxicity, no significant drug interactions, and no need for drug level monitoring. This makes it particularly attractive in the neurosurgical patient who is on multiple medications or has hepatic or cardiac comorbidities.

Option three: valproate, dosed at 40 milligrams per kilogram IV. Valproate is effective but has important contraindications. Do not use it in patients with hepatic dysfunction, pancreatitis, mitochondrial disorders, or pregnancy. It also inhibits platelet aggregation and can worsen coagulopathy, which is a real consideration in your neurosurgical patients who may be heading to the OR or who have recent surgical beds. In a patient with a fresh craniotomy and concern for hemorrhagic complications, valproate would not be your first choice.

Now the ESETT trial itself. This was published by Kapur and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2019. It was a randomized, double-blind, adaptive trial comparing levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, and valproate in patients with benzodiazepine-refractory established status epilepticus. The primary outcome was seizure cessation by sixty minutes without additional intervention. The results showed that all three agents were equally effective, with seizure cessation rates of approximately forty-five percent for each drug. There was no statistically significant difference. The practical takeaway for the boards is this: there is no wrong second-line agent. Choose based on the patient in front of you. Use levetiracetam if you want to avoid cardiac and hepatic toxicity. Use fosphenytoin if the patient is already on levetiracetam or if you need the most established evidence base. Avoid valproate in coagulopathy, liver disease, or pregnancy.

Twenty to sixty minutes. The seizure persists despite adequate dosing of a benzodiazepine and a second-line agent. This is now refractory status epilepticus. The management escalation at this point is significant. You must intubate the patient for airway protection and begin continuous EEG monitoring. Without continuous EEG, you are flying blind because once you paralyze the patient for intubation, you lose your clinical seizure markers and the only way to know if seizures continue is the EEG. Start an anesthetic infusion. Again, you have three options.

Midazolam: bolus 0.2 milligrams per kilogram IV, then start a continuous infusion at 0.1 to 2 milligrams per kilogram per hour. Titrate to burst suppression on the EEG. Midazolam has the advantage of the shortest context-sensitive half-time among the three agents, meaning the patient will wake up fastest when you stop the drip.

Propofol: bolus 1 to 2 milligrams per kilogram IV, then start a continuous infusion at 20 to 60 micrograms per kilogram per minute. Propofol is an excellent anticonvulsant with rapid onset and offset, but you must watch for propofol infusion syndrome. This occurs with prolonged infusions exceeding forty-eight hours or rates exceeding 5 milligrams per kilogram per hour. It presents with metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, hepatomegaly, cardiac failure, and lipemic serum. It is fatal if not recognized. Monitor creatine kinase and triglycerides daily in patients on propofol drips. If you see a rising CK or unexplained metabolic acidosis, stop the propofol immediately.

Pentobarbital: bolus 5 milligrams per kilogram IV, then start a continuous infusion at 1 to 3 milligrams per kilogram per hour. Pentobarbital is the most potent anticonvulsant of the three and achieves burst suppression most reliably, but it causes profound hypotension requiring vasopressor support and has an extremely long half-life, meaning the patient will be comatose for days after discontinuation. Reserve this for cases where midazolam and propofol have failed.

Your EEG target with any of these agents is burst suppression: one to two second bursts of EEG activity alternating with three to ten second intervals of suppression. Maintain burst suppression for twenty-four to forty-eight hours, then slowly taper the anesthetic while watching the continuous EEG for seizure recurrence.

Super-refractory status epilepticus. This is defined as status epilepticus that continues or recurs twenty-four hours or more after the onset of anesthetic therapy, including cases where seizures return during the taper of anesthetic agents. This is the most challenging clinical scenario and carries a high mortality. At this point, you should consider ketamine. Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist, and there is a pathophysiologic rationale for why it may work when GABA-ergic agents have failed. During prolonged status epilepticus, a process called receptor trafficking occurs. GABA-A receptors are internalized from the synaptic membrane, making the neuron progressively resistant to benzodiazepines, propofol, and barbiturates, which all work through GABA mechanisms. Simultaneously, NMDA receptors are trafficked to the synaptic surface, making glutamatergic excitation even more potent. Ketamine blocks these upregulated NMDA receptors, attacking the seizure through a completely different mechanism. Dose ketamine at 1 to 3 milligrams per kilogram bolus, then 1 to 10 milligrams per kilogram per hour as a continuous infusion.

If there is any suspicion of an autoimmune etiology, such as anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, which classically presents in young women with psychiatric symptoms followed by seizures, movement disorders, and autonomic instability, initiate immunotherapy with IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, or plasmapheresis. Send antibody panels but do not wait for results to treat. Ketogenic diet has emerging evidence in super-refractory SE and can be initiated enterally through a feeding tube, but this is typically a decision made in consultation with epileptologists and dietitians.

Let me cover the neurosurgery-specific considerations that separate you from the internist managing this problem. Post-craniotomy seizure prophylaxis is a topic that comes up on every neurosurgery board exam. For traumatic brain injury, the Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines recommend seven days of seizure prophylaxis with phenytoin or levetiracetam. The landmark Temkin 1990 study showed that phenytoin reduced early post-traumatic seizures, those occurring within seven days, but did not prevent late epilepsy. There is no evidence that prophylaxis beyond seven days is beneficial in TBI, and it should be stopped. For brain tumor surgery, the evidence is much weaker. The American Academy of Neurology recommends against routine long-term prophylaxis in brain tumor patients who have never had a seizure. However, many neurosurgeons use a short course of levetiracetam perioperatively, particularly for supratentorial cortical tumors. If the patient has had a preoperative seizure, they need long-term antiepileptic therapy regardless.

Hyponatremia-induced seizures deserve emphasis again because this is a classic board trap. The examiner will present a patient who is post-operative day three after transsphenoidal resection of a pituitary adenoma. The patient has a seizure. The instinct is to load an antiepileptic. But the sodium comes back at one hundred fourteen. This is a medical emergency that requires sodium correction as the primary intervention. You give three percent hypertonic saline, 100 mL bolus over ten minutes, and you can repeat this up to three times. Your target is to raise sodium by four to six milliequivalents per liter in the first few hours. The mechanism matters for the boards: this patient likely has SIADH, which is the most common cause of hyponatremia after transsphenoidal surgery, with a peak incidence at days five to nine but it can occur earlier. Distinguish this from cerebral salt wasting, which is more common after subarachnoid hemorrhage and is characterized by volume depletion with renal sodium loss, whereas SIADH is characterized by euvolemia or mild hypervolemia with inappropriately concentrated urine. The treatment differs because cerebral salt wasting requires volume replacement with normal saline or hypertonic saline, while SIADH is treated with fluid restriction and hypertonic saline if symptomatic.

Continuous EEG monitoring indications in the neuro ICU. You should be placing patients on continuous EEG in the following situations: any unexplained alteration in consciousness, especially if the patient is not following their expected neurological trajectory after surgery; all patients after subarachnoid hemorrhage who have altered mental status, because seizures in SAH are associated with rebleeding and vasospasm and worsen outcomes; post-craniotomy patients with any concerning change in exam; after any episode of convulsive status epilepticus to rule out ongoing nonconvulsive status epilepticus, because up to forty-eight percent of patients who appear to have stopped seizing clinically are still seizing electrographically; and any patient being treated with neuromuscular blockade who has a history of seizures or a structural brain lesion.

Now let's go through some board-style trap questions. Trap question one: a patient has a seizure in the recovery room after supratentorial craniotomy. The CT shows no hemorrhage, no edema, no pneumocephalus. What is your next step? The trap is to say "load phenytoin and observe." The correct answer is that you need to rule out nonconvulsive status epilepticus with continuous EEG, check a metabolic panel including sodium and calcium and magnesium, and have a low threshold for returning to the operating room if the seizure is difficult to control, because an expanding hematoma not yet visible on CT can present this way. Repeat imaging in four to six hours if the clinical picture does not improve.

Trap question two: the examiner asks you to choose between phenytoin and levetiracetam for post-traumatic seizure prophylaxis. Both are acceptable per the guidelines. But if the patient has a coagulopathy or liver injury, levetiracetam is preferred because it has no hepatic metabolism and no effect on coagulation. If the patient is on chemotherapy, levetiracetam is preferred because phenytoin is a potent CYP450 inducer that can dramatically alter chemotherapy drug levels.

Trap question three: a patient on propofol infusion for refractory status epilepticus develops unexplained metabolic acidosis on day three. What is the diagnosis? Propofol infusion syndrome. Stop the propofol immediately. Switch to midazolam or pentobarbital. Check CK, triglycerides, and lactate. This is a lethal complication and you need to name it without hesitation.

Here is a clinical case to tie it all together. A fifty-two year old woman is postoperative day one from a left frontal craniotomy for resection of a WHO grade two diffuse astrocytoma. She was on levetiracetam 1000 milligrams twice daily preoperatively for seizure control. The nurse calls you because the patient is having rhythmic left arm jerking that has been going on for three minutes. Walk through your management. You get to the bedside. The seizure is ongoing. Establish IV access if not already present, check glucose, and give lorazepam 4 milligrams IV push. Continue to observe. The seizure does not stop at five minutes. You now have established status epilepticus. She is already on levetiracetam, so loading additional levetiracetam is not your best move. Give fosphenytoin 20 milligrams PE per kilogram IV. Continue to observe. The seizure stops at eight minutes. You place her on continuous EEG. You order a stat CT head to rule out postoperative hemorrhage. Labs come back with sodium of one hundred thirty-four, which is low normal but not causative. The CT shows expected postoperative changes without hemorrhage. Continuous EEG shows no further seizures. You increase her levetiracetam to 1500 milligrams twice daily and add fosphenytoin as a second agent given the breakthrough seizure. If the CT had shown a hematoma causing mass effect, you would be headed back to the operating room for evacuation, because no amount of antiepileptic therapy will control seizures caused by an expanding surgical hematoma compressing cortex.

A few final high-yield board pearls. First, the most important thing in managing status epilepticus is time. Every minute of ongoing seizure activity causes progressive neuronal injury and increases refractoriness to treatment. Do not wait for labs or imaging before giving benzodiazepines. Treat the seizure first. Second, always think about the cause. In a neurosurgical patient, the differential is surgical until proven otherwise. Expanding hematoma, cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, and vascular compromise are all surgical emergencies that can present as seizures. Third, nonconvulsive status epilepticus is the great masquerader in the neuro ICU. When in doubt, get continuous EEG. Fourth, when you correct hyponatremia-induced seizures, you must correct the sodium. Antiepileptics are the bridge, not the treatment. Fifth, know the ESETT trial result in one sentence: levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, and valproate are equally effective for established status epilepticus at approximately forty-five percent seizure cessation each. Sixth, remember receptor trafficking in prolonged status epilepticus as the rationale for ketamine. GABA receptors go down, NMDA receptors come up. Ketamine targets the receptor that is becoming more abundant, not the one that is disappearing.

That covers status epilepticus in neurosurgical patients. You're ready for this one.
