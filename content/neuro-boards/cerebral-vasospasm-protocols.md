Welcome to cerebral vasospasm protocols. This is one of the highest-yield vascular topics you will face on the oral boards. Every examiner has a vasospasm scenario in their back pocket. You need to know the pathophysiology cold, the monitoring thresholds by number, the treatment algorithm step by step, and the trial data that supports each decision. Let's build that framework now.

We begin with the foundation. Vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage is a delayed arterial narrowing driven by the breakdown products of blood sitting in the subarachnoid space. The primary molecular culprit is oxyhemoglobin. When red blood cells lyse in the cerebrospinal fluid, oxyhemoglobin is released and undergoes auto-oxidation to methemoglobin. During this process, reactive oxygen species are generated, which scavenge nitric oxide. Nitric oxide is your primary endothelial vasodilator. Once it is depleted, you lose the tonic relaxation signal to the vascular smooth muscle. Simultaneously, endothelin-1 is upregulated. Endothelin-1 is the most potent endogenous vasoconstrictor in the human body. So you have a double hit: loss of the vasodilator and gain of the vasoconstrictor. The smooth muscle cells contract, and if the spasm is sustained, you develop structural changes in the vessel wall, including myonecrosis, intimal proliferation, and inflammatory infiltration. These structural changes are what make prolonged vasospasm resistant to pharmacological reversal and explain why mechanical dilation with balloon angioplasty can be more durable than drug infusion alone.

Now here is the critical distinction you must articulate clearly on the boards. Angiographic vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia are not the same thing. Angiographic vasospasm, also called radiographic vasospasm, refers to arterial narrowing visible on catheter angiography, CT angiography, or transcranial Doppler. It occurs in approximately seventy percent of patients after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Delayed cerebral ischemia, or DCI, is a clinical diagnosis. It is defined as a new focal neurological deficit or a decrease of at least two points on the Glasgow Coma Scale that lasts for more than one hour, is not explained by another cause, and occurs between day three and day fourteen after the hemorrhage. DCI occurs in approximately thirty percent of SAH patients. The disconnect is important. Not all patients with angiographic vasospasm develop DCI. Many patients have vessel narrowing on imaging but remain neurologically intact. Conversely, DCI can occur without large-vessel vasospasm. The mechanisms for this include microvascular dysfunction with small arteriolar constriction below the resolution of angiography, cortical spreading depolarizations that cause waves of neuronal and glial depolarization with associated vasoconstriction, and microthrombosis driven by platelet activation and coagulation cascade derangement in the subarachnoid space. If you conflate vasospasm with DCI on the boards, you will lose points. Be precise.

The timeline is predictable. Vasospasm typically begins on day three to five after the hemorrhage, peaks on day seven to ten, and resolves by day fourteen to twenty-one. This timeline is driven by the kinetics of hemoglobin degradation in the subarachnoid space. The higher the clot burden, the higher the risk, which is why modified Fisher grade is the strongest radiographic predictor. Modified Fisher grade three, thick cisternal clot without intraventricular hemorrhage, and modified Fisher grade four, thick cisternal clot with intraventricular hemorrhage, carry the highest risk.

Moving to the clinical case. You are covering the neurosurgical ICU and are called to evaluate a fifty-two-year-old woman who is now post-bleed day seven. She presented with a Hunt-Hess grade three subarachnoid hemorrhage, modified Fisher grade four, from a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm that was coiled on day zero. She had been doing well, following commands, conversing appropriately, moving all extremities. Over the past two hours, the nursing staff reports she has become progressively drowsy. On your examination, she is now opening eyes to voice only, she has new expressive aphasia, and her right upper extremity has subtle pronator drift that was not present on the prior check. The transcranial Doppler performed this morning shows a left middle cerebral artery mean flow velocity of 220 centimeters per second. The ipsilateral extracranial internal carotid artery velocity is 37 centimeters per second, giving a Lindegaard ratio of 6. This is your classic day seven vasospasm presentation.

Now for the examiner interrogation. This is where the oral boards live. Let me walk you through the questions and the answers you need to deliver.

The examiner asks, what is your monitoring strategy for vasospasm in the ICU? You answer that you perform daily transcranial Doppler ultrasound beginning on post-bleed day three and continuing through day fourteen. You are measuring mean flow velocities in the middle cerebral artery, anterior cerebral artery, and posterior cerebral artery bilaterally. The key metric is the middle cerebral artery mean flow velocity. A velocity above 120 centimeters per second is concerning for vasospasm. A velocity above 200 centimeters per second indicates severe vasospasm. However, velocity alone can be misleading because it is affected by cardiac output, hematocrit, and systemic blood pressure. This is why you calculate the Lindegaard ratio, which is the mean MCA velocity divided by the mean extracranial ICA velocity. A Lindegaard ratio greater than 3 suggests vasospasm. A ratio greater than 6 indicates severe vasospasm. The extracranial ICA serves as a control for systemic hyperdynamic flow. If both the MCA and extracranial ICA velocities are elevated, the patient may simply be hyperdynamic rather than vasospastic. In addition to TCDs, you perform clinical neurological checks every one hour. Any new deficit triggers an immediate workup. CT perfusion has an emerging role and can identify regions of decreased cerebral blood flow and prolonged mean transit time before infarction occurs, helping you catch DCI in patients with poor clinical exams, such as those who are high Hunt-Hess grade and intubated.

The examiner then asks, what is your differential diagnosis for deterioration on day seven? You must not anchor on vasospasm alone. Your differential includes vasospasm with delayed cerebral ischemia, which is the most likely given the timeline, but also rebleeding from the aneurysm, especially if the aneurysm was incompletely treated. It includes acute hydrocephalus from external ventricular drain malfunction or obstruction. It includes seizure, both convulsive and nonconvulsive. It includes hyponatremia, which is common after SAH from cerebral salt wasting, and you should note the distinction from SIADH because the treatment is different. And it includes infection, particularly ventriculitis if an EVD is in place, or pneumonia with sepsis causing systemic deterioration.

The examiner asks, what is your initial workup? You order a stat noncontrast CT head to rule out rebleeding and evaluate hydrocephalus and ventricular catheter position. You obtain a CT angiogram and CT perfusion simultaneously. The CTA will show large-vessel narrowing, and the CT perfusion will show regions of reduced cerebral blood flow with prolonged mean transit time, which indicates tissue at risk. You draw a basic metabolic panel to check the sodium level. If the sodium is below 130, you have a contributing metabolic cause that must be corrected. You obtain an EEG or at minimum a spot EEG to rule out nonconvulsive status epilepticus. You review the vitals trend and culture data.

The examiner says the CT shows no new hemorrhage, ventricles are stable, the CTA confirms severe left MCA and left A1 vasospasm, CT perfusion shows elevated mean transit time in the left MCA territory but no completed infarct, sodium is 138, no seizure activity. What do you do? This is where you walk them through medical management first. You initiate induced hypertension. Your vasopressor of choice is phenylephrine or norepinephrine, titrated to a systolic blood pressure target of 180 to 220 millimeters of mercury. You start with a target of 180 and escalate in increments if the patient does not improve. You give a fluid bolus of isotonic crystalloid to ensure euvolemia. This is a key point. You maintain euvolemia, not hypervolemia. You continue nimodipine 60 milligrams orally every four hours. Nimodipine is the only pharmacological agent proven to improve outcomes after SAH. It was established in the 1989 British aneurysm nimodipine trial. You give it orally, not intravenously, in the United States because intravenous nimodipine causes significant hypotension. Some European centers use intravenous nimodipine with careful hemodynamic monitoring, but for the boards, the answer is oral administration. If the patient cannot swallow, you aspirate the liquid from the capsule and give it via nasogastric tube.

The examiner presses, would you use hypervolemia as part of triple-H therapy? This is a trap question. The answer is no. You explain that the traditional triple-H therapy of hypertension, hypervolemia, and hemodilution has been largely abandoned. The HIMALAIA trial, published in 2017, was a randomized controlled trial that compared prophylactic hypervolemia to euvolemia in SAH patients and found no benefit of hypervolemia in preventing DCI. In fact, hypervolemia carries significant risks including pulmonary edema, ARDS, and dilutional hyponatremia. The only component of triple-H that has survived the evidence is induced hypertension. You must never say triple-H on the boards without immediately clarifying that induced hypertension is the single effective component.

The examiner asks, would you start a statin? Another trap. The answer is no. The STASH trial, a multicenter randomized controlled trial published in 2014, compared simvastatin to placebo in aneurysmal SAH and found no benefit in reducing DCI, vasospasm, or improving functional outcomes at six months. Do not start a statin for vasospasm prophylaxis.

The examiner asks, what about magnesium? Again, no. The MASH-2 trial, published in 2012, was a large international randomized trial of intravenous magnesium sulfate versus placebo after aneurysmal SAH. It showed no reduction in DCI and no improvement in outcomes. Magnesium is not indicated for vasospasm prevention or treatment.

The examiner asks, the patient's blood pressure has been augmented to a systolic of 200 for two hours and there is no clinical improvement. What is your next step? You escalate to endovascular intervention. You call your neurointerventionalist and take the patient to the angiography suite. The threshold for escalation is failure of medical management within one to two hours. Do not wait longer. Ischemia is time-dependent.

The examiner asks, what are your endovascular options? You have two primary tools. First is intra-arterial vasodilator infusion. Your agents are verapamil at a dose of 10 to 20 milligrams infused selectively into the spastic vessel, or nicardipine at a dose of 5 to 10 milligrams. These are calcium channel blockers that act directly on the vascular smooth muscle. The effect is often temporary, lasting hours to days, and repeat treatments may be necessary. Second is balloon angioplasty. This involves navigating a compliant balloon into the spastic arterial segment and mechanically dilating it. Balloon angioplasty is used for proximal vessel spasm, meaning the supraclinoid internal carotid artery, M1 segment of the MCA, and A1 segment of the ACA. It is more durable than pharmacological treatment because it physically disrupts the contracted smooth muscle. However, it carries a two to five percent risk of vessel rupture, which is catastrophic. You cannot use balloon angioplasty for distal vessels because the caliber is too small and the risk of perforation is too high. For distal vasospasm, you rely on intra-arterial vasodilators. In practice, you often use a combination, performing balloon angioplasty on the proximal segments and infusing vasodilators into the distal territory.

The examiner asks about complications of your management. You must acknowledge ARDS from aggressive fluid resuscitation, which is why you maintain euvolemia rather than hypervolemia. You discuss neurogenic myocardial stunning, also called takotsubo cardiomyopathy, which occurs in up to twenty percent of high-grade SAH patients and can cause significant cardiac dysfunction with regional wall motion abnormalities, elevated troponin, and reduced ejection fraction. This limits your ability to augment blood pressure and may require inotropic support with dobutamine or milrinone rather than pure vasopressors. You mention neurogenic pulmonary edema, which is caused by a massive sympathetic surge damaging the pulmonary capillary endothelium. It can occur even without fluid overload.

Now for the evidence. Let me consolidate the key trials. The British aneurysm nimodipine trial, published in 1989, is the landmark. It is the only randomized controlled trial to demonstrate improved outcomes after aneurysmal SAH with a pharmacological agent. Nimodipine 60 milligrams every four hours for twenty-one days reduced the incidence of cerebral infarction and poor outcome. It did not significantly reduce angiographic vasospasm, which is further evidence that DCI and vasospasm are not synonymous. The STASH trial, 2014, randomized 803 patients to simvastatin 40 milligrams daily versus placebo. No difference in the primary outcome of modified Rankin Scale at six months. No difference in DCI. Statins are not indicated. The MASH-2 trial, 2012, randomized 1,204 patients across multiple centers to intravenous magnesium sulfate 64 millimoles per day versus placebo for up to twenty days. No difference in outcome. Magnesium is not indicated. The HIMALAIA trial, 2017, showed that prophylactic hypervolemia with albumin did not reduce DCI compared to normovolemia. The CONSCIOUS trials, specifically CONSCIOUS-1 in 2008 and CONSCIOUS-2 in 2011, tested clazosentan, an endothelin receptor antagonist. CONSCIOUS-1 showed a dose-dependent reduction in angiographic vasospasm. However, CONSCIOUS-2 and CONSCIOUS-3, the phase three trials, failed to demonstrate any improvement in DCI or functional outcomes. This is a powerful illustration of the vasospasm-DCI dissociation. You can eliminate the vessel narrowing and still not improve the patient because DCI has additional mechanisms beyond large-vessel spasm. Emerging evidence exists for intrathecal nicardipine. Small case series and retrospective studies suggest that nicardipine pellets or nicardipine injected into the lumbar drain or EVD can reduce vasospasm, but there is no randomized controlled trial, and this is not yet standard of care. You can mention it as an area of active investigation but do not present it as established practice.

Moving to board pearls. Here are your rapid-fire takeaways. First, never say triple-H without immediately clarifying that induced hypertension is the only evidence-supported component. If you say triple-H and leave it there, the examiner will probe, and you will look outdated. Second, vasospasm is not the same as DCI. Say this explicitly. Show the examiner you understand that seventy percent of patients get angiographic vasospasm but only thirty percent get DCI, and that the mechanisms of DCI extend beyond large-vessel narrowing. Third, nimodipine is the only drug with proven outcome benefit after SAH, and it works not by preventing vasospasm but likely through direct neuroprotective effects on the ischemic penumbra. Fourth, your TCD cutoffs must be automatic. MCA mean velocity above 120, concerning. Above 200, severe. Lindegaard ratio above 3, vasospasm. Above 6, severe. These numbers will come up on the boards. Fifth, modified Fisher grade is the strongest predictor of vasospasm risk, not Hunt-Hess grade. Hunt-Hess predicts overall outcome; modified Fisher predicts vasospasm. Sixth, balloon angioplasty is for proximal vessels only, is more durable than pharmacological treatment, and carries a two to five percent rupture risk. You never balloon a distal vessel. Seventh, always check sodium on day seven. Cerebral salt wasting with hyponatremia can mimic DCI and is easily correctable with hypertonic saline. Do not miss a treatable cause.

Three things that will fail you on this topic. One, treating vasospasm and DCI as interchangeable terms. Two, advocating for hypervolemia or triple-H therapy without acknowledging the current evidence against it. Three, not knowing your drug doses and TCD thresholds by number. The examiner is listening for specific values: nimodipine 60 milligrams every four hours, verapamil 10 to 20 milligrams intra-arterial, nicardipine 5 to 10 milligrams intra-arterial, MCA velocity cutoffs, Lindegaard ratio cutoffs. If you are vague, you will be pushed until you either produce the number or reveal that you do not know it.

That covers cerebral vasospasm protocols. You're ready for this one.
