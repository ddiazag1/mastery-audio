Let's master tethered cord syndrome for the oral boards.

This topic is an examiner favorite for one specific reason: it is the clearest test in pediatric neurosurgery of whether a candidate treats images or treats patients. A low lying conus on a scan is a radiographic finding. Tethered cord syndrome is a clinical diagnosis. Candidates who conflate the two will recommend operating on children who do not need surgery and will be taken apart for it. Let's build the topic around that distinction.

Start with the embryology and anatomy. During development, the spinal cord initially extends the full length of the vertebral canal. Because the vertebral column grows faster than the cord, the conus medullaris ascends in relative terms during fetal life and early infancy. By term, the conus typically lies at approximately the second or third lumbar level, and by roughly two to three months of age it has reached the adult position at or above the lower border of the first lumbar vertebra, or at most the L one to L two disc space. The filum terminale, a fibrous extension of the pia from the conus to the coccyx, normally measures less than roughly two millimeters in diameter and is elastic.

The pathophysiology is mechanical and then metabolic. Abnormal tethering fixes the caudal cord, so normal movement of the spine, particularly flexion, and longitudinal growth impose traction on the conus. This stretch reduces blood flow and impairs oxidative metabolism in the lumbosacral cord, producing progressive dysfunction. The key consequence is that symptoms characteristically appear or accelerate during periods of rapid growth, which is why children present around growth spurts, and why a child who was previously stable can decline. Say that explicitly, because it explains the natural history and justifies surveillance.

Now the causes, which you should be able to enumerate. Tight or thickened filum terminale, sometimes called primary tethered cord, where the filum is short, thickened, or fatty. Lipomyelomeningocele and other spinal lipomas, subclassified as dorsal, transitional, and terminal. Retethering after prior myelomeningocele repair, which is extremely common and essentially expected. Diastematomyelia, or split cord malformation, where a bony or fibrous septum divides the cord. Dermal sinus tract, with or without an associated inclusion dermoid or epidermoid. Terminal myelocystocele. Meningocele manqué, with fibrous bands tethering the cord dorsally. Caudal regression syndrome and anorectal malformations. Scarring after trauma, infection, or prior surgery. And in adults, presentation may follow a precipitating event such as a fall, a lithotomy position, or pregnancy.

Now the clinical presentation, organized by age because it differs substantially.

In neonates and infants, the presentation is usually cutaneous rather than neurologic. Look for the midline lumbosacral stigmata: a subcutaneous lipoma or fatty mass, a hairy patch or faun tail, a dermal sinus or dimple, a capillary hemangioma or port wine stain, a skin tag or human tail, an atypical or asymmetric gluteal cleft, and a dimple located above the gluteal crease or more than two and a half centimeters from the anal verge. Simple, low, midline sacrococcygeal dimples within the gluteal crease are common and benign and generally do not require imaging; the concerning dimples are high, large, off midline, or accompanied by other stigmata. Roughly half or more of children with a closed dysraphism have a cutaneous marker, which is why the newborn back examination matters.

In older children, the presentation is the classic progressive syndrome. Urologic dysfunction is often the earliest and most sensitive: new or recurrent urinary tract infections, incontinence or failure to achieve continence, urgency, retention, and constipation. Motor findings include progressive weakness, gait deterioration, and increasing spasticity, often asymmetric. Sensory loss in the lower extremities and saddle region. Orthopedic findings are frequently the presenting complaint: progressive scoliosis, particularly an atypical curve, and foot deformities such as a progressive cavovarus foot, claw toes, or asymmetric foot size, and leg length discrepancy. Pain in the back and legs, often poorly localized and non-dermatomal.

In adults, pain is the dominant complaint, typically diffuse low back and perineal pain exacerbated by activity and flexion, along with progressive urologic decline and lower extremity symptoms.

Emphasize the word progressive throughout. A static deficit from a congenital lesion is not the same as a progressive deficit from tethering, and only the latter constitutes the syndrome.

Now the evaluation. Imaging in infants under roughly three to six months can use ultrasound, which visualizes the conus position and cord motion well before the posterior elements ossify, making it an excellent screening study. Beyond that, magnetic resonance of the entire spine is the study of choice, and you should image the whole spine, not just the lumbar region, because associated anomalies including syrinx, split cord, and additional lesions occur at other levels. Assess the conus level, the filum thickness and fatty content, the presence of a lipoma and its configuration, any septum, and the presence of syringomyelia.

Critically, obtain formal urodynamic studies, not just a history. Urodynamics detect detrusor overactivity, detrusor sphincter dyssynergia, reduced compliance, and elevated leak point pressures before the child or family notices anything, and they provide an objective, reproducible baseline against which to measure progression and postoperative change. Saying that you would obtain preoperative and postoperative urodynamics is one of the strongest single statements you can make on this topic, because it demonstrates that you understand how to document progression objectively.

Add neurophysiologic assessment as needed, plain radiographs and scoliosis films for the orthopedic assessment, and renal ultrasound to assess for upper tract changes from a neurogenic bladder.

Now the management decision, which is the core of the examination.

For a symptomatic patient with documented progression, the indication is clear: surgical untethering. The purpose of surgery is to arrest progression, not to reverse established deficits. Counsel accordingly: pain typically responds best, urologic function may stabilize or modestly improve particularly if treated early, motor deficits often stabilize but established weakness frequently does not recover, and orthopedic deformity does not correct itself and may still require orthopedic management. Setting these expectations honestly is part of the graded answer.

For the asymptomatic patient with a radiographic abnormality, the decision is genuinely contested and you should present it as such rather than asserting a rule. For a simple thickened or fatty filum in an asymptomatic child, sectioning the filum is a low morbidity procedure and many surgeons offer it prophylactically to prevent later deterioration. For an asymptomatic complex lipoma, the calculus is very different: prophylactic surgery carries meaningful risk of neurologic injury and a high retethering rate, and several series suggest that outcomes of prophylactic surgery in asymptomatic complex lipomas may not exceed careful observation. The defensible answer is that you would individualize, counsel the family about the uncertainty, and if choosing observation, commit to structured surveillance with serial neurologic examinations, serial urodynamics, and scoliosis monitoring, with a low threshold to operate at the first objective sign of progression.

The same principle governs the child with a repaired myelomeningocele. Every one of them has a radiographically tethered, low lying cord; that is the expected postoperative anatomy. You do not untether based on the image. You untether when the child demonstrates progressive deterioration in motor function, urologic function, scoliosis, spasticity, or pain, and after you have excluded the alternative explanations, which is a crucial step: shunt malfunction, symptomatic Chiari two malformation, syringomyelia, and hydromyelia can all produce identical progressive deterioration. Always check the shunt first in a child with myelomeningocele who is deteriorating. Stating that differential before you commit to untethering is exactly what the examiner is waiting for.

Now the surgical principles. Position prone, and use intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring throughout, including somatosensory and motor evoked potentials, electromyography of the lower extremity muscles, and crucially the bulbocavernosus reflex and anal sphincter electromyography to monitor the sacral roots, because sphincter function is the thing you are most trying to protect and most at risk of injuring. Say that you would monitor the sphincter, because it is the specific detail that distinguishes a candidate who has done these operations.

Expose via laminectomy or laminoplasty, generally starting at a normal level above the lesion to find normal dura and a normal plane before working into the abnormal anatomy. Open the dura in the midline. For a tight filum, identify the filum, distinguishing it from a nerve root using its characteristic appearance with a small vessel on its surface, its lack of response to stimulation at appropriate thresholds, and its position, then coagulate and divide it, confirming retraction of the ends. For a lipoma, the goal is untethering and restoration of a capacious thecal sac, not heroic total resection: pursue subtotal debulking with reconstruction of the neural placode, because aggressive attempts at complete resection significantly increase neurologic morbidity. For a split cord malformation type one, resect the bony septum and reconstruct a single dural tube. For a dermal sinus, excise the entire tract to its termination.

Close the dura watertight, consider a duraplasty to enlarge the thecal sac and reduce retethering, and keep the patient flat for a period postoperatively to reduce cerebrospinal fluid leak, which is the most common complication. Other complications include wound infection and breakdown, particularly over a previously operated and scarred lumbosacral region, new neurologic deficit especially sphincter dysfunction, pseudomeningocele, and retethering.

Address retethering directly, because it is common, particularly after lipoma surgery and after myelomeningocele repair, with rates commonly quoted in the range of ten to twenty percent or higher depending on the lesion and duration of follow up. Repeat untethering is more difficult and carries higher risk, and for the patient with multiple prior untethering procedures, spinal column shortening osteotomy is an alternative strategy that reduces tension without re-entering the scarred dura, and naming it demonstrates current knowledge.

Be precise about the urodynamic findings, because they are the objective backbone of the whole topic. The abnormalities that matter are detrusor overactivity, detrusor sphincter dyssynergia, reduced bladder compliance, elevated detrusor leak point pressure above roughly forty centimetres of water, increased post void residual, and a denervated or absent bulbocavernosus reflex on electromyography. These appear before the child or the family notices anything, and a deterioration between two studies is the single most defensible trigger for surgery in an otherwise stable patient. Say that you would obtain a study at diagnosis, before any operation, and at intervals afterwards, and that you would treat a documented decline rather than an image.

Give the occult tethered cord controversy an honest airing, because an examiner may probe it deliberately. Some patients present with the classic clinical syndrome, including urological dysfunction and pain, but have a conus in normal position and a filum of normal calibre. Proponents of the occult tethered cord concept argue that filum section still benefits these patients; critics point out that the evidence rests largely on uncontrolled series with substantial placebo potential and that the diagnosis risks operating on children with non-neurogenic bladder dysfunction. The defensible position is to acknowledge the controversy explicitly, to insist on objective urodynamic abnormality and exclusion of non-neurogenic causes before considering surgery, and to avoid asserting either dogma. Demonstrating that you know where the evidence is weak is worth more than picking a side.

Add the adult presentation, since it differs and is easy to neglect. Adults present predominantly with pain, typically diffuse low back, perineal and lower limb pain, worse with activity, flexion and prolonged sitting, often triggered by a precipitating event such as a fall, a lithotomy position, pregnancy or heavy lifting. Progressive urological decline and lower limb weakness follow. Adults tolerate untethering less well than children, with higher rates of cerebrospinal fluid leak and wound problems, and pain responds better than deficit. The presence of a previously undiagnosed lipoma, split cord or filum lipoma on imaging in an adult with new progressive symptoms is the situation in which surgery is most clearly justified.

Deepen the surgical description. Position prone on a frame that keeps the abdomen free to reduce epidural venous pressure. Use intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring throughout: somatosensory and motor evoked potentials, free running and triggered electromyography of the lower limb myotomes, and critically the bulbocavernosus reflex and anal sphincter electromyography, because sphincter function is both the thing you are protecting and the thing most easily injured. Expose through a laminectomy or laminoplasty beginning at a normal level above the pathology so that you enter normal dura and find a clean plane before working into abnormal anatomy. Open the dura in the midline under magnification.

For a tight filum, identify it by its position at the dorsal midline caudal to the conus, its distinctive whitish appearance, the characteristic small vessel running on its surface, and its failure to produce a motor response at stimulation thresholds that do excite adjacent roots. Confirm with stimulation before dividing anything, coagulate and divide it, and observe the cut ends retract, which confirms it was under tension. For a lipoma, the goal is untethering and creation of a capacious thecal sac, not heroic total resection: debulk subtotally, reconstruct the neural placode by suturing the pial edges to reduce the raw surface, and accept residual fat rather than risk the placode. For a split cord malformation type one, resect the bony or osteocartilaginous spur, remove the intervening dural sleeve and reconstruct a single dural tube. For a dermal sinus, excise the entire tract to its termination, however deep, and deal with any associated inclusion dermoid.

Close with a watertight dural repair, consider an expansile duraplasty to enlarge the sac and reduce retethering, use a fascial or muscle closure in layers, and keep the patient flat for a period postoperatively to reduce cerebrospinal fluid leak, which is the commonest complication and is more likely over a previously operated, scarred lumbosacral region. Other complications include wound breakdown, infection, pseudomeningocele, new neurological deficit particularly of sphincter function, and retethering.

Quantify retethering, because it shapes counselling. Rates depend on the lesion and the duration of follow up, and are commonly quoted around ten to twenty percent overall, appreciably higher after complex lipoma surgery and after myelomeningocele repair, and higher again with longer follow up. Repeat untethering is technically harder and carries greater risk of deficit. For the patient with multiple prior untethering procedures and recurrent symptoms, spinal column shortening osteotomy, usually a posterior vertebral column resection in the thoracolumbar region, relieves tension on the cord without re-entering the scarred dura and is the current salvage strategy worth naming.

Finally, set expectations honestly in the consent conversation. Surgery arrests progression; it does not reliably reverse established deficit. Pain responds best. Urological function may stabilise or improve modestly, and improves most when treated early. Motor deficits usually stabilise but established weakness often persists. Orthopaedic deformity does not correct itself and will still need orthopaedic management. And the child will need lifelong surveillance, because growth can retether a cord that was successfully released.

To summarize. The conus reaches its adult position above L one to L two by about three months. Tethering produces traction, ischemia, and progressive dysfunction, which accelerates during growth. Look for cutaneous stigmata in infants and for progressive urologic, motor, orthopedic, and pain symptoms in children. Image the whole spine and obtain formal urodynamics before and after surgery. Operate for documented progression. In asymptomatic patients, section a simple filum readily but individualize carefully for complex lipomas. In a deteriorating child with a repaired myelomeningocele, check the shunt and evaluate Chiari and syrinx before attributing the decline to tethering. Monitor the sphincter intraoperatively, debulk rather than chase complete lipoma resection, and counsel that surgery arrests progression rather than restoring lost function.
