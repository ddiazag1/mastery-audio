questions:
  - q: What sagittal vertical axis threshold predicts disability, and on whose evidence?
    a: Be careful with this one. Glassman's 2005 study in Spine correlated standing radiographic parameters with the SF-36 and Oswestry Disability Index and found disability rises linearly with sagittal vertical axis rather than at a single inflection point, becoming clinically important beyond roughly five centimetres. Nine point five centimetres is a different number: it is the SRS-Schwab double-plus modifier, the most severe grade.
    probe: What is the routine target you aim for surgically?
    probeA: Sagittal vertical axis under five centimetres, pelvic tilt under twenty degrees, and pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis under ten degrees. Those are the SRS-Schwab grade zero thresholds and achieving all three correlates with the greatest improvement in patient-reported outcomes. I would add the age caveat from Lafage: ideal alignment relaxes with age, with ideal sagittal vertical axis rising roughly a centimetre per decade after forty and the acceptable PI-LL mismatch widening, so applying a forty-year-old's targets to a seventy-five-year-old overcorrects and invites proximal junctional kyphosis.
  - q: Give me the SRS-Schwab classification in full.
    a: A coronal curve type — T thoracic, L lumbar, D double, N no major coronal deformity — plus three sagittal modifiers: pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis, sagittal vertical axis, and pelvic tilt, each graded zero, plus or double plus.
    probe: Which pelvic tilt grade is the most severe modifier?
    probeA: Double plus, which is a pelvic tilt greater than thirty degrees. Zero is under twenty, plus is twenty to thirty, and double plus is over thirty. A double-plus pelvic tilt tells me the patient is maximally retroverting the pelvis to keep the trunk over the hips, so it is a measure of how much compensatory reserve has already been spent rather than of the deformity itself.
  - q: What pelvic tilt defines the most severe modifier?
    a: Greater than thirty degrees. High pelvic tilt means the patient is retroverting the pelvis to stay upright, so it is a marker of compensation rather than a primary deformity, and it is what fails first when they fatigue.
    probe: Name the other compensatory mechanisms you would look for.
    probeA: Pelvic retroversion, which is what the pelvic tilt measures. Then knee flexion and ankle dorsiflexion, which is why these patients stand with bent knees and why you should look at the legs, not just the spine. In the spine itself, thoracic hypokyphosis, cervical hyperlordosis to keep the head level and the eyes horizontal, and hip extension. Segmental hyperextension at levels adjacent to a fusion is another. The point is that a patient can appear balanced on a standing film while having exhausted all of these, which is why pelvic tilt and knee flexion matter as much as the sagittal vertical axis itself.
  - q: Name the osteotomy grades and their correction.
    a: The SRS-Schwab anatomical osteotomy classification, grades one to six. Smith-Petersen osteotomy occupies the lower grades, giving roughly ten degrees per level. Pedicle subtraction osteotomy is a three-column osteotomy giving around thirty degrees at a single level. Vertebral column resection is the highest grade with the greatest correction and the greatest risk.
    probe: Which would you choose for a fixed flat-back after a prior fusion?
    probeA: A pedicle subtraction osteotomy, which is a grade three three-column osteotomy giving around thirty degrees of correction at a single level. A Smith-Petersen or Ponte osteotomy will not work here, because it relies on lengthening the anterior column through a mobile disc, and in a fixed flat-back after a solid prior fusion the anterior column is ankylosed and cannot open. So the posterior column shortening of a PSO, which does not depend on a mobile disc, is the correct choice. A vertebral column resection would give more correction but carries substantially greater neurological and haemorrhagic risk and is reserved for sharp angular or coronal deformity.
  - q: Where do you site a pedicle subtraction osteotomy, and why?
    a: In the lower lumbar spine, typically L3 or L4, because lordosis is normally maximal there and correction at that level most efficiently restores the sagittal vertical axis. In flat-back after a prior L4-5 fusion, I would site it above the fusion mass at L3.
    probe: What is the trade-off of going lower rather than higher?
    probeA: Correction lower down restores the sagittal vertical axis more efficiently, because the lever arm to the head is longer, so each degree of lordosis gained at L4 translates into more forward displacement corrected than the same degree at L2. The trade-offs are that the lower lumbar spine is where the lordosis physiologically belongs, so correcting there restores a more normal shape, but the cauda equina and the L5 root are at greater risk, the vascular anatomy anteriorly is less forgiving, and the closure is harder with a wide sacrum below. Going higher is technically easier and safer neurologically, but risks creating an unnatural lordosis at the wrong level with compensatory problems and less efficient correction. Most surgeons site it at L3, which is the practical compromise.
  - q: What is the rod fracture rate at a PSO site, and what do you do about it?
    a: Fifteen to twenty-five percent with a single rod construct. Accessory rods are strongly recommended — satellite or four-rod constructs across the osteotomy — because the three-column osteotomy concentrates stress precisely at the site where the construct is most vulnerable.
    probe: What else reduces mechanical failure in these constructs?
    probeA: Achieving a solid fusion, because a construct only has to survive until the bone does the work: meticulous decortication and grafting, bone morphogenetic protein where appropriate, and interbody support at the base of the construct. Anterior column support at L5-S1 and L4-5 with interbody cages, which shares load and protects the rods. Extending fixation to the pelvis with iliac or S2-alar-iliac screws in long constructs. Avoiding overcorrection relative to the patient's age-adjusted target, since overcorrection drives proximal junctional failure. Optimising the patient: treating osteoporosis before surgery with an anabolic agent where possible, nutrition, smoking cessation and diabetic control. And using cobalt chrome or titanium rods of adequate diameter rather than accepting a single small-diameter rod across a three-column osteotomy.
  - q: When would you stage the operation?
    a: In long, high-blood-loss reconstructions or frail patients. Staging can mean an anterior release followed by posterior instrumented fusion, or a posterior-only approach divided into two sessions separated by a few days, allowing resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy and reassessment between stages.
    probe: What are the risks of staging?
    probeA: A second anaesthetic and a second physiological insult, which in a frail patient is not trivial. The specific risks are infection, since the wound is reopened and the interval carries a risk of contamination; further blood loss and transfusion; thromboembolism during the interval of immobility; and the possibility of neurological injury occurring between stages when the spine is destabilised but not yet instrumented, so I would not leave a spine destabilised between sessions. There is also a real risk of deterioration in the interval such that the second stage becomes higher risk or does not happen at all. Against all that, staging allows resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy and reassessment, so it is a judgement rather than a rule.
  - q: What are your three surgical targets?
    a: Sagittal vertical axis under five centimetres, pelvic tilt under twenty degrees, and pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis under ten degrees. Pelvic incidence itself is fixed and cannot be changed — you change lordosis to match it.
    probe: What happens if you under-correct the PI-LL mismatch?
    probeA: The patient remains symptomatic and the construct fails. Residual PI-LL mismatch means the patient continues to compensate through pelvic retroversion and knee flexion, so the fatigue, the inability to stand for long, and often the pain persist despite a technically successful operation. Mechanically, the unbalanced spine loads the construct eccentrically, which raises the rate of rod fracture, pseudarthrosis and, most characteristically, proximal junctional kyphosis and failure as the segments above the fusion are forced to compensate. Under-correction is one of the strongest predictors of revision surgery in the deformity literature, which is why the intraoperative assessment of alignment before closing matters so much.
cases:
  - label: Flat back after prior fusion
    stem: A 64-year-old woman had an L4-5 fusion eight years ago. She now cannot stand upright for more than ten minutes, walks with flexed knees, and has severe back pain. Standing 36-inch films show a sagittal vertical axis of 11 centimetres, pelvic incidence 58 degrees, lumbar lordosis 22 degrees, and pelvic tilt 34 degrees.
    q1: Interpret those numbers and say what her body is doing.
    a1: A sagittal vertical axis of eleven centimetres is far beyond the five centimetre level at which Glassman showed disability climbing, and past nine point five, the most severe SRS-Schwab modifier. Pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis is fifty-eight minus twenty-two, a mismatch of thirty-six degrees against a target under ten. Pelvic tilt of thirty-four is above thirty, the most severe SRS-Schwab modifier. She is retroverting her pelvis and flexing her knees to stay upright, and she has run out of compensation.
    p1: She is 64. Should you be using the age-adjusted targets instead?
    pa1: Yes, and it is an important correction to the standard numbers. The Lafage age-adjusted formulas recognise that ideal alignment relaxes with age, with acceptable sagittal vertical axis rising roughly a centimetre per decade after forty and the acceptable pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis mismatch widening similarly, so a sixty-four-year-old does not need the alignment of a forty-year-old. Applying the young targets to her risks overcorrection, which is one of the strongest predictors of proximal junctional kyphosis and failure. So rather than driving her to a sagittal vertical axis under five and a mismatch under ten, I would aim for an age-appropriate target somewhat more relaxed than that, while still achieving a very substantial correction from where she is.
    q2: What correction do you need and by what technique?
    a2: She needs roughly thirty-five degrees of lordosis restored. A Smith-Petersen osteotomy gives about ten degrees per level and relies on a mobile anterior column, which she does not have across a solid L4-5 fusion. So this is a pedicle subtraction osteotomy, a three-column osteotomy giving around thirty degrees at a single level, sited at L3 above the fusion mass.
    p2: Could you avoid the osteotomy altogether with anterior interbody work?
    pa2: Possibly, and it deserves consideration before committing to a three-column osteotomy. Anterior column realignment, using a hyperlordotic interbody cage through a lateral or anterior approach with release of the anterior longitudinal ligament, can generate twenty to thirty degrees of lordosis per level at mobile segments, and multilevel interbody work at L1-2, L2-3 and L3-4 could deliver much of her thirty-five degrees without the blood loss and neurological risk of a pedicle subtraction osteotomy. The constraint is that it requires mobile discs, so it works above her fused L4-5 but not across it, and it carries its own vascular, visceral and lumbar plexus risks. In practice I would consider a hybrid: anterior column realignment at the mobile levels, reserving or reducing the osteotomy, which is increasingly the direction of the field.
    q3: What is the main mechanical risk and how do you mitigate it?
    a3: Rod fracture at the osteotomy site, which approaches fifteen to twenty-five percent with a single rod construct because the three-column osteotomy concentrates stress there. I would use accessory or satellite rods across the osteotomy, ensure meticulous bone grafting to achieve fusion, and consider extending the construct to the pelvis given the magnitude of correction.
    p3: What is her risk of proximal junctional kyphosis, and can you reduce it?
    pa3: High, and it is the commonest mechanical complication of this surgery, reported in roughly twenty to forty percent of long constructs with proximal junctional failure requiring revision in a smaller proportion. Her risk factors are age, osteoporosis, the magnitude of correction and a long fusion to the pelvis. To reduce it: avoid overcorrection relative to her age-adjusted target, which is the single most modifiable factor; choose the upper instrumented vertebra carefully, avoiding ending at the thoracolumbar junction; preserve the posterior ligamentous complex and facet capsules above the construct; use a gradual transition in rod stiffness rather than an abrupt one, with techniques such as tethers, hooks rather than screws at the top, or cement augmentation; and treat her osteoporosis before surgery, ideally with an anabolic agent such as teriparatide.
    q4: She is 64 with osteoporosis. Would you stage it, and what do you tell her about risk?
    a4: I would consider staging, either anterior release then posterior instrumentation, or a posterior-only approach split across two sessions days apart, allowing resuscitation and correction of coagulopathy between. I would be frank that adult deformity surgery carries major complication rates in the tens of percent — blood loss, infection, neurological injury, proximal junctional kyphosis, pseudarthrosis and reoperation — and I would optimise her bone density and nutrition first, because operating on untreated osteoporosis invites junctional failure.
    p4: How would you treat her osteoporosis, and how long would you delay surgery?
    pa4: I would obtain a DEXA scan and a CT Hounsfield unit measurement of the lumbar vertebrae, which is a useful opportunistic measure of local bone quality, and involve a metabolic bone service. For a patient facing instrumented deformity surgery I would favour an anabolic agent, teriparatide or abaloparatide, or romosozumab, rather than a bisphosphonate, because anabolic agents build bone and there is evidence they improve fusion rates and reduce screw loosening and junctional failure, whereas bisphosphonates are antiresorptive and their effect on fusion is less favourable. That means a delay of around three to six months of treatment before surgery, which is a genuine trade-off against her current disability. I would also correct vitamin D and calcium, address nutrition, and insist on smoking cessation if relevant.

Welcome to this oral board teaching session on adult spinal deformity principles. This is one of the higher-yield spine topics for the ABNS oral examination because it integrates radiographic analysis, classification systems, surgical decision-making, and complication management into a single clinical problem. If you can command this topic, you demonstrate the kind of systematic thinking examiners reward. Let's build from the ground up.

We begin with sagittal balance, which is the single most important concept in adult spinal deformity. The entire field has shifted over the past two decades from a coronal-plane focus to a sagittal-plane focus, because research has conclusively shown that sagittal malalignment is the strongest radiographic predictor of patient-reported disability. A patient can tolerate a moderate coronal curve, but a patient who cannot stand upright over her pelvis is functionally devastated. Everything in your surgical planning flows from restoring sagittal balance, so you must know the spinopelvic parameters cold.

Pelvic Incidence, or PI, is the fixed anatomic parameter. It is the angle between a line drawn perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and a line drawn from that midpoint to the center of the femoral heads. PI does not change after skeletal maturity. It is a morphologic constant for each individual patient, and the normal average is approximately 50 degrees, though the range in the general population spans roughly 35 to 85 degrees. PI is the anchor around which you calculate everything else.

Lumbar Lordosis, or LL, is the Cobb angle measured from the superior endplate of L1 to the superior endplate of S1 on a standing lateral radiograph. In a well-aligned spine, lumbar lordosis closely matches pelvic incidence. The critical metric is PI minus LL mismatch. When PI minus LL exceeds 10 degrees, that mismatch is considered pathologic. It means the lumbar spine is not lordotic enough to balance the pelvis, and the patient will pitch forward. This is the number one driver of surgical planning in adult deformity correction.

The Sagittal Vertical Axis, or SVA, is measured by dropping a plumb line from the center of the C7 vertebral body and measuring its horizontal distance to the posterosuperior corner of the S1 endplate. Normal SVA is less than 5 centimeters. When SVA exceeds 9.5 centimeters, it strongly and reliably correlates with clinical disability as measured by validated outcomes instruments including the Oswestry Disability Index and the SF-36 physical component score. This 9.5-centimeter threshold comes from the landmark work by Glassman and colleagues, and it is a number you should be able to cite on the boards.

Pelvic Tilt, or PT, is the angle between a vertical reference line and a line from the center of the sacral endplate to the center of the femoral heads. Pelvic tilt represents compensatory pelvic retroversion. When the spine falls forward, the pelvis rotates backward to keep the head over the feet. A PT greater than 25 degrees indicates the patient has reached maximal pelvic compensation and has exhausted this reserve. On the SRS-Schwab classification, a PT greater than 30 degrees is the most severe modifier grade.

The T1 Pelvic Angle, or TPA, is a newer parameter that combines the effects of SVA and pelvic tilt into a single angular measure. It is the angle between a line from the center of the T1 vertebral body to the femoral head axis and a line from the femoral head axis to the center of the sacral endplate. A TPA greater than 20 degrees correlates with significant disability. The advantage of TPA is that it is not affected by patient positioning or knee flexion the way SVA can be, making it a more reliable single measure of global sagittal alignment.

Now let's lock in the SRS-Schwab classification, because examiners will expect you to classify any adult deformity case using this system. It has two components. The first is the coronal curve type. Type T is a thoracic-only curve where the apex is at T9 or above. Type L is a thoracolumbar or lumbar curve with the apex at T10 or below. Type D is a double curve, meaning both thoracic and lumbar structural curves are present. Type N means no major coronal deformity, which is the flat-back or pure sagittal malalignment patient. The second component is the three sagittal modifiers, and these are what truly drive surgical planning and predict outcomes. The PI-LL modifier is graded 0 when mismatch is less than 10 degrees, plus when mismatch is 10 to 20 degrees, and double-plus when mismatch exceeds 20 degrees. The Global Alignment modifier uses SVA and is graded 0 when SVA is less than 4 centimeters, plus when SVA is 4 to 9.5 centimeters, and double-plus when SVA exceeds 9.5 centimeters. The Pelvic Tilt modifier is graded 0 when PT is less than 20 degrees, plus when PT is 20 to 30 degrees, and double-plus when PT exceeds 30 degrees. The validation studies for this classification demonstrated that the sagittal modifiers, particularly the double-plus grades, are the strongest predictors of poor health-related quality of life and therefore the strongest indicators that surgical correction will provide meaningful benefit.

With that foundation established, let's work through a clinical case the way you would encounter it on the oral boards.

You are presented with a 68-year-old woman with progressive forward stoop over the past five years. She reports that she cannot stand fully upright and experiences significant low back pain that limits her walking tolerance to approximately one block. She uses a shopping cart or walker to lean on in order to stay upright when she goes out. Her daily activities are severely limited. She has tried physical therapy, epidural steroid injections, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications without meaningful improvement. Her surgical history is notable for an L4-5 laminectomy performed ten years ago for symptomatic spinal stenosis. Her medical comorbidities include controlled hypertension and well-managed type 2 diabetes. Her standing full-spine 36-inch scoliosis radiographs reveal the following measurements. Lumbar lordosis is 15 degrees. Her pelvic incidence is 60 degrees, giving a PI-LL mismatch of 45 degrees. Her SVA is 12 centimeters. Her pelvic tilt is 35 degrees. Her thoracic kyphosis is 45 degrees, which is within normal range and not contributing to the sagittal imbalance. On the coronal films, she has a 25-degree thoracolumbar curve. Using the SRS-Schwab classification, this is a Type L deformity with PI-LL double-plus, SVA double-plus, and PT double-plus. This is a severely malaligned patient across all three sagittal modifiers. Notice that the examiner has given you a patient who has failed conservative management, which establishes the surgical indication. The examiner wants to see that you recognize the severity and can plan accordingly.

Now we move into the examiner interrogation phase, where the pressure ramps up. Expect the questions to come rapidly and to test whether you have a systematic approach.

The first line of questioning will address your complete radiographic and preoperative workup. You need standing 36-inch posteroanterior and lateral scoliosis films. These must be standing, not supine, because sagittal alignment changes dramatically with position. You need a CT scan of the lumbar spine to assess bone quality at planned pedicle screw sites, to evaluate the prior laminectomy fusion status, and to plan osteotomy anatomy. You need an MRI to assess for neural element compression, particularly at the apex of the curve and adjacent to the prior surgical level. And critically, you need a DEXA scan. Bone mineral density is essential for fixation planning in a 68-year-old woman. Osteoporosis fundamentally changes your construct strategy, your osteotomy safety, and your risk of proximal junctional failure. Do not forget the DEXA.

The examiner will then push you on alignment targets. Your goals for this patient are to restore PI-LL mismatch to less than 10 degrees, to bring SVA to less than 5 centimeters, and to reduce pelvic tilt to less than 20 degrees. Since her PI is 60 degrees, you need to achieve approximately 50 to 60 degrees of lumbar lordosis. She currently has 15 degrees. That means you need roughly 45 degrees of lordosis correction. State that number clearly and confidently.

The next question is how you achieve that correction, and this is where osteotomy selection comes in. You must know the osteotomy hierarchy. A Smith-Petersen osteotomy, also called a Ponte osteotomy, is a posterior column-only osteotomy. You resect the facet joints, the ligamentum flavum, and a portion of the lamina at a given level, then close the posterior column to hinge on the anterior disc space. Each SPO provides approximately 5 to 10 degrees of correction. These are useful for flexible, mobile segments and can be stacked across multiple levels, but they will not give you the magnitude of correction this patient needs at a single level.

A pedicle subtraction osteotomy, or PSO, is a three-column closing wedge osteotomy performed through the vertebral body. You remove the posterior elements, the pedicles, and a wedge of the vertebral body, then close the osteotomy by hinging on the anterior cortex. A PSO provides 25 to 35 degrees of correction at a single level. It is technically demanding. Expected blood loss is 2 to 4 liters. The osteotomy creates a fixed point where all three columns meet, and rod contouring at this site must be precise. For this patient who needs 45 degrees of correction, a PSO at the apex of the deformity combined with SPOs at adjacent levels is a reasonable strategy.

A vertebral column resection, or VCR, is the most aggressive osteotomy. You remove the entire vertebral segment including the body, and it provides more than 40 degrees of correction at a single level. Blood loss is typically 3 to 6 liters, and the neurological complication rate is 10 to 15 percent. VCR is reserved for rigid, severe deformities where a PSO cannot achieve adequate correction, or for sharp angular kyphotic deformities. It would be excessive for this patient and the examiner would penalize you for jumping to VCR when a PSO is sufficient.

Fusion level selection is the next topic. For significant adult deformity correction, you typically extend your construct from the upper thoracic spine to the pelvis. The upper instrumented vertebra should be at least T10, and more commonly T2 to T4, because stopping in the thoracolumbar junction exposes the patient to unacceptable rates of proximal junctional kyphosis. When you fuse to the sacrum, you must include pelvic fixation. S2-alar-iliac screws are the current preferred method over traditional iliac bolts because S2AI screws are lower profile, in line with the other screw tulips, and biomechanically provide superior fixation with lower rates of symptomatic hardware prominence. Pelvic fixation dramatically reduces the pseudarthrosis rate at the lumbosacral junction.

For rod selection, cobalt chrome rods provide greater stiffness, which is desirable for maintaining correction across a three-column osteotomy. Titanium rods offer better MRI compatibility. Many surgeons use cobalt chrome primary rods with accessory or satellite rods across the osteotomy site for additional support. At a PSO site, rod fracture rates approach 15 to 25 percent with a single rod construct, so accessory rods are strongly recommended.

Regarding staging, you may perform the procedure as a single operation or as a staged procedure. Staging can mean an anterior release followed by posterior instrumented fusion, or it can mean a posterior-only approach divided into two sessions separated by a few days. The decision depends on operative time, estimated blood loss, and patient comorbidities. In a 68-year-old, keeping each operative session under six to eight hours reduces the physiologic insult. If the total procedure is expected to exceed this, a staged approach is prudent.

Now the examiner will set traps, and you need to recognize them. The first trap question is whether you would perform a PSO in a patient with severe osteoporosis. The answer is that severe osteoporosis dramatically increases the risk of proximal junctional failure and hardware pullout. You must optimize bone density preoperatively. Teriparatide, the anabolic parathyroid hormone analog, is the agent of choice and should be started at least three months before surgery if possible. Intraoperatively, you should use cement augmentation of pedicle screws, wider fixation constructs spanning more levels, and consider reducing the magnitude of correction to lower the mechanical demand on the bone-screw interface. You would not flatly refuse the PSO, but you would insist on optimization first.

The second trap question involves the prior L4-5 surgery. The examiner asks where you would perform the PSO in a patient with flat-back syndrome after a prior L4-5 fusion. The answer is that you perform the PSO at or near the level of the prior fusion, typically at L3 or L4, because that is where the lordosis was lost. You cannot restore lordosis distal to a solid fusion mass. The osteotomy must be placed where the deformity exists. If the prior fusion extends to L5, you may need to perform the PSO at L4 or L3 and accept the constraint that the distal construct remains fused to the pelvis.

The third trap question is whether you would correct this patient to a PI-LL mismatch of zero. The answer requires nuance. While a PI-LL of zero sounds ideal, overcorrection in an elderly patient significantly increases the risk of proximal junctional kyphosis and failure. Lafage and colleagues have published age-adjusted alignment targets showing that older patients tolerate some degree of positive sagittal malalignment. For a 68-year-old, aiming for PI-LL mismatch of 10 degrees or slightly under is more appropriate than pushing to zero. The penalty for overcorrection can be proximal junctional failure requiring revision surgery, which in this population carries substantial morbidity.

Speaking of proximal junctional kyphosis, or PJK, this is the single most common structural complication after adult deformity surgery. The reported incidence ranges from 20 to 40 percent. PJK is defined as an increase of greater than 10 degrees in kyphosis between the upper instrumented vertebra and the vertebra two levels above. Proximal junctional failure, or PJF, is the more severe form involving fracture, hardware pullout, or instability requiring revision. Risk factors include age greater than 65, osteoporosis, placing the upper instrumented vertebra at the thoracolumbar junction rather than the upper thoracic spine, large magnitude of sagittal correction, and disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex and paraspinal musculature at the UIV. Prevention strategies include using transition rods at the proximal end of the construct, ligament augmentation tethers between the UIV and the supra-adjacent level, and hybrid constructs that use hooks or sublaminar bands in addition to pedicle screws at the UIV to distribute the load across a broader area of bone.

For overall complication rates, adult spinal deformity surgery carries a total complication rate of 40 to 80 percent in the major published series. Neurological deficit occurs in 5 to 15 percent of patients, with the highest rates associated with three-column osteotomies. Intraoperative neuromonitoring with somatosensory evoked potentials and motor evoked potentials is mandatory during these cases, and you should mention this proactively to the examiner. A significant neuromonitoring change during osteotomy closure should prompt you to release the closure, check for epidural hematoma or hardware malposition, raise the mean arterial pressure, and reassess signals before proceeding. Pseudarthrosis rates are significant, particularly at the lumbosacral junction if pelvic fixation is not used. Rod fracture occurs most commonly at the osteotomy site, and as mentioned, accessory rods substantially reduce this risk. Infection rates are higher than in standard spine surgery given the length of the incision, the operative time, and the blood loss. Wound complications can be reduced with meticulous layered closure, subfascial drains, and consideration of vancomycin powder in the wound. Medical complications including deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia are common in this elderly population and require aggressive perioperative prophylaxis and early mobilization. Risk stratification tools such as the Scoliosis Research Society morbidity and mortality database and the adult deformity frailty index can help guide patient selection and informed consent.

Let's turn to the evidence base, because examiners may probe your familiarity with the key literature. The SRS-Schwab classification was validated in a series of multicenter studies led by Schwab, Lafage, and colleagues, demonstrating that the sagittal modifiers reliably predict health-related quality of life outcomes and that surgical correction of these modifiers to the threshold targets produces clinically meaningful improvement. The key finding was that achieving all three sagittal modifier thresholds correlated with the greatest improvement in patient-reported outcomes. Glassman's 2005 study in Spine was the pivotal work correlating sagittal vertical axis with disability, using a large multicenter database against the SF-36 and ODI. Its finding was that disability increases linearly with SVA rather than at a threshold, with symptoms becoming clinically important beyond roughly 5 centimeters. The 9.5 centimeter figure belongs to the SRS-Schwab classification, where it defines the most severe SVA modifier. This paper fundamentally shifted the field toward sagittal-plane-focused surgical planning. Lafage and colleagues from the Hospital for Special Surgery published the age-adjusted alignment formulas that modified the original targets to account for the natural loss of lordosis with aging, showing that applying the same targets to a 70-year-old as a 40-year-old leads to overcorrection and proximal junctional problems. Their work introduced the concept that ideal SVA increases by approximately 1 centimeter per decade after age 40, and ideal PI-LL mismatch also relaxes with age. The International Spine Study Group, or ISSG, has published extensively on complication rates, with their prospective multicenter database providing the most robust data on neurological deficits, rod fracture, and PJK incidence after adult deformity surgery. Their data showed that while major complications are frequent, patients who achieve alignment targets still experience significant functional improvement compared to those who remain malaligned. Bridwell's work on osteotomy outcomes established much of our understanding of correction magnitude by osteotomy type and the associated risk profiles, and but the six-grade anatomical osteotomy classification in routine use today is the SRS-Schwab system published in 2014, with SPO at the lower grades and VCR at grade five.

Let's close with board pearls. You must have the spinopelvic parameters memorized without hesitation. PI is fixed. PI minus LL mismatch greater than 10 degrees is pathologic. SVA greater than 9.5 centimeters correlates with disability. PT greater than 25 degrees means maximal compensation. TPA greater than 20 degrees correlates with disability. Know the SRS-Schwab classification in full, including the coronal types T, L, D, and N, and the three sagittal modifiers with their grading thresholds.

Know your osteotomy correction amounts. SPO gives 5 to 10 degrees per level. PSO gives 25 to 35 degrees at a single level. VCR gives more than 40 degrees. Know the associated blood loss and complication profiles.

Know the PJK risk factors. Age, osteoporosis, thoracolumbar junction UIV, large correction, posterior ligament disruption. Know the prevention strategies. Transition rods, ligament augmentation, hybrid hook-screw constructs.

When the examiner asks about osteotomy in an osteoporotic patient, talk about teriparatide, cement augmentation, and wider constructs. When asked about PSO location, place it where the deformity exists. When asked about correction targets, respect age-adjusted alignment and do not overcorrect the elderly patient. And always mention neuromonitoring. If you forget intraoperative neuromonitoring during a three-column osteotomy discussion, the examiner will wonder whether you actually do these cases.

Finally, remember that adult deformity is fundamentally a quality-of-life operation. The examiner may ask you about patient selection. The ideal surgical candidate has failed appropriate conservative management, has significant functional disability correlating with documented sagittal malalignment, is medically optimized for a lengthy surgical procedure, and understands the high complication profile. A patient who is unwilling to accept a 40 to 80 percent complication rate or who has unrealistic expectations about pain relief is not a good surgical candidate regardless of how dramatic the radiographic malalignment appears.

That covers adult spinal deformity principles. You're ready for this one.
