questions:
  - q: Give me the Wiltse aetiological classification.
    a: Five types: dysplastic, isthmic, degenerative, traumatic and pathological. It is the standard aetiological classification and you must know all five, because the type determines the natural history and the operation far more than the grade does.
    probe: Which type appears in an adolescent athlete?
    probeA: Isthmic, Wiltse type II, which arises from a defect in the pars interarticularis. In an adolescent athlete it is typically type IIA, a fatigue or stress fracture of the pars from repetitive hyperextension and rotation, classically in gymnasts, cricket fast bowlers, divers, weightlifters and American footballers. It occurs overwhelmingly at L5-S1. The other subtypes are IIB, an elongated but intact pars from repeated healed microfractures, and IIC, an acute traumatic pars fracture, which is rare.
  - q: Grade it by Meyerding and tell me what grade V is.
    a: Percentage translation of the superior body on the inferior: grade I up to twenty-five percent, II to fifty, III to seventy-five, IV to one hundred, and grade V — spondyloptosis — greater than one hundred percent, where the superior body has fallen completely off the front of the inferior one.
    probe: Is the Meyerding grade your main surgical target?
    probeA: No, and that is the central teaching point of this topic. The Meyerding grade measures translation, which is easy to see and relatively unimportant biomechanically. What determines sagittal alignment, symptoms and the outcome of surgery is the slip angle, or lumbosacral kyphosis, together with the sacropelvic parameters. So my target is to correct the slip angle from, say, forty degrees to under ten, restoring lumbosacral lordosis, while reducing translation only partially. Chasing the Meyerding grade toward anatomical reduction is precisely how the L5 root gets injured, so the grade describes the deformity rather than defining the goal.
  - q: Why does a high pelvic incidence matter?
    a: A pelvic incidence above sixty degrees predisposes to high-grade slip, because the sacrum is more vertical and greater shear force is placed across L5-S1. It is an anatomical driver of progression, not an incidental measurement, and it also shapes the reconstruction.
    probe: How does that feed into the Hresko classification?
    probeA: Pelvic incidence is the fixed morphological driver, and Hresko's contribution is to show that what matters clinically is how the pelvis is oriented in response to it. In a high-grade slip the patient either maintains a balanced pelvis, with a relatively high sacral slope and low pelvic tilt, or decompensates into an unbalanced, retroverted pelvis, with a low sacral slope and high pelvic tilt, adopting the crouched posture. The balanced group can be managed with in situ fusion. The unbalanced group has exhausted compensation and requires reduction to restore sagittal alignment, because fusing them in situ locks in the deformity. So a high pelvic incidence predisposes to the slip; the sacral slope and pelvic tilt tell me whether they have compensated for it.
  - q: What is the Hresko classification for?
    a: Proposed in 2007 and refined by the Spinal Deformity Study Group, it classifies high-grade spondylolisthesis by pelvic incidence and sacropelvic balance into two subtypes: a balanced pelvis with low pelvic incidence, manageable with in situ fusion, and an unbalanced pelvis with high pelvic incidence, which requires reduction to restore sagittal balance.
    probe: So what decides whether you reduce?
    probeA: Sacropelvic balance rather than the slip grade. Specifically, I reduce the unbalanced or retroverted pelvis, identified by a low sacral slope with a high pelvic tilt and a crouched, short-strided posture with knee flexion, because that patient has run out of compensation and in situ fusion would fix them in a kyphotic lumbosacral segment. I do not reduce the balanced pelvis with a high sacral slope and low pelvic tilt, where in situ fusion gives a good result without exposing the L5 root to the risk of reduction. A progressive slip in a skeletally immature patient and a significant neurological deficit are additional factors that push toward reduction.
  - q: What do you actually try to correct in a high-grade slip?
    a: The slip angle, not the Meyerding grade. I aim to bring the slip angle from say forty degrees to under ten, restoring lumbosacral alignment, and to reduce translation only partially — to about a grade I. Chasing a complete anatomical reduction is where patients get hurt.
    probe: Quantify the risk of full reduction.
    probeA: Twenty to thirty percent risk of L5 nerve root palsy with complete reduction of a high-grade slip. The mechanism is that the L5 root is draped over the posterosuperior corner of the sacrum and is already under tension in the slipped position, so pulling L5 back onto S1 stretches it further, and it is the lengthening rather than direct compression that injures it. That figure is why the accepted goal is partial reduction to about a grade I combined with slip angle correction, and it is also why intraoperative neuromonitoring focused on the L5 myotomes is mandatory during any reduction manoeuvre.
  - q: What is the risk of reducing a high-grade slip completely?
    a: Twenty to thirty percent risk of L5 nerve root palsy, because the L5 root is draped over the posterosuperior corner of the sacrum and is stretched during reduction. That figure is why partial reduction with slip angle correction is the safer goal.
    probe: How would you monitor for it intraoperatively?
    probeA: With continuous free-running and triggered electromyography of the L5 myotomes specifically, tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus, alongside motor evoked potentials and somatosensory evoked potentials. I would ask for the reduction to be performed incrementally with the monitoring team calling out changes, stop and reverse the reduction if I see neurotonic discharges, a fall in motor evoked potential amplitude, or a drop in triggered thresholds, and accept a lesser reduction rather than push through a signal change. I would also perform a generous decompression of the L5 roots and the lumbosacral tunnel before reducing, so the root has room, and I would document the monitoring trace at each stage.
  - q: Why circumferential fusion in high-grade slips?
    a: Because posterior-only fusion in grade III to V slips has pseudarthrosis rates of twenty to forty percent, against roughly five to ten percent with circumferential fusion. Adding an anterior interbody — through a separate anterior approach or an ALIF — addresses the anterior column under shear.
    probe: What does a TLIF give you that a posterolateral fusion does not?
    probeA: A TLIF places a structural graft and cage in the anterior and middle column, which is where the load is, so it restores disc height and segmental lordosis, provides direct anterior column support against shear, and gives a fusion bed in compression rather than in tension. A posterolateral fusion places graft in the intertransverse gutters, which is a less favourable biological and biomechanical environment and leaves the anterior column unsupported. That difference is exactly why pseudarthrosis rates fall from twenty to forty percent with posterior-only fusion in high-grade slips to roughly five to ten percent when the anterior column is addressed. The TLIF also achieves it through a single posterior approach, avoiding a separate anterior exposure with its vascular and retrograde ejaculation risks.
  - q: First-line management of an adolescent with an acute pars stress reaction?
    a: Conservative: activity modification, avoidance of hyperextension activities, core strengthening with physiotherapy, and a trial of a thoracolumbosacral orthosis for six to twelve weeks if the pain is acute and thought to represent an active pars stress reaction. Most settle without surgery.
    probe: How would you confirm the lesion is active rather than chronic?
    probeA: With MRI showing marrow oedema in the pars and pedicle on STIR or fat-suppressed T2, which indicates an active stress reaction that has the potential to heal, as opposed to a chronic established defect with sclerotic margins and no oedema. That distinction matters because it is the active lesion that justifies a period of bracing and activity restriction aimed at bony healing, whereas a chronic non-united defect will not heal with a brace and is treated symptomatically. Single photon emission computed tomography with CT is the alternative and is sensitive to active lesions, but I would prefer MRI in an adolescent to avoid the radiation. Plain films and CT define the anatomy of the defect but do not tell me whether it is active.
cases:
  - label: High-grade isthmic slip in a young adult
    stem: A 19-year-old gymnast has worsening low back pain radiating to both legs, with hamstring tightness and a crouched, short-strided gait. Standing lateral radiograph shows a Grade III isthmic spondylolisthesis at L5-S1 with a slip angle of 40 degrees and a pelvic incidence of 72 degrees. She has new weakness of ankle dorsiflexion.
    q1: Describe what the gait tells you and classify the problem.
    a1: The crouched, short-strided gait with hamstring tightness is the Phalen-Dickson gait, reflecting lumbosacral kyphosis and sagittal imbalance. This is a Wiltse isthmic spondylolisthesis, Meyerding grade III, with a high slip angle of forty degrees and a pelvic incidence of seventy-two degrees, which predisposes to high-grade slip because the vertical sacrum increases shear at L5-S1.
    p1: She has new dorsiflexion weakness. Does that change the urgency?
    pa1: Yes, it converts this from an elective deformity correction into a semi-urgent decompression. A new L5 motor deficit in a high-grade slip usually reflects compression of the L5 root in the lumbosacral tunnel, between the posteroinferior corner of the L5 body and the sacral ala, and a progressive motor deficit is a recognised indication for prompt surgery. So I would not place her on a routine list. I would document the power precisely with MRC grading, check for any bladder or bowel involvement and perianal sensation, obtain an MRI to confirm the site of compression, and plan surgery within days. It also strengthens the case for decompressing the L5 roots generously before any reduction manoeuvre.
    q2: Where does she sit on the Hresko classification, and what does that mean?
    a2: A high pelvic incidence of seventy-two with an unbalanced pelvis and a crouched compensatory posture places her in the unbalanced, high pelvic incidence subtype. Hresko's point is that this group requires reduction to restore sagittal balance, unlike the balanced low-incidence subtype which can be managed with in situ fusion.
    p2: How exactly do you measure sacral slope and pelvic tilt on her film?
    pa2: Both are measured from the sacral endplate and the femoral heads on a standing lateral radiograph that includes the femoral heads. Sacral slope is the angle between the superior endplate of S1 and a horizontal reference line. Pelvic tilt is the angle between a vertical reference line and a line drawn from the midpoint of the sacral endplate to the centre of the bicoxofemoral axis, taken as the midpoint between the two femoral head centres. Pelvic incidence is the angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and that same line to the femoral head axis, and the relationship is that pelvic incidence equals pelvic tilt plus sacral slope. In a high-grade slip the S1 endplate is often obscured, which is a practical source of measurement error, and using the sacral dome rather than the endplate is an accepted workaround.
    q3: How much do you reduce, and why not all the way?
    a3: I correct the slip angle, not the Meyerding grade — bringing forty degrees to under ten — and reduce translation only partially, to about a grade I. Complete reduction of a grade III or IV slip carries a twenty to thirty percent risk of L5 nerve root palsy, because the L5 root is draped over the posterosuperior corner of the sacrum and is stretched during reduction.
    p3: How do you decompress the L5 root before reducing?
    pa3: Generously and deliberately, because the root is the structure that fails. I would perform a wide bilateral decompression, removing the loose posterior element of L5, the Gill fragment, which is floating on the pars defect and is frequently the cause of the radiculopathy. Then I would follow each L5 root from the thecal sac out through its foramen, decompressing the lumbosacral tunnel by removing the fibrocartilaginous mass at the pars defect and any osteophyte from the posterosuperior sacrum, so the root is visibly free and mobile before I apply any reduction force. I would keep the roots under direct vision throughout the reduction with a nerve hook protecting them, and I would have the monitoring team calling the L5 myotomes continuously.
    q4: What construct, and why not posterior instrumentation alone?
    a4: Circumferential fusion: posterior instrumented fusion combined with an anterior interbody, either through a separate anterior approach or an ALIF. Posterior-only fusion in high-grade slips has pseudarthrosis rates of twenty to forty percent against roughly five to ten percent circumferentially, because the anterior column is under enormous shear. I would use intraoperative neuromonitoring throughout, watching the L5 roots specifically during any reduction.
    p4: A separate anterior approach in a 19-year-old woman. Any specific consent issues?
    pa4: Yes, and they need to be raised explicitly rather than buried in a general list. An anterior retroperitoneal approach to L5-S1 risks injury to the superior hypogastric plexus, which in a woman causes dyspareunia and altered sexual sensation rather than the retrograde ejaculation seen in men, and that must be discussed by name. Vascular injury to the iliac vessels and the middle sacral artery, with the left common iliac vein being the structure most at risk at L5-S1. Ureteric injury. Ileus and incisional hernia. And in a nineteen-year-old, the question of future pregnancy and delivery, where I would reassure her that a lumbosacral fusion is not a contraindication to pregnancy or to vaginal delivery, but that the abdominal approach and scarring should be known to her obstetric team. I would offer to have that conversation with a chaperone and to provide written information.

Let's master spondylolisthesis grading and treatment for the oral boards.

Spondylolisthesis is one of the most heavily tested spine topics because it demands precise classification, grading, and nuanced surgical decision-making across a wide range of patient ages and pathology types. The examiner expects you to rattle off classifications without hesitation, know exactly when to fuse, when not to fuse, and navigate the treacherous territory of high-grade slip reduction without injuring the L5 nerve root. Let's build this systematically.

Start with definitions. Spondylolisthesis is the forward translation of one vertebral body on the one below it. Spondylolysis is a defect in the pars interarticularis, the bony bridge between the superior and inferior articular processes. These are related but distinct entities. A patient can have spondylolysis without spondylolisthesis, and spondylolisthesis can occur without a pars defect, as in degenerative disease. You must keep these concepts separate because the examiner will test your understanding of the mechanism.

Now the Wiltse classification. This is the standard etiologic classification and you must know all five types cold.

Type I is dysplastic spondylolisthesis. This results from a congenital abnormality of the upper sacral facets or the arch of L5 that permits the slip to occur. The facets are hypoplastic or abnormally oriented, and the intact but attenuated posterior elements fail to prevent forward translation. This type is most common at L5-S1 and tends to present in childhood or adolescence. Because the neural arch is intact, progressive slippage causes significant narrowing of the spinal canal and can produce cauda equina symptoms even at moderate grades.

Type II is isthmic spondylolisthesis, the most common type overall and the most common cause of spondylolisthesis in patients under fifty. There are three subtypes. Type IIA is a fatigue fracture or stress fracture of the pars interarticularis. This is by far the most common subtype and is the classic lesion seen in young athletes, particularly gymnasts, football linemen, and divers. Repetitive hyperextension and axial loading stress the pars until it fractures. Type IIB is an elongated but intact pars. The pars undergoes repeated microfractures that heal with elongation rather than frank discontinuity, resulting in a stretched-out pars that allows forward slip. Type IIC is an acute pars fracture from a single traumatic event, which is relatively rare. The most common level for isthmic spondylolisthesis is L5-S1, occurring in roughly eighty-five to ninety percent of cases.

Type III is degenerative spondylolisthesis. This is the most common type in patients over sixty and is caused by facet joint arthrosis and ligamentous laxity leading to segmental instability without a pars defect. The posterior elements are intact. The most common level is L4-5, which is notable because L4-5 has a more sagittally oriented facet joint than L3-4, predisposing it to forward translation. Degenerative spondylolisthesis is six times more common in women than men and is associated with spinal stenosis because the intact posterior arch moves forward with the vertebral body, narrowing the canal.

Type IV is traumatic spondylolisthesis. This results from an acute fracture of a posterior element other than the pars, such as the pedicle or facet, causing acute instability and slip. This is distinct from Type IIC, which is specifically a pars fracture.

Type V is pathologic spondylolisthesis. This is caused by structural weakening of the posterior elements from tumor or infection, allowing the slip to occur. Metastatic disease and primary bone tumors are the most common causes.

You should also know the Marchetti-Bartolozzi classification, which divides spondylolisthesis into developmental and acquired categories. Developmental encompasses the dysplastic and isthmic types. Acquired includes degenerative, traumatic, pathologic, and postsurgical types. This classification is increasingly referenced for high-grade slips, but Wiltse remains the standard for board purposes.

Now the Meyerding grading system. This quantifies the degree of slip as a percentage of the anteroposterior diameter of the caudal vertebral body. Grade I is zero to twenty-five percent slip. Grade II is twenty-five to fifty percent. Grade III is fifty to seventy-five percent. Grade IV is seventy-five to one hundred percent. Grade V, also called spondyloptosis, is greater than one hundred percent slip, meaning the superior vertebral body has completely fallen off the front of the inferior one. Know these numbers exactly. The examiner will show you a lateral radiograph and expect you to grade it immediately.

For high-grade slips, meaning Grade III and above, you must understand pelvic incidence and slip angle. Pelvic incidence is the angle between the line perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and the line connecting that midpoint to the center of the femoral heads. It is a fixed anatomic parameter that does not change with posture. A high pelvic incidence, above sixty degrees, predisposes to high-grade spondylolisthesis because the sacrum is more vertical, placing greater shear force across L5-S1. The slip angle, measured between a line along the posterior aspect of S1 and a line along the inferior endplate of L5, reflects the degree of lumbosacral kyphosis. A high slip angle indicates sagittal imbalance and is a strong predictor of slip progression. In the Hresko classification for high-grade slips, a high pelvic incidence with a high slip angle defines a "balanced pelvis with unbalanced spine" phenotype that demands surgical correction of alignment, whereas a low slip angle with compensated sagittal balance may be managed with in situ fusion.

Natural history varies by type. Isthmic spondylolisthesis is most commonly Grade I or II and has a favorable natural history with conservative management. Progression is uncommon in adults. In children and adolescents, progression risk is highest during the growth spurt, particularly in females with high-grade slips, high pelvic incidence, and a dome-shaped sacral endplate. Degenerative spondylolisthesis tends to stabilize over time as osteophytes form, though the associated stenosis typically worsens.

Now let's move to the clinical cases.

Case A. A fifty-five-year-old woman presents with two years of progressive low back pain and bilateral lower extremity pain in an L5 distribution. She has neurogenic claudication limiting her walking distance to one block. Exam shows bilateral EHL weakness graded four out of five. MRI demonstrates Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with severe central and lateral recess stenosis. Standing lateral radiographs confirm the slip at nine millimeters. Flexion-extension films show five millimeters of dynamic translation.

Case B. A sixteen-year-old male competitive gymnast presents with six months of worsening low back pain radiating to both legs. He has bilateral hamstring tightness, a Phalen-Dickson gait with a crouched stance and short stride length, and bilateral L5 radiculopathy with EHL and tibialis anterior weakness. Standing lateral radiograph shows Grade III isthmic spondylolisthesis at L5-S1 with a slip angle of forty degrees and a pelvic incidence of seventy-two degrees. CT confirms bilateral pars defects. MRI shows severe bilateral L5 foraminal stenosis without cauda equina compression.

These two cases represent the bread and butter of the board examination. Case A is the classic degenerative spondylolisthesis with stenosis scenario. Case B is the high-grade isthmic slip in an adolescent that tests your understanding of sagittal balance and reduction.

Now the examiner interrogation.

Examiner: "Walk me through your imaging workup for suspected spondylolisthesis."

Your answer: I start with standing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the lumbar spine. Standing films are essential because supine films can underestimate or miss a dynamic slip entirely. On the lateral film, I measure the Meyerding grade and the slip angle. I assess sacral morphology, looking for a dome-shaped or trapezoidal L5 body and sacral rounding, which are associated with high-grade dysplastic and isthmic slips. I calculate pelvic incidence from standing full-length lateral films or dedicated pelvic radiographs. I obtain flexion-extension lateral radiographs to assess dynamic instability. Greater than three millimeters of translation or greater than ten degrees of angular change between flexion and extension defines instability. I order a CT scan to evaluate the pars interarticularis directly, as CT is the gold standard for identifying pars defects, which may be missed on MRI. I obtain an MRI to evaluate neural compression, disc degeneration, and the status of adjacent levels. For high-grade slips, I may also obtain a CT myelogram if MRI artifact from hardware or body habitus limits evaluation.

Examiner: "Let's discuss Case A. How would you manage this patient?"

Your answer: This patient has symptomatic degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis, radiculopathy, and documented instability on flexion-extension films. She has failed conservative management given her two-year history. The surgical treatment is decompression and instrumented fusion. The landmark evidence here is the SLIP trial, published by Ghogawala and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016. This was a randomized controlled trial comparing decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion for Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 with stenosis. The primary outcome, the SF-36 physical component score at two years, favored fusion, and the advantage persisted at four years. This trial provides level one evidence that fusion should be added to decompression for degenerative spondylolisthesis. The earlier SPORT trial for degenerative spondylolisthesis also showed that surgical treatment, predominantly decompression with fusion, was superior to nonoperative management at four years.

For my surgical approach, I perform a posterior decompression with bilateral laminectomy and foraminotomies, followed by instrumented posterolateral fusion with pedicle screws and rods. I add an interbody fusion, typically a transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion or TLIF, for several reasons: it provides anterior column support, restores disc height and foraminal height, increases fusion surface area, and allows for some indirect decompression. The TLIF approach through one side gives me a three-hundred-sixty-degree fusion through a single posterior incision. I would use a structural interbody cage packed with local autograft supplemented with bone morphogenetic protein or allograft.

Examiner: "Would you decompress without fusion for this patient?"

Your answer: No. This is a direct application of the SLIP data. The trial specifically addressed this question and showed that fusion improves outcomes. Furthermore, this patient has documented dynamic instability with five millimeters of translation on flexion-extension films, which is an independent indication for fusion. Decompression alone in the setting of instability risks progressive slip and recurrent stenosis. The historical argument for decompression alone was to avoid fusion-related morbidity and adjacent segment disease, but SLIP settled this debate in favor of fusion for degenerative spondylolisthesis with stenosis.

Examiner: "Now let's discuss Case B. This is a sixteen-year-old with a Grade III isthmic slip at L5-S1. What is your management?"

Your answer: This patient requires surgical treatment. He has a high-grade isthmic spondylolisthesis with progressive neurologic deficit, sagittal imbalance evidenced by the Phalen-Dickson gait, and a high slip angle of forty degrees indicating lumbosacral kyphosis. Conservative management is not appropriate here. In a growing adolescent, a Grade III or higher slip with a high slip angle and high pelvic incidence has a significant risk of progression, and this patient already has bilateral radiculopathy.

The central surgical question is whether to perform an in situ fusion or attempt reduction. This is the classic board debate.

In situ fusion means fusing L5 to S1 in the position the slip is found, without attempting to correct it. The argument for in situ fusion is safety: it avoids the risk of L5 nerve root palsy that comes with reduction. Historically, in situ posterolateral fusion was the standard for high-grade slips and has good long-term results in many series.

Reduction means attempting to translate L5 posteriorly back toward a more anatomic position before fusing. The argument for reduction is restoration of sagittal balance. A patient with a forty-degree slip angle has significant lumbosacral kyphosis. If I fuse this in situ, I lock in that deformity, and the patient must compensate with hyperextension of the lumbar spine above and retroversion of the pelvis below. Over a lifetime, this compensation fails, and the patient develops adjacent segment disease and progressive imbalance.

My approach for this patient is partial reduction. I do not attempt to fully reduce a Grade III or IV slip to a Grade 0. Complete reduction of a high-grade slip carries a twenty to thirty percent risk of L5 nerve root palsy because the L5 root is draped over the posterosuperior corner of the sacrum and is stretched during reduction. Instead, I aim to reduce the slip angle to restore sagittal alignment, bringing it from forty degrees to under ten degrees, and to reduce the translation partially to approximately a Grade I. Partial reduction gives me the alignment benefit while minimizing the neurologic risk.

For surgical technique, I perform a posterior approach with wide bilateral L5 laminectomy and foraminotomies to completely free the L5 nerve roots before any reduction maneuver. Nerve root decompression is mandatory before reduction. I place L4, L5, and S1 pedicle screws, and often extend to iliac or S2 alar-iliac screws for distal fixation because the sacral purchase at S1 is poor in high-grade slips due to sacral dysplasia. I perform a posterior interbody fusion using a TLIF or PLIF approach with structural interbody support. I then perform a gradual cantilever reduction using the pedicle screw-rod construct, reducing slip angle first, then translation, under continuous neuromonitoring with both somatosensory evoked potentials and free-running and triggered electromyography of the L5 myotome. If I see any EMG changes during reduction, I stop immediately and accept the position I have achieved.

For the highest-grade slips, Grade IV and spondyloptosis, some surgeons advocate a Bohlman technique or transsacral fibular strut grafting, where a fibular strut autograft is placed from the posterior sacrum through the S1 body and into the L5 body to provide a direct anterior column buttress. This is a powerful technique for spondyloptosis. Circumferential fusion combining a posterior instrumented fusion with an anterior interbody fusion, either through a separate anterior approach or an ALIF, is often recommended for Grade III through V slips because the pseudarthrosis rate with posterior-only fusion in high-grade slips can reach twenty to forty percent.

Examiner, and this is a trap question: "Would you reduce a Grade IV slip completely?"

Your answer: No. Complete reduction of a Grade IV slip is unacceptably dangerous. The L5 nerve root palsy rate with complete reduction of high-grade slips is twenty to thirty percent. My goal is partial reduction to restore sagittal alignment, specifically reducing the slip angle, and achieving a Grade I or at most Grade II translation. If I can correct the lumbosacral kyphosis, that is more important functionally than reducing the percentage of translation. The slip angle, not the Meyerding grade, is the primary target of correction.

Examiner, trap question: "A twenty-year-old college athlete comes to you with back pain. CT shows bilateral pars defects at L5. There is a Grade I slip on standing films. Would you fuse this patient?"

Your answer: Not necessarily. This is a young, active patient with a low-grade slip. My first-line treatment is conservative: activity modification, avoidance of hyperextension activities, core strengthening with physical therapy, and a trial of a thoracolumbosacral orthosis for six to twelve weeks if the pain is acute and thought to represent an active pars stress reaction. Many patients with low-grade isthmic spondylolisthesis become asymptomatic with conservative management. If the patient fails at least six months of conservative care and the pars defect is the pain generator, I would consider a direct pars repair rather than a fusion in a young patient with an intact L5-S1 disc. Direct pars repair techniques include the Buck screw technique, where a lag screw is placed across the pars defect, and the Scott wiring technique, where a wire is looped under the spinous process and around the transverse process to compress the pars defect with bone graft. A pedicle screw and hook construct with pars grafting is another modern option. The key criterion for direct pars repair is an intact, well-hydrated L5-S1 disc on MRI. If the disc is degenerated, pars repair will fail and a fusion is necessary.

Examiner: "What about instrumentation choices for interbody fusion in spondylolisthesis? TLIF versus PLIF versus ALIF?"

Your answer: TLIF, or transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, is performed through a unilateral posterolateral approach, removing one facet to access the disc space. It allows interbody fusion and posterior instrumentation through a single incision, with lower risk to the thecal sac and nerve roots compared to PLIF because the dura is retracted less. PLIF, or posterior lumbar interbody fusion, accesses the disc space through bilateral laminotomies and requires more dural retraction, with a higher reported rate of dural tears and nerve root injuries, but allows placement of two symmetrical cages for better endplate coverage. ALIF, or anterior lumbar interbody fusion, uses an anterior retroperitoneal approach and is particularly useful at L5-S1 where the iliac bifurcation is usually above the disc space. ALIF provides the largest cage footprint and direct disc space distraction without neural manipulation. At L5-S1, an ALIF combined with posterior pedicle screw fixation is an excellent construct for high-grade slips.

Examiner: "What are the major complications you counsel this family about for the high-grade slip case?"

Your answer: The most feared complication is L5 nerve root palsy, manifesting as foot drop with tibialis anterior and EHL weakness. The risk ranges from five percent with careful partial reduction to twenty to thirty percent with attempted complete reduction. Most L5 palsies are neurapraxic and recover over three to twelve months, but some are permanent. Pseudarthrosis is a significant risk in high-grade slips, with posterior-only fusion rates as high as twenty to forty percent compared to five to ten percent with circumferential fusion. Hardware failure, including sacral screw pullout and rod fracture, is more common due to high biomechanical demands at the lumbosacral junction, which is why I use S2 alar-iliac screws. Loss of correction can occur if fusion does not consolidate. Cauda equina syndrome is a rare but devastating complication from overdistraction, hematoma, or malpositioned instrumentation.

Now let's review the evidence.

The SLIP trial, published by Ghogawala and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016, is the definitive study for degenerative spondylolisthesis. It randomized patients with Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4-5 and stenosis to decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion. The primary outcome was the SF-36 physical component score at two years, which favored fusion, with better physical function and fewer reoperations still evident at four years. This is level one evidence that fusion should be added to decompression for symptomatic degenerative spondylolisthesis with stenosis.

The SPORT trial included a spondylolisthesis arm comparing operative versus nonoperative treatment for degenerative spondylolisthesis. The as-treated analysis showed significant advantages for surgery over nonoperative care in pain, function, and satisfaction at four years. Though limited by high crossover rates, the combined analysis provides strong evidence favoring surgery for symptomatic degenerative spondylolisthesis.

The Hresko classification, proposed in 2007 and refined by the Spinal Deformity Study Group, classifies high-grade spondylolisthesis based on pelvic incidence and sacropelvic balance. It identifies two subtypes: low-grade dysplasia with a balanced pelvis and low pelvic incidence, which can be managed with in situ fusion, and high-grade dysplasia with an unbalanced pelvis and high pelvic incidence, which requires reduction to restore sagittal balance. This classification guides surgical strategy for Grade III through V slips and is increasingly referenced in the current literature.

The Labelle and SOSORT guidelines address pediatric and adolescent spondylolisthesis specifically. These guidelines recommend surgical treatment for high-grade slips, particularly those with documented progression, neurologic deficit, or slip angles greater than thirty degrees. They emphasize serial radiographic monitoring during the growth spurt and define risk factors for progression including female sex, younger age at diagnosis, dome-shaped sacral endplate, high pelvic incidence, and high slip angle.

Now for the board pearls.

The Meyerding grading numbers are nonnegotiable. Grade I is zero to twenty-five percent, Grade II is twenty-five to fifty, Grade III is fifty to seventy-five, Grade IV is seventy-five to one hundred, Grade V is spondyloptosis beyond one hundred percent. If you hesitate on these, you lose the examiner's confidence immediately.

The most common level for degenerative spondylolisthesis is L4-5. The most common level for isthmic spondylolisthesis is L5-S1. If the examiner gives you degenerative at L4-5, think decompression plus fusion and cite SLIP. If the examiner gives you a young patient with pars defects and an intact disc, think conservative management first, then direct pars repair.

The slip angle is more important than the Meyerding grade for surgical planning in high-grade slips. Correcting the slip angle restores sagittal balance. Correcting translation is secondary.

Never attempt complete reduction of a Grade IV or V slip. Partial reduction to restore slip angle with acceptance of residual Grade I or II translation is the safe and effective strategy.

Always free the L5 nerve roots completely before any reduction maneuver. Use neuromonitoring throughout the reduction. If EMG fires, stop reducing.

For high-grade slips, always discuss circumferential fusion. Posterior-only fusion has an unacceptable pseudarthrosis rate. Use S2 alar-iliac screws, not S1 alone, for distal fixation.

The three things that will fail you on this topic. One: not knowing the Wiltse classification subtypes, especially the three subtypes of Type II isthmic. Two: recommending complete reduction of a high-grade slip or failing to discuss the L5 nerve root palsy risk. Three: not citing SLIP when asked about degenerative spondylolisthesis management.
