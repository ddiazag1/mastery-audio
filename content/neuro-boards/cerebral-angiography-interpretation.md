Let's master cerebral angiography interpretation for the oral boards.

The angiogram is the study candidates fear most, because unlike a head CT it cannot be read by pattern alone. You must know the anatomy cold, you must know which vessel you are injecting, and you must be able to follow a vessel from its origin to its territory while talking. The good news is that angiographic anatomy is finite and highly testable, and once you own it you can answer almost any vascular question the examiner constructs.

Start with orientation, because this is where candidates lose points immediately. Before describing anything, state three things: which vessel has been injected, which projection you are looking at, and which phase of the study this is. For example: this is a left internal carotid artery injection in the lateral projection during the arterial phase. If you are unsure which vessel, reason it out loud. An injection filling the anterior and middle cerebral arteries is an internal carotid injection. An injection filling the vertebrobasilar system and the posterior cerebral arteries is a vertebral injection. An injection filling the middle meningeal artery and the branches of the external carotid supplying the face and scalp is an external carotid injection. In the lateral projection, the anterior cerebral artery sweeps up and over the corpus callosum in a characteristic arc and the middle cerebral branches spray posteriorly in the Sylvian region. In the anteroposterior projection, the anterior cerebral arteries run vertically in the midline and the middle cerebral arteries run horizontally and laterally, forming a shape often likened to a candelabra.

Know the phases, because pathology is defined by them. The arterial phase, roughly the first two seconds, fills the arteries. The capillary or parenchymal phase produces a diffuse blush of the brain tissue. The venous phase fills the cortical veins, the deep venous system, and the dural sinuses. A normal transit time from arterial to venous filling is roughly four to six seconds. Shortened transit with early venous filling during the arterial phase is the single most important abnormal finding in the entire study, because it means arteriovenous shunting. If you see a vein filling while arteries are still filling, say the words early venous drainage indicating arteriovenous shunting, and you have just made the diagnosis of an arteriovenous malformation or a dural arteriovenous fistula.

Now walk the anterior circulation. The internal carotid artery is conventionally divided into segments: cervical, petrous, lacerum, cavernous, clinoid, ophthalmic, and communicating, in the Bouthillier classification, and you should be able to name at least the cavernous, ophthalmic, and communicating segments and their branches. The ophthalmic artery arises just after the carotid exits the cavernous sinus and is the first major intradural branch, an important landmark for determining whether an aneurysm is intradural and therefore at risk of causing subarachnoid hemorrhage. Then the posterior communicating artery, then the anterior choroidal artery, then the terminal bifurcation into the anterior and middle cerebral arteries. The anterior choroidal artery is small but clinically enormous, supplying the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the optic tract, and parts of the thalamus and medial temporal lobe, and its sacrifice produces the classic triad of contralateral hemiplegia, hemianesthesia, and homonymous hemianopia. Never sacrifice it, and say that when clipping a posterior communicating aneurysm.

The middle cerebral artery divides into segments M1 through M4. The M1 segment gives off the lenticulostriate perforators, which supply the basal ganglia and internal capsule and are end arteries with no collateral supply. The bifurcation or trifurcation occurs at the genu, and the M2 segments run in the Sylvian fissure. The examination point is that lenticulostriate compromise during M1 surgery produces a devastating capsular stroke, so you discuss preserving perforators explicitly.

The anterior cerebral artery runs A1 from the carotid to the anterior communicating artery, then A2 onward. The recurrent artery of Heubner arises near the anterior communicating complex, usually from the proximal A2, and supplies the head of the caudate and the anterior limb of the internal capsule. It is the other perforator you must name and protect, particularly in anterior communicating artery aneurysm surgery. Anterior cerebral territory infarction produces contralateral leg predominant weakness, and bilateral involvement produces abulia and akinetic mutism.

Now the posterior circulation. The vertebral arteries arise from the subclavian arteries, ascend through the transverse foramina, and join to form the basilar artery. The posterior inferior cerebellar artery is the major branch of the intradural vertebral artery and supplies the lateral medulla and the inferior cerebellum. Occlusion produces the lateral medullary or Wallenberg syndrome, with ipsilateral facial sensory loss, Horner syndrome, ataxia, dysphagia and hoarseness from nucleus ambiguus involvement, and contralateral body sensory loss. The anterior inferior cerebellar artery arises from the lower basilar and supplies the lateral pons and the region of the internal auditory canal, and it classically gives rise to the labyrinthine artery, so occlusion can produce deafness and vertigo. The superior cerebellar artery arises just below the basilar tip. The basilar terminates in the posterior cerebral arteries. Remember that the oculomotor nerve runs between the superior cerebellar and posterior cerebral arteries, which is why a posterior communicating artery aneurysm and a basilar tip aneurysm both produce third nerve palsies.

Know the collateral pathways, because the examiner will ask what happens if you occlude a vessel. The circle of Willis is the primary collateral network, and it is complete in only a minority of people, perhaps twenty to twenty five percent. The anterior communicating artery connects the two anterior circulations, and the posterior communicating arteries connect anterior to posterior. Secondary collaterals include leptomeningeal or pial collaterals over the convexity, and the ophthalmic artery, which can reverse flow and carry blood from the external carotid through the orbit into the internal carotid. When discussing carotid sacrifice, describe a balloon test occlusion with clinical monitoring and ideally perfusion imaging, and state that even a passed test does not eliminate delayed ischemic risk, which is why a bypass may still be indicated.

Now pathology. For aneurysms, describe location, size, neck morphology, dome projection, and the relationship to branch vessels. Say whether the neck is narrow or broad, because that determines clippability versus coilability, and state the dome to neck ratio concept: a ratio of roughly two or greater favors coiling. Look for a daughter sac or bleb, which indicates the likely rupture point. Always ask for three dimensional rotational angiography if you cannot see the neck. And always look at the entire study for additional aneurysms, because multiple aneurysms occur in roughly twenty to thirty percent of patients, and failing to mention that you would survey for others is a common and avoidable error.

For arteriovenous malformations, you must be able to characterize the three components: the arterial feeders, the nidus, and the draining veins. Then apply the Spetzler Martin grade, assigning one, two, or three points for size less than three centimeters, three to six centimeters, or greater than six centimeters, one point for eloquent location, and one point for deep venous drainage. State the grade and then the implication for surgical risk. Look specifically for associated aneurysms, which occur on feeding arteries or within the nidus and carry elevated hemorrhage risk, and for venous outflow stenosis, which also raises hemorrhage risk.

For dural arteriovenous fistulas, the essential point is that the shunt is in the dura, fed by dural arteries such as the middle meningeal or occipital arteries, and that the risk stratification depends entirely on the venous drainage pattern. Cortical venous reflux, meaning retrograde drainage into cortical veins, converts a benign lesion into an aggressive one with substantial annual hemorrhage risk. The Borden and Cognard classifications both encode this. If you see cortical venous reflux, recommend treatment.

For vasospasm, recognize the smooth, tapered, often diffuse narrowing of the proximal vessels in the days after subarachnoid hemorrhage, and distinguish it from atherosclerotic irregularity, which is focal and eccentric, and from vasculitis, which produces a beaded appearance with alternating stenoses and dilatations.

For dissection, look for the string sign of a long smooth tapered stenosis, the flame shaped occlusion, a double lumen, or a dissecting pseudoaneurysm. For fibromuscular dysplasia, look for the string of beads appearance in the mid to distal cervical internal carotid, which differs from vasculitis in location and from atherosclerosis in sparing the bifurcation.

Finally, know the risks of the procedure and be ready to consent the patient out loud, because the examiner may ask. Catheter angiography carries roughly a one percent risk of neurologic complication overall, with permanent deficit in the range of a few tenths of a percent in experienced hands. Other risks include groin hematoma, retroperitoneal hemorrhage, femoral pseudoaneurysm, contrast nephropathy, contrast allergy, and radiation. Know that computed tomographic angiography is faster and more available with excellent sensitivity for aneurysms greater than roughly three millimeters, but that catheter angiography remains the gold standard for small aneurysms, for characterizing arteriovenous shunting lesions, and when the noninvasive study is negative in a patient with a convincing subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Know how the study is actually performed, because the examiner may ask you to consent the patient or to troubleshoot a complication. Access is usually transfemoral, increasingly transradial, with a sheath placed by Seldinger technique. A catheter is advanced over a wire into the aortic arch and then selectively into the great vessels, and it is good practice to name the arch type, since a bovine arch or a type three arch makes catheterisation substantially harder. Systemic heparinisation and continuous pressurised flush prevent thromboembolism. Images are acquired by digital subtraction, in which a mask image is subtracted from the contrast filled run so that bone and soft tissue disappear and only the vessels remain; this is why patient movement ruins a run and produces misregistration artefact.

Build the habit of a complete study rather than a targeted one. A full diagnostic angiogram for subarachnoid haemorrhage interrogates both internal carotid arteries and both vertebral arteries, because roughly twenty to thirty percent of patients harbour multiple aneurysms and because the culprit may not be where the blood looks heaviest. Include the external carotid circulation when a dural fistula or a hypervascular tumour is suspected. If the initial study is negative in a patient with a convincing subarachnoid pattern, state that you would repeat the angiogram in roughly one to two weeks, because a proportion of initially negative studies become positive once vasospasm or clot has resolved; the exception is a true perimesencephalic pattern, which many centres will not re-image.

Have the dynamic manoeuvres ready, since they demonstrate real angiographic fluency. Cross compression, in which the contralateral carotid is compressed during an injection, opacifies the anterior communicating artery and shows whether collateral flow crosses the midline. The Allcock test compresses the carotid during a vertebral injection to fill the posterior communicating artery and demonstrate posterior to anterior collateral supply. A balloon test occlusion assesses tolerance of carotid sacrifice, with clinical monitoring during temporary occlusion, often augmented by a hypotensive challenge and by perfusion imaging; passing the test reduces but does not eliminate the risk of delayed ischaemia, which is why a bypass may still be offered to a young patient facing permanent occlusion.

Do not neglect the venous phase, because candidates routinely stop looking once the arteries empty. Identify the superior sagittal sinus, the straight sinus, the transverse and sigmoid sinuses and the jugular bulbs, and the deep system comprising the internal cerebral veins, the vein of Rosenthal and the vein of Galen draining into the straight sinus. Note the dominant transverse sinus, which matters enormously if you are planning a retrosigmoid or a supracerebellar approach, and note the vein of Labbé draining the temporal lobe into the transverse sinus, whose sacrifice risks venous infarction and aphasia in the dominant hemisphere. Delayed venous drainage suggests raised intracranial pressure or venous outflow obstruction; a filling defect suggests thrombosis.

For aneurysm work, add the quantitative habits. State the maximal dome diameter, the neck width, the dome to neck ratio and the aspect ratio, which is the dome height divided by the neck width; a dome to neck ratio of roughly two or greater and an aspect ratio above about one point six favour coiling and predict stability. Identify branch vessels arising from the neck or the dome, because those determine whether the neck can be clipped or coiled without occluding a perforator. Look for a daughter sac, which marks the likely rupture point. Ask for three dimensional rotational angiography and working projections if the neck is obscured, and say so rather than guessing. Know that computed tomographic angiography has excellent sensitivity for aneurysms above roughly three millimetres and is often sufficient to take a patient to theatre, but that catheter angiography remains the reference standard for small aneurysms, for blister aneurysms, and for characterising shunting lesions.

Be aware of how the endovascular era has changed the answers. Flow diverting stents redirect flow away from the aneurysm and promote progressive thrombosis and endothelialisation, are particularly suited to large, wide necked and fusiform lesions of the internal carotid artery, and require dual antiplatelet therapy, which makes them problematic in the acutely ruptured patient. Intrasaccular flow disruptors address wide necked bifurcation aneurysms without committing to dual antiplatelets. Balloon and stent assisted coiling expand what can be treated endovascularly. None of this abolishes the role of surgery, and you should be able to say when you would still clip: a young patient with a favourable middle cerebral artery aneurysm, a lesion with a broad neck incorporating branches, a patient with a large clot requiring evacuation, and failure or recurrence after endovascular treatment.

Finally, own the complications. The overall neurological complication rate is roughly one percent, with permanent deficit in the range of a few tenths of a percent in experienced hands; the mechanisms are thromboembolism, dissection, and air embolism. Local access complications include groin haematoma, retroperitoneal haemorrhage, which should be suspected in a hypotensive patient after a high puncture, femoral pseudoaneurysm, and arteriovenous fistula. Contrast related complications include allergy and contrast induced nephropathy, mitigated by hydration and by limiting contrast load. Radiation matters: fluoroscopy time and dose area product should be minimised, particularly in young patients and in repeat studies, and deterministic skin injury is a real risk in prolonged interventions. And contrast induced encephalopathy is a rare but memorable mimic of stroke after a long procedure, presenting with cortical blindness or hemiparesis with cortical contrast staining on computed tomography, and it resolves with supportive care.

To summarize your approach. Orient yourself out loud with vessel, projection, and phase. Confirm the injected vessel by what fills. Walk the vessel from origin to territory and name the perforators, especially the anterior choroidal and the recurrent artery of Heubner. Watch the transit time and call early venous drainage the moment you see it. Characterize aneurysms by neck, dome, and branch relationships, and always survey for additional ones. Grade arteriovenous malformations formally. Judge dural fistulas by venous drainage. Distinguish spasm from atherosclerosis from vasculitis by the pattern of narrowing. Do that reliably and the angiogram becomes an asset rather than the part of the case you dread.
