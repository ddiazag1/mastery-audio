Welcome back. This session covers intradural spinal cord tumors, a topic the oral board examiners love because it tests anatomy, imaging interpretation, surgical judgment, and intraoperative decision-making all in one scenario. You need to be precise here. Let us build from the anatomy up, then walk through a clinical case, and then I will interrogate you the way the examiner will.

Let us start with the foundation. The intradural compartment sits inside the dura mater but outside the bony spinal column, and it contains two fundamentally different tumor locations. First, intramedullary tumors arise within the substance of the spinal cord itself. Second, intradural extramedullary tumors sit outside the cord but inside the dura, typically arising from nerve roots or the meninges. This distinction is everything. It determines your differential, your surgical plan, and your expected outcome. Get the compartment right, and the rest follows logically.

Within the intramedullary category, three tumors dominate. Ependymoma accounts for roughly sixty percent of adult intramedullary tumors. Astrocytoma accounts for approximately thirty percent. Hemangioblastoma makes up five to ten percent. You need to know each one cold.

Ependymoma arises from the ependymal cells lining the central canal. Because of that central origin, it grows symmetrically within the cord, expanding outward and compressing normal tissue into a peripheral rim. This is why ependymomas are well-circumscribed and, critically, why a cleavage plane exists between tumor and normal cord. On MRI, ependymoma is T2-hyperintense and enhances homogeneously with gadolinium. The classic finding is the hemosiderin cap sign, areas of T2-hypointense signal at the superior and inferior poles of the tumor representing prior microhemorrhages. Associated syringomyelia is common, extending rostral and caudal to the tumor. Because of the cleavage plane, gross total resection is achievable in the majority of cases, and when you get a gross total resection, long-term recurrence-free survival exceeds ninety percent at ten years based on the Klekamp series.

Astrocytoma is the opposite beast. It is infiltrative, arising from the glial cells of the cord parenchyma. It grows eccentrically, without a capsule, without a clear plane between tumor and normal cord. On MRI, astrocytoma is T2-hyperintense but enhances heterogeneously or sometimes poorly. The margins are ill-defined. There is no hemosiderin cap. The critical point is that gross total resection is usually not possible without causing devastating neurological deficit, because there is no plane to follow. The one exception is pilocytic astrocytoma, which can have a more discrete margin and may be amenable to gross total resection. But for diffuse fibrillary astrocytoma, the standard is biopsy for tissue diagnosis and debulking only to the extent that you can safely decompress the cord.

Hemangioblastoma is the third intramedullary tumor. It presents as an intensely enhancing nodule, often small, with a disproportionately large associated cyst or syrinx. The key association is Von Hippel-Lindau disease. Approximately thirty-three percent of spinal hemangioblastomas occur in the setting of VHL, and when VHL is present, tumors are frequently multiple. The surgical rule for hemangioblastoma is absolute: you must resect it en bloc. You circumferentially coagulate the surface feeding vessels first, then mobilize and remove the nodule as a single piece. Piecemeal resection leads to catastrophic, uncontrollable hemorrhage because these tumors are densely vascular. We will come back to this in the trap questions.

Now for the intradural extramedullary compartment. Two tumors dominate here in roughly equal proportion, each about forty percent. Schwannoma arises from the nerve root sheath. On MRI it is well-circumscribed, enhances avidly, and classically shows a dumbbell shape when it extends through the neural foramen into the extraforaminal space. On CT, you may see foraminal widening from chronic erosion. Schwannomas can be purely intradural, purely extradural, or both. They are associated with NF2, and when NF2 is present, bilateral vestibular schwannomas and multiple spinal schwannomas are expected. Neurofibromas, by contrast, are associated with NF1.

Meningioma arises from arachnoid cap cells in the dura. There is a strong predilection for middle-aged women and for the thoracic spine. On MRI, meningiomas are isointense to cord on T1, enhance homogeneously, and may show calcification and a dural tail. They are dural-based and displace the spinal cord rather than invading it. Most are lateral or posterolateral, but ventral thoracic meningiomas present a particular surgical challenge because of the difficulty of accessing the ventral dura without excessive cord manipulation. NF2 patients may have multiple meningiomas.

With that foundation in place, let us move to a clinical case.

A forty-two-year-old man presents with progressive gait difficulty over six months. He describes his legs as stiff and clumsy. He also reports worsening hand clumsiness, dropping objects, and difficulty with fine motor tasks like buttoning his shirt. On further questioning, he describes a cape-like distribution of sensory loss across the upper extremities and upper trunk, a suspended sensory level. He can feel pinprick on his face and in his lower extremities, but the shoulders, arms, and upper chest are diminished. On examination, he has a spastic gait, hyperreflexia in the lower extremities, bilateral Babinski signs, and intrinsic hand muscle wasting. MRI of the cervical spine shows an intramedullary lesion at C4 through C6 that is T2-hyperintense, enhances homogeneously with gadolinium, has associated syrinx extending rostrally and caudally, and demonstrates hemosiderin caps at the superior and inferior tumor poles.

Pause here and think about this case before we proceed. The cape-like sensory loss is the classic central cord syndrome pattern where the crossing spinothalamic fibers in the anterior white commissure are disrupted by an expanding central lesion. These fibers cross at each segmental level, and a central expanding mass at C4 through C6 destroys them as they decussate, producing bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensation in a shawl or cape distribution corresponding to those dermatomes while preserving sensation above and below. The hemosiderin caps, central location, homogeneous enhancement, and associated syrinx all point strongly toward one diagnosis. The progressive myelopathy with upper and lower motor neuron findings in the hands combined with upper motor neuron findings in the legs tells you this is a cervical cord lesion causing a combined central cord and long tract syndrome.

Now let us move into examiner interrogation. This is where the oral board lives.

The examiner asks, what is your differential? Your answer should be organized and confident. The most likely diagnosis is ependymoma, based on the hemosiderin cap sign, the central location within the cord, the homogeneous enhancement pattern, and the associated syrinx. Your differential also includes astrocytoma and hemangioblastoma, but astrocytoma typically enhances heterogeneously, has ill-defined margins, and does not produce hemosiderin caps. Hemangioblastoma would show an intensely enhancing nodule rather than a long-segment homogeneously enhancing mass, though you must rule out VHL in any intramedullary enhancing lesion.

The examiner then pushes on workup. What do you order? You need a full-spine MRI with gadolinium to evaluate for drop metastases, because ependymomas, particularly myxopapillary ependymomas of the filum, can seed the CSF. You need a brain MRI, which serves multiple purposes: it screens for intracranial hemangioblastoma if you suspect VHL, it evaluates for NF2-associated lesions, and it rules out intracranial pathology that might present with myelopathic symptoms. If VHL is a consideration, you pursue ophthalmologic evaluation for retinal hemangioblastomas and genetic testing. Obtain a detailed family history for VHL and neurofibromatosis.

The examiner now asks about your surgical approach. For an intramedullary cervical lesion, you perform a posterior midline approach. The patient is positioned prone with the head in a Mayfield clamp for cervical lesions. You perform a midline exposure of the posterior elements from C3 through C7, giving yourself one level above and one level below the tumor. For a three-level lesion like this, you should strongly consider laminoplasty rather than laminectomy to preserve posterior tension band and reduce the risk of post-laminectomy kyphosis, particularly in the cervical spine. After opening the lamina, you open the dura in the midline under the microscope. You then identify the posterior median sulcus and perform a posterior median sulcus myelotomy, using the natural midline raphe to enter the cord with minimal disruption to functional tracts. For eccentric tumors, the dorsal root entry zone can serve as an alternative entry point. Once inside the cord, you develop the plane between tumor and compressed normal cord, working from inside out. Use pial stay sutures to keep the myelotomy open and provide gentle retraction of the cord edges. The ultrasonic aspirator is invaluable for internal debulking of ependymoma, reducing tumor volume so you can see and develop the peripheral plane without excessive cord manipulation. Keep the surgical field meticulously hemostatic using low-power bipolar cautery and gentle irrigation. For ependymoma, the plane is usually gliotic and slightly discolored compared to the surrounding white cord tissue. Follow that color change. When the tumor separates cleanly, you are in the right plane.

This brings us to intraoperative neuromonitoring, and you must demonstrate command of this topic. Three modalities are essential: somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials, and D-wave monitoring. SSEPs monitor the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway and detect injury to posterior cord structures. MEPs, elicited by transcranial electrical stimulation, monitor the corticospinal tracts. But the single most important modality for intramedullary tumor surgery is the D-wave.

The D-wave is a direct recording of the descending corticospinal volley from an epidural electrode placed caudal to the surgical site. It represents the total population of fast-conducting corticospinal axons. The critical threshold is a fifty-percent drop in D-wave amplitude. If the D-wave amplitude decreases by more than fifty percent from baseline, you stop the resection immediately, regardless of how much tumor remains. A D-wave drop greater than fifty percent correlates with permanent motor deficit. This is the Sala and Deletis data, and the examiners expect you to cite it.

Now, what about MEPs? MEP loss can be transient. Muscle MEPs are an all-or-nothing phenomenon generated by temporal summation at the alpha motor neuron, and they can disappear with minimal corticospinal tract compromise and then recover. So MEP loss alone, if the D-wave is preserved, does not mandate stopping the resection. The patient may wake up with a transient motor deficit that recovers over days to weeks. But if both MEPs and D-wave are lost, the deficit will be permanent.

The examiner will test you directly on this with a trap question. They will say, you lose MEPs during ependymoma resection but the D-wave is preserved. Do you stop? The answer is not necessarily. You check the D-wave amplitude. If the D-wave remains above fifty percent of baseline, the patient will likely recover motor function even though MEPs are gone. You may continue resection cautiously, reassessing frequently. If the D-wave drops below fifty percent, you stop immediately. This distinction between D-wave and MEP significance is a board-level answer.

Next, the examiner will probe extent of resection by tumor type, and here you must be tumor-specific. For ependymoma, you aim for gross total resection. The cleavage plane exists between tumor pseudocapsule and compressed cord. You develop this plane circumferentially, working from inside the tumor cavity outward, debulking the center first to create room, then dissecting the interface. En bloc removal is ideal but often the tumor is removed in large fragments after internal debulking. For astrocytoma, you do not attempt gross total resection unless it is a pilocytic variant with a discrete plane. Diffuse fibrillary astrocytoma is infiltrative, and aggressive resection destroys functional cord without improving survival. You biopsy for histologic diagnosis, debulk the exophytic component if present, and decompress the cord. For hemangioblastoma, gross total resection is mandatory and must be en bloc. You identify the nodule, coagulate its surface feeding arteries circumferentially, shrink it, mobilize it from surrounding cord, and remove it as a single piece. Never take it piecemeal.

The examiner will set a trap here. They will say, the hemangioblastoma is fifty percent resected and now it is bleeding heavily. What happened? The answer is piecemeal resection. This is the exact wrong technique for hemangioblastoma. By cutting into the tumor, you have opened densely vascular channels without having controlled the arterial feeders. The correct technique is circumferential coagulation of surface vessels first, en bloc mobilization second. At this point in the scenario, you must irrigate, apply hemostatic agents, and attempt to gain control of remaining feeders to complete the resection, because a partially resected hemangioblastoma will continue to hemorrhage.

Another trap question: would you attempt gross total resection of a cervical cord astrocytoma? The answer is no, unless frozen section confirms pilocytic histology. Diffuse astrocytomas of the cord are infiltrative. Aggressive resection produces permanent neurological deficit without a survival benefit. You debulk what is safe, obtain tissue for diagnosis, and plan adjuvant therapy based on grade.

Now let us discuss adjuvant therapy. For ependymoma, if you achieve gross total resection, no adjuvant therapy is needed. Radiation is reserved for subtotal resection or recurrence. For astrocytoma, radiation therapy is indicated for high-grade tumors or progressive disease after biopsy and debulking. For low-grade astrocytoma with stable residual, you can observe with serial imaging. For hemangioblastoma, no adjuvant therapy is needed after gross total resection. These are benign WHO grade one tumors, and complete removal is curative.

The examiner may shift to extramedullary tumors. For schwannoma, the goal is gross total resection. These tumors arise from a single nerve root fascicle, and most patients tolerate sacrifice of a single nerve root without clinically significant deficit. For dumbbell tumors extending through the foramen, you may need to drill the facet and pedicle to access the extradural component, or you may stage the surgery with a separate anterior or lateral approach. Be aware that if you sacrifice more than one adjacent nerve root, the patient is more likely to develop a functional deficit, so preoperative EMG and nerve root stimulation intraoperatively can help identify which root carries the tumor and whether the adjacent roots are functional. For meningioma, you aim for Simpson Grade I or Grade II resection. This means removing the tumor with its dural attachment and coagulating the dural base. Thoracic meningiomas that are lateral or posterolateral are straightforward. Ventral thoracic meningiomas are the challenge because the cord sits directly in your path. You cannot retract the thoracic cord without causing injury. The technique involves working laterally around the cord, debulking the tumor center first, then collapsing the capsule inward away from the cord and dissecting the ventral dural attachment last.

Let us address complications. Neurological worsening is the most feared complication and can be transient or permanent. Transient deficits from MEP loss with preserved D-wave typically recover over days to weeks. Permanent deficits correlate with D-wave loss or direct vascular injury. CSF leak and pseudomeningocele can occur despite watertight dural closure and may require lumbar drainage or surgical revision. Wound infection rates are comparable to other posterior spine procedures. Post-laminectomy spinal deformity, particularly kyphosis in the cervical spine, is a real concern after multilevel laminectomy and is the reason you should consider laminoplasty or instrumented fusion in the cervical region, especially in younger patients. In children, the risk of post-laminectomy deformity is even higher due to the developing spine, and prophylactic fusion should be strongly considered. Additionally, recurrence is a long-term complication, particularly for subtotally resected ependymoma and for astrocytoma, which is why surveillance MRI every six to twelve months for the first three to five years and then annually thereafter is the standard follow-up protocol. Wound dehiscence and hardware failure are additional considerations when instrumentation is placed, particularly in the setting of potential future radiation therapy.

Now the evidence you need. The McCormick functional grading scale classifies intramedullary tumor patients into four grades. Grade I is neurologically normal or has mild deficit, fully functional. Grade II is moderate deficit, independent function. Grade III is more severe deficit, requires assistance. Grade IV is severe, wheelchair or bed bound. You use this scale to describe preoperative function, postoperative outcomes, and long-term follow-up. Know it.

The Constantini and Epstein series are foundational in the pediatric intramedullary tumor literature and established the principles of radical resection for ependymoma and conservative resection for astrocytoma in children. The Klekamp long-term series demonstrated that gross total resection of spinal ependymoma produces greater than ninety percent recurrence-free survival at ten years, establishing GTR as the standard of care. The Sala and Deletis publications established the neurophysiological framework for D-wave monitoring, showing that D-wave preservation above fifty percent of baseline correlates with functional motor recovery, while loss below fifty percent predicts permanent deficit. This is the evidence that supports continuing resection when MEPs are lost but D-wave is preserved. It is also worth knowing the Brotchi series, which contributed significantly to the surgical technique of intramedullary tumor resection, particularly emphasizing the importance of the posterior median sulcus myelotomy and the role of the ultrasonic aspirator. For extramedullary tumors, the Solero series on spinal meningiomas demonstrated excellent outcomes with Simpson Grade I and II resection, with recurrence rates under five percent at long-term follow-up. The Seppala series on spinal schwannomas showed that gross total resection is curative in the vast majority of sporadic cases, with recurrence being a concern primarily in NF2-associated tumors where multiple lesions complicate management.

Let us close with board pearls. These are the things that will keep you safe in the exam room.

First, approach rules are tumor-specific. Ependymoma gets posterior median sulcus myelotomy for gross total resection. Astrocytoma gets biopsy and safe debulking only, no heroic resection. Hemangioblastoma gets circumferential vessel coagulation followed by en bloc removal, never piecemeal.

Second, the D-wave fifty percent rule. If D-wave amplitude drops more than fifty percent, you stop. Period. MEP loss alone with preserved D-wave does not mandate stopping. D-wave is the gold standard for predicting permanent motor outcome in intramedullary tumor surgery.

Third, never piecemeal a hemangioblastoma. This is one of those absolute rules in neurosurgery. Piecemeal resection opens vascular channels without controlling inflow and results in catastrophic hemorrhage. En bloc removal after circumferential feeder coagulation is the only safe technique.

Three things that will fail you on this topic. One, saying you would attempt gross total resection of a diffuse cervical cord astrocytoma. That tells the examiner you do not understand tumor biology and you will hurt your patient. Two, not knowing the D-wave rule. If you cannot articulate the fifty-percent threshold and distinguish D-wave from MEP significance, you have not prepared adequately. Three, describing piecemeal resection of a hemangioblastoma. The examiner will end your case right there.

Know these tumor types, their imaging characteristics, their surgical planes, and their specific resection strategies. Know when to push for gross total resection and when to stop. Know your monitoring and what it means. And know the evidence behind your decisions.

That covers spinal cord tumors. You are ready for this one.
